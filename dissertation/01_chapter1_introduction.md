## 1.1 INTRODUCTION

Education is the most powerful tool by which a society shapes its young members into useful, productive and responsible citizens. The Secondary School stage, covering Classes IX and X, is one of the most important stages of the entire school life. It is during these two years that an adolescent boy or girl begins to think about *what subjects to choose at the +2 stage*, *what college to join*, and ultimately, *what occupation to take up in life*. The choices made at this stage have lifelong effects on his or her happiness, success and contribution to society.

A right occupational choice is not a matter of *chance* but a matter of *informed decision*. The decision is informed when it is based on a clear understanding of three things — first, the *abilities* of the individual; second, the *interests* of the individual; and third, the *opportunities* available in the world of work. Among these three, the *interest* of the individual is the most personal and the most lasting factor. A person who chooses an occupation in line with his or her interest works with joy, learns more easily, faces difficulties bravely and rises steadily in his or her career. A person who chooses an occupation against his or her interest, on the other hand, finds work tiring and meaningless and sooner or later changes his or her line of work, often after losing precious years.

The systematic study of *vocational interests* of school students is, therefore, both an educational and a social necessity. Educationally, it helps the teachers, parents and counsellors guide the students into right subjects and courses. Socially, it helps the country to make best use of its human resources and to reduce the wastage of talent that occurs when a person works in an unsuitable line.

The present investigation is a small step in this direction. It studies the vocational interests of 60 secondary school students of two contrasting schools of Meerut district — one *Rural Government* school and one *Urban Private CBSE-affiliated* school — using a well-known standardised tool, the *Vocational Interest Record* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha. The aim is to find out (i) which areas of work attract the maximum and the minimum number of students, (ii) whether boys and girls differ in their vocational interests, (iii) whether the rural government and urban private school students differ, and (iv) whether the Class IX and Class X students differ from each other.

## 1.2 CONCEPT OF INTEREST

The word *interest* is one of the most commonly used words of everyday life. We say things like, "He is interested in cricket", "She is interested in reading novels", "I am not interested in politics" and so on. In every such sentence, *interest* refers to a kind of *attraction* between a person and an activity. The activity catches the person's attention, gives him pleasure, and makes him want to continue with it.

In Educational Psychology, the term *interest* has been carefully defined by a number of writers. A few of the well-known definitions are presented below.

According to **Crow and Crow** (1973):

> "Interest may refer to the motivating force that impels us to attend to a person, a thing or an activity, or it may be the affective experience that has been stimulated by the activity itself."

According to **Hilgard** (1962):

> "Interest is a tendency to give selective attention to something."

According to **John Dewey** (1913):

> "Interest is the active or affective state in which the self and the object are not opposed but are bound together as factors in a common single experience."

According to **Drever's Dictionary of Psychology**:

> "Interest is a special kind of attitude in which the subject is so much attracted by an object that he is willing to give attention to it, work for it and learn about it."

A careful examination of these definitions brings out the following common features of *interest*:

1. **Interest is a selective tendency.** Out of a hundred objects placed before us, our attention is naturally drawn towards only a few. Those few are our objects of interest. Our attention is *selective* by nature, and that selectivity is governed by our interests.

2. **Interest involves both feeling and action.** A person who is interested in an object not only *feels* attracted by it but also *does* something about it. He gathers information, takes part in related activities, spends time and money on it, and willingly suffers minor inconveniences for it.

3. **Interest is partly inborn and partly acquired.** Some interests come naturally to a child — for instance, an interest in music or in colours. Others are slowly developed through experience, learning and the influence of family, school and society.

4. **Interest is dynamic.** Interest is not a fixed quantity. It changes from time to time depending on the age, the stage of development, the experiences and the opportunities available to the person.

5. **Interest is the friend of attention.** Whatever we do with interest receives our full attention and is, therefore, learnt easily and remembered well. Whatever we do without interest is done half-heartedly and is forgotten quickly.

For the purposes of the present study, *interest* is taken to mean **a relatively lasting feeling of attraction towards a particular object, activity or area of life that prompts the individual to give selective attention to it, to seek information about it, to take part in it, and to derive pleasure from it.**

### 1.2.1 Types of Interest

Educational Psychologists generally distinguish four major types of interests in a school child:

1. **Personal Interests** — interests connected with the person's own self, such as care of one's body, dress, food, etc.

2. **Social Interests** — interests connected with other people and society at large, such as friendship, helping the poor, etc.

3. **Academic Interests** — interests connected with subjects of study, such as Mathematics, Science, Literature, etc.

4. **Vocational Interests** — interests connected with future occupations and careers.

It is the fourth type, namely *vocational interests*, that forms the subject-matter of the present study.

## 1.3 CONCEPT OF VOCATIONAL INTEREST

The word *vocation* comes from the Latin word *vocare*, which means "to call". A *vocation*, therefore, is the line of work to which a person feels called. In ordinary English, the words *vocation*, *occupation*, *profession* and *career* are often used as synonyms, although the careful writer distinguishes them in the following manner:

- A **career** is the complete sequence of jobs that a person holds in a working lifetime.
- A **profession** is an occupation that requires specialised knowledge and long preparation, such as medicine, law, teaching, engineering, etc.
- An **occupation** is the present line of work of the person.
- A **vocation** is the inner *calling* that the person feels — the work that he or she truly wishes to do.

A *vocational interest* is, therefore, the *attraction* that a person feels for a particular line of work. According to **Strong** (1943), one of the pioneers of vocational interest research:

> "Vocational interest may be defined as a liking or preference for one occupational area as against the others, accompanied by a willingness to take up activities related to that area."

Other well-known definitions of *vocational interest* are as follows:

According to **Super** (1957):

> "Vocational interest is the product of the interaction between inherited aptitudes, glandular and emotional makeup on the one hand, and opportunities and social estimates of the occupations on the other."

According to **Crow and Crow** (1956):

> "A vocational interest of a person is his choice of a particular vocation in which he is interested to work and earn his living."

According to **S.P. Kulshrestha** (the author of the tool used in the present study):

> "Vocational interest is that aspect of an individual's personality which makes him think about, work for, prepare himself for, and ultimately enter a particular kind of occupation in preference to other possible occupations."

The above definitions clearly bring out four features of *vocational interest*:

1. It is a **specific kind of interest** — namely, interest in a particular line of work.
2. It is **comparative** — the person prefers one kind of work *to* the others.
3. It is **relatively stable** — once formed, it does not change very easily, although it may *develop* further over time.
4. It is **action-related** — it makes the person *do* certain things — read related books, take related subjects in school, attend related programmes, and so on.

### 1.3.1 Sources of Vocational Interest

Where does a vocational interest come from? Various studies have identified the following major sources:

1. **Family.** The occupation of the father or other elders is the very first source. Children of doctors often think of becoming doctors; children of farmers easily develop an interest in agriculture; and so on.

2. **School.** A particular subject taught well in school often awakens a strong vocational interest. A good teacher of Mathematics may make the child wish to be a Mathematics teacher; a Science teacher who conducts interesting experiments may inspire the child to wish to be a scientist or an engineer.

3. **Friends and Peer Group.** Adolescents discuss their dreams with their friends. The dreams shared by friends often become contagious.

4. **Mass Media.** Television, films, newspapers, magazines and now the Internet display the lives and work of people of various occupations. These images strongly influence the dreams of young people.

5. **Direct Experience.** A child who has actually visited a hospital, a court, a factory, a farm or a research laboratory often develops a strong attraction towards that line of work.

6. **Personal Aptitude.** The child slowly discovers, through school and life, that he is good at certain things. The areas in which he succeeds easily often become his areas of interest.

The vocational interests visible at the secondary stage are, therefore, the result of the *combined working* of all these sources over the past 14 to 16 years of the child's life.

## 1.4 THEORIES OF VOCATIONAL INTEREST

Several psychologists have attempted to explain *how* vocational interests develop and *how* a person ultimately makes a vocational choice. The most important of these theories are the following.

### 1.4.1 Frank Parsons' Trait-and-Factor Theory (1909)

**Frank Parsons** of Boston is generally regarded as the *Father of Vocational Guidance*. In his book *Choosing a Vocation* (1909), Parsons argued that a *wise vocational choice* is made up of three steps:

1. A clear understanding of *oneself* — one's abilities, interests, ambitions and limitations.
2. A clear knowledge of the *requirements*, *advantages*, *disadvantages* and *opportunities* of different lines of work.
3. *True reasoning* on the relationship between (1) and (2).

According to Parsons, when a person has these three pieces of information, the right vocational choice naturally follows. The role of the counsellor and the school is to help the student gather these three pieces of information.

The theory is simple but powerful, and it remains the foundation of vocational guidance even today. The use of standardised *interest inventories* (like the *V.I.R.* of Dr. Kulshrestha) is a direct outcome of this theory: the inventory helps the student understand his own interests in step (1) of the Parsons' formula.

### 1.4.2 Donald Super's Self-Concept Theory (1953)

**Donald E. Super** developed a more advanced theory in 1953. According to Super, vocational choice is the *implementation of the self-concept*. The *self-concept* is the person's idea about *who he is* and *what he can be*. As the self-concept grows clearer with age, the vocational choice grows more definite.

Super divided the working life of a person into five stages:

1. **Growth** (birth to 14 years) — when the child develops his self-concept through play, school and family.
2. **Exploration** (15 to 24 years) — when the youth explores various occupational possibilities. *The secondary school stage falls in this period*.
3. **Establishment** (25 to 44 years) — when the person settles down in his chosen line.
4. **Maintenance** (45 to 64 years) — when the person consolidates his position.
5. **Decline** (65 onwards) — when the person retires and reduces his work activity.

The secondary school stage is, according to Super, the *peak* of the *exploration* stage. The Class IX and Class X student is busily exploring the world of occupations, and his vocational interests are taking shape. This is exactly why the *measurement* of vocational interests at this stage is so meaningful.

### 1.4.3 Eli Ginzberg's Developmental Theory (1951)

**Eli Ginzberg** and his associates put forward a *developmental* theory of vocational choice. According to them, vocational choice is not a single event but a *process* that runs through three stages:

1. **Fantasy stage** (up to 11 years) — when the child wishes to be everything he sees — fireman, pilot, soldier, doctor, all by turn.
2. **Tentative stage** (11 to 17 years) — when interests, capacity, values and goals begin to influence the choice. *The secondary school stage falls here.*
3. **Realistic stage** (17 to 25 years) — when the youth makes a final, realistic choice based on a true study of self and of opportunities.

This theory clearly underlines the importance of the secondary school years. It is during these years that the child's vocational thinking *changes character* — from fancy to *interest*.

### 1.4.4 John L. Holland's Theory of Vocational Personalities (1959)

**John L. Holland** developed a theory based on the idea that a person's vocational interest is essentially an *expression of his personality*. He proposed *six basic personality types* and matched them with *six work environments*. The six types are:

1. **Realistic** (R) — practical, mechanical, outdoor (matches farming, engineering, military).
2. **Investigative** (I) — scientific, analytical, intellectual (matches research, medicine, science).
3. **Artistic** (A) — creative, expressive, imaginative (matches writing, art, music).
4. **Social** (S) — helpful, teaching, caring (matches teaching, counselling, social work).
5. **Enterprising** (E) — leading, persuading, business-oriented (matches business, politics, sales).
6. **Conventional** (C) — orderly, clerical, organising (matches accounting, banking, administration).

According to Holland, the closer the match between the *person's type* and the *work environment*, the greater is his job satisfaction and success. The ten areas of the *V.I.R.* by Dr. Kulshrestha cover almost the same ground as Holland's six types, suggesting a basic agreement between the Indian and Western frameworks.

### 1.4.5 Anne Roe's Need-based Theory (1957)

**Anne Roe** related vocational choice to the *needs* of the person, drawing upon Maslow's hierarchy. She suggested that the *kind of upbringing* the child receives in the family — warm-and-protective, cold-and-rejecting, or warm-and-accepting — decides whether he will, in adult life, prefer occupations that involve people or things. A child brought up in a warm, loving family generally develops a *people-oriented* vocational interest, while one brought up in a cold environment develops a *thing-oriented* interest.

### 1.4.6 Summary of the Theories

A close look at the above theories shows that, although they differ in detail, they agree on the following points:

1. Vocational interest is *not* a sudden phenomenon — it *develops* over many years.
2. The secondary school stage is a *crucial* stage in this development.
3. Vocational interest is the joint product of *personality*, *family*, *experience* and *the wider environment*.
4. The *systematic measurement* of vocational interests using a *standardised tool* is a direct, scientific way to support the boy or girl during this important stage.

The present investigation has, therefore, been designed in the spirit of these theories.

## 1.5 IMPORTANCE OF VOCATIONAL INTEREST AT THE SECONDARY STAGE

The secondary school stage is the *crossroads* of the school-life of a young person. Several important reasons make the *measurement of vocational interests* at this stage especially important. Some of these reasons are listed below:

1. **Subject Choice at +2 Stage.** Soon after Class X, the student has to choose Science, Commerce, Arts or Vocational stream at the +2 stage. This is the *first major* choice of his career. If the student knows his vocational interests well, he can make a sensible choice. If he does not know them, he is likely to drift along with friends or to follow the wishes of his parents — a situation that often leads to later disappointment.

2. **Use of Time.** A student who has identified his vocational interest can spend his after-school hours in *useful* extra reading, hobby clubs and projects related to his future career. A student who has *not* identified his vocational interest tends to fritter away his free time.

3. **Reduction of Wastage.** Every year, a large number of students leave their +2 stream or college course mid-way because the course was unsuited to them. A timely measurement of vocational interests at the secondary stage *reduces this wastage*.

4. **Development of Self-Concept.** Knowledge of one's own vocational interests strengthens the student's *self-concept*. He feels surer about himself and faces life with greater confidence.

5. **Effective Career Counselling.** The school counsellor or class teacher is able to advise the student more *meaningfully* if he has a written record of the student's vocational interests in front of him.

6. **National Manpower Planning.** When the vocational interests of school students of a country are systematically known, the planners can match the *production of manpower* to the *needs of the economy*. The country avoids the twin evils of *unemployment* of the over-supplied lines and *shortages* in the under-supplied lines.

7. **Personal Happiness.** Last but not the least, work matched to interest brings *personal happiness*. As the famous saying goes, *"Choose a job you love, and you will never have to work a day in your life"*.

## 1.6 RURAL GOVERNMENT AND URBAN PRIVATE SCHOOLS IN INDIA

India has a complex and varied school system. Schools differ widely from one another in terms of *location*, *management*, *medium of instruction*, *curriculum*, *board of affiliation* and *socio-economic background of the students*. Two of the most important dimensions along which Indian schools differ are:

1. **Locality** — *rural* or *urban*.
2. **Management** — *government* or *private*.

When these two dimensions are combined, four kinds of schools result — Rural Government, Rural Private, Urban Government and Urban Private. The two extremes of this 2 × 2 set are *Rural Government* and *Urban Private*. These two extremes have been chosen as the two settings of the present study.

### 1.6.1 Features of Rural Government Schools

Rural government schools are usually located in villages or small towns and are run by the State Government. The medium of instruction is generally *Hindi* (or the local regional language). The fees are nominal or zero. The students come from agricultural and labouring families. The school often lacks well-equipped laboratories, computer rooms and a regular guidance counsellor. The teachers are usually from the local area itself. The cultural environment is mostly *traditional*.

### 1.6.2 Features of Urban Private Schools

Urban private schools are usually located in cities and are run by private societies, trusts or companies. They are often affiliated to the *Central Board of Secondary Education (C.B.S.E.)* and follow the English medium. The fees are substantially higher than those of government schools. The students typically come from middle and upper-middle income families. The school usually has well-equipped Physics, Chemistry, Biology and Computer laboratories, smart classrooms, libraries, art and music rooms and a guidance cell. The cultural environment is *modern and aspirational*.

### 1.6.3 Implications for Vocational Interest

It is reasonable to expect that the vocational interest patterns of children growing up in these two contrasting settings will be *very different*. Rural government school students, growing up in farming families, are likely to show greater interest in *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas. Urban private school students, growing up in modern, fee-paying, English-medium schools, are likely to show greater interest in *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Commercial*, *Artistic* and *Persuasive* areas. Whether this expectation is *actually borne out* by data is the central question of the present investigation.

## 1.7 NEED AND SIGNIFICANCE OF THE STUDY

The present study is needed for a number of reasons. Some of the most important of these are listed below.

### 1.7.1 Need for Comparative Studies

Although a number of studies on vocational interests of Indian school students have been conducted in the last fifty years, only a small number of them have *systematically compared* rural government and urban private school students. Most of the studies have either been confined to one type of school, or have used too small or too unequal a sample, or have focussed on only one or two areas of interest. There is, therefore, a clear *research gap* for a focussed comparative study of these two contrasting school types in the same district. The present study tries to fill this gap.

### 1.7.2 Local Significance for Meerut District

Meerut is one of the largest and most rapidly developing districts of Western Uttar Pradesh. It is also a district of *sharp contrasts* — the city of Meerut is a busy industrial and educational centre, whereas a few kilometres outside the city the rural areas remain predominantly agricultural. To the best of the investigator's knowledge, no recent comparative study of vocational interests of these two segments of the Meerut population has been carried out. The present study, therefore, has a special *local* significance.

### 1.7.3 Practical Importance for the Selected Schools

The two schools — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, and K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh — that have been selected for the study, have been struggling, like all other secondary schools in India, with the question of *career guidance*. The findings of the present study will provide them with a *clear vocational profile* of their students, on the basis of which the Principals and the Class Teachers can plan suitable *career awareness programmes*, *exposure visits* and *parent counselling sessions*.

### 1.7.4 Significance for the State and the Nation

The findings of the present study, although based on a small sample of 60 students, can serve as a *case study* for the larger educational community of Uttar Pradesh. They can suggest the kind of *career guidance machinery* that needs to be developed in the rural government and the urban private secondary schools of the country.

### 1.7.5 Significance for the Researcher

For the investigator personally, the study is a *first independent research project*. It has provided experience in *defining a problem*, *reviewing literature*, *administering a standardised tool*, *analysing data* and *interpreting results*. These are essential skills of every educational professional and are likely to be useful throughout the future career of the investigator.

## 1.9 OPERATIONAL DEFINITIONS OF THE TERMS USED

The important terms used in the title of the study and in the objectives have been defined operationally below, so that the meaning is clear and unambiguous.

1. **Vocational Interest.** *Vocational Interest* of a student means his or her score on each of the ten areas of the *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha, namely Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household. The score on each area can range from 0 to 20.

2. **Secondary School Student.** *Secondary School Student* means a student studying in Class IX or Class X of a school recognised by the U.P. Board / C.B.S.E. during the academic session 2025–2026.

3. **Rural Government School.** *Rural Government School* means a school which is (a) located in a village or rural area at least 10 km away from Meerut city, and (b) run / aided by the U.P. Government under the Department of Secondary Education. In the present study, *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut* has been taken to represent this category.

4. **Urban Private School.** *Urban Private School* means a school which is (a) located within the municipal limits of Meerut city, and (b) run by a private society / trust / company without government grant. In the present study, *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut* (a C.B.S.E. affiliated, English medium school) has been taken to represent this category.

5. **Boys / Girls.** *Boys* and *Girls* mean the male and the female students respectively, of the two schools selected.

6. **Class IX / Class X.** *Class IX* and *Class X* mean the students of the ninth and the tenth classes, respectively, of the two schools selected.

7. **Comparative Study.** *Comparative Study* means a study in which the average vocational interest scores of two or more groups of students are compared with each other on the basis of statistical techniques such as *mean*, *standard deviation*, *t-test* and *percentages*.

---

## 1.10 OBJECTIVES OF THE STUDY

The present investigation has been planned with the following five major objectives:

1. **To study** the area-wise vocational interest pattern of the total sample (N = 60) of secondary school students on the ten areas of the *Vocational Interest Record* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha.

2. **To find out** the percentage distribution of high, average and low levels of vocational interest of the total sample, and of the various sub-groups, on each of the ten areas.

3. **To compare** the vocational interests of the **boys** (n = 30) and the **girls** (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.

4. **To compare** the vocational interests of the **Rural Government** school students (n = 30) and the **Urban Private** school students (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.

5. **To compare** the vocational interests of the **Class IX** students (n = 30) and the **Class X** students (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.

## 1.11 HYPOTHESES OF THE STUDY

A *null hypothesis* is a statement of *no difference*. The role of statistical analysis is to *test* the null hypothesis by examining how far the observed difference between the means departs from a difference of zero. If the observed difference is *too large* to be the result of mere chance, the null hypothesis is *rejected*; otherwise it is *retained*.

The following three null hypotheses have been formulated for the present study:

> **H₀1.** There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of *boys* and *girls* of secondary school stage on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

> **H₀2.** There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of *Rural Government* and *Urban Private* secondary school students on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

> **H₀3.** There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of *Class IX* and *Class X* secondary school students on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

The truth or falsity of each of these hypotheses will be examined through the *t-test* in Chapter IV.

## 1.12 DELIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

Every research investigation is bound by certain *delimitations* — i.e. the boundaries that the investigator has, by his own conscious choice, set around the study to make it manageable. The delimitations of the present study are listed below:

1. The study is delimited to the **secondary school stage** (Class IX and Class X) only. Higher secondary (+2) and college students are *not* covered.

2. The study is delimited to the **district of Meerut** (Uttar Pradesh) only. Other districts are *not* covered.

3. The study is delimited to **two schools** only — one Rural Government school (Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur) and one Urban Private CBSE school (K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh).

4. The study is delimited to a **sample of 60 students** — 30 from each school, with 15 students of each class (7 boys + 8 girls of Class IX, 8 boys + 7 girls of Class X) — selected through stratified random sampling.

5. The tool used is delimited to **one standardised tool** — the *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha. Other tools have *not* been used.

6. The areas of vocational interest covered are delimited to the **ten areas** of the V.I.R. — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household. Areas not covered by this tool are *not* studied.

7. The independent variables studied are delimited to **gender** (Boys / Girls), **type of school** (Rural Government / Urban Private) and **class** (IX / X). Other possible variables — such as *age*, *family income*, *birth order*, *parental occupation*, *intelligence* and *academic achievement* — have *not* been studied.

8. The statistical techniques used are delimited to **mean**, **standard deviation**, **t-test for independent samples** and **percentages**. Higher techniques such as factor analysis and regression have *not* been used.

These delimitations should be kept in mind when interpreting the findings and generalising them to wider populations.
