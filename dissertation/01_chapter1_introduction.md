# CHAPTER – I

# INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Introduction

Education, in its widest sense, is a continuous process of *all-round development* of the human personality. It is concerned not only with the acquisition of knowledge or with the cultivation of intellectual abilities, but also with the harmonious development of the physical, emotional, social, moral, aesthetic and *vocational* dimensions of life. Among these, the vocational dimension occupies a unique place, for it is through productive work that the individual finds his or her economic security, social status, self-respect and a sense of worthwhile contribution to society. The Secondary Education Commission (1952–53), under the chairmanship of Dr. A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar, observed almost seven decades ago that "the supreme end of the educative process should be the training of the character of pupils to fit them to participate creatively as citizens in the complex modern world." A century earlier, the great Indian thinker Swami Vivekananda had defined education as "the manifestation of the perfection already in man." Both formulations emphasise that the *latent capacities* of every learner — including his or her *vocational potentialities* — must be patiently identified and systematically nurtured by the school.

The most natural and economical guide to such identification is the learner's own *interest*. Educational psychology recognises *interest* as the dynamic mainspring of attention, motivation, learning and persistence. When an individual works in a field of his or her *spontaneous interest*, the work ceases to be drudgery; it becomes a source of joy, creativity and excellence. On the other hand, work undertaken without interest, however well-rewarded materially, often degenerates into a tedious obligation. The accurate identification of vocational interest is therefore one of the surest ways of matching the right person to the right vocation.

The present chapter introduces the conceptual framework of the study. It examines the meaning and concept of *interest* and *vocational interest*, presents the principal theories advanced by Western and Indian scholars, discusses the importance of vocational interest at the secondary stage, indicates the place of secondary education in the Indian educational structure, traces the development of vocational guidance in India, brings out the existing gender differences in career choices, and finally formulates the problem, objectives, hypotheses, delimitations, assumptions and operational definitions of the present study.

## 1.2 Meaning and Concept of Interest

The English word "interest" is derived from the Latin *interesse* meaning "to make a difference" or "to be between". In educational and psychological literature it has been variously defined. Some of the well-known definitions are reproduced below:

**Bingham (1937)** — "Interest is a tendency to become absorbed in an experience and to continue it." It is essentially a *purposive engagement* of the self with an object, an activity or an idea.

**Crow and Crow (1973)** — "Interest may refer to the motivating force that impels us to attend to a person, a thing or an activity, or it may be the affective experience that has been stimulated by the activity itself."

**Drever (1952)** — "Interest is a specific concern, motive or feeling which makes one's attention move and stay over a particular object, person or activity."

**Hilgard (1962)** — "Interest is a tendency to give selective attention to an object."

**S.S. Chauhan (1978)** — "Interest is a tendency to seek out an activity, an idea or a state of affairs and to find satisfaction therein."

A careful examination of the above definitions reveals three common features. First, an interest involves *selective attention* — out of the innumerable stimuli surrounding us at any given moment, an individual attends only to a few. Second, an interest is *affective* in nature, i.e., it is accompanied by feelings of pleasure or satisfaction. Third, an interest is *motivational* — it impels the individual to seek out, repeat and persist in the activity. Interest, therefore, is more than a passive liking; it is an *active disposition* of the self that energises and directs behaviour.

Educational psychologists distinguish between *immediate* and *mediate* interest. Immediate interest is the spontaneous attraction towards an activity for its own sake (e.g., a child enjoying a story). Mediate or *derived* interest is one that arises out of the realisation of the value of the activity for some larger purpose (e.g., a student studying chemistry because she wants to become a doctor). Vocational interest, as discussed below, is generally of the second type, since it is closely linked with future career goals.

## 1.3 Meaning of Vocational Interest

When the concept of interest is applied to occupations, professions and the world of work, it gives rise to the concept of *vocational interest*. A vocational interest is a *relatively stable preference* for engaging in certain types of vocational activities and an aversion to others. It expresses itself in the form of likes and dislikes for occupations, occupational tasks and occupational environments.

Some authoritative definitions of vocational interest are quoted below:

**Donald E. Super (1957).** Super, the foremost American theorist of vocational development, defined vocational interest as "*the product of interaction between inherited neural and endocrine factors, on the one hand, and opportunity and social evaluation, on the other.*" According to him, vocational interests develop because some activities give the individual greater satisfaction than others; the activities that provide satisfaction are repeated, become well-developed habits and finally crystallise into stable interests.

**E.K. Strong, Jr. (1943).** Strong, the pioneer of objective interest measurement, defined a vocational interest as "*a liking or preference for an activity*". His epoch-making *Strong Vocational Interest Blank (SVIB)* was based on the empirical principle that the interest patterns of successful members of an occupation distinguish them from people in general, and that interest scores in the high school years remain remarkably stable into adulthood.

**John L. Holland (1973, 1985).** Holland's *theory of vocational personalities and work environments* views vocational interest as an *expression of personality*. He held that "the choice of a vocation is an expression of personality," and that there are six basic personality–environment types — *Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising* and *Conventional* (RIASEC). Each individual's interest profile can be summarised in a three-letter code (e.g., RIA, SAE) that indicates the relative dominance of these six types.

**S.P. Kulshrestha (1972).** Kulshrestha, the Indian educationist who developed the standardised *Vocational Interest Record* used in the present study, defined vocational interest as "*the feeling of liking or disliking towards a particular vocation, depending upon the satisfaction obtained from the activities involved in that vocation, the prestige attached to it and the rewards available through it.*" He identified ten broad areas of vocational interest, which form the basis of his measuring tool.

**Crites (1969)** — "Vocational interest is a more or less mature predilection for one occupation rather than another, based upon a preference for the activities involved in the former."

**Tyler (1955)** — "Vocational interest is a learned preference, and although a part of it may be biologically determined, the major share is the product of experience and learning."

A synthesis of these definitions yields the following picture of vocational interest:

i. It is a *preference for occupational activities*, not merely a transitory liking.

ii. It is *relatively stable*, especially after the age of 17 or 18 years, although in adolescence it is still in the process of formation.

iii. It is partly *innate* and partly *learned* through experience, observation, exposure and reinforcement.

iv. It is the *product of interaction* of the individual with his or her social, cultural and educational environment.

v. It is *measurable* through standardised psychological tests, the most widely used Indian instrument being the *Vocational Interest Record* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha.

vi. It serves as one of the *most powerful predictors* of vocational choice, vocational satisfaction and vocational success.

## 1.4 Concept of Vocational Guidance

Closely related to the concept of vocational interest is the concept of *vocational guidance*. The term came into systematic use after the appearance of Frank Parsons' classical work *Choosing a Vocation* (1909). Parsons defined the central problem of vocational guidance as "*the matching of the man and the job*", and argued that a wise choice of vocation involves three steps: (a) a clear understanding of oneself, one's aptitudes, abilities, interests and limitations; (b) a thorough knowledge of the requirements, conditions and prospects of various occupations; and (c) "true reasoning" on the relations between these two groups of facts.

The *Secondary Education Commission (1952–53)* of India observed that "*vocational guidance and counselling is an integral part of education*" and recommended the appointment of a trained guidance worker in every secondary school. The *Kothari Commission (1964–66)* gave a still stronger endorsement, declaring that "*vocational guidance is no longer to be regarded as a luxury, but as a vital service that should reach every secondary school in the country.*" The recommendations of these two Commissions, however, have not been fully implemented even after seven decades. Most secondary schools in India, particularly in rural areas, do not yet have a trained career counsellor on their staff.

The *Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009* and, more recently, the *National Education Policy 2020*, have re-emphasised the importance of *career awareness, skill education and vocational counselling* at the upper-primary and secondary stages. The National Education Policy 2020, in para 4.10, recommends that "*every student will take a fun course, during Grades 6–8, that gives a survey and hands-on experience of a sampling of important vocational crafts*", and in para 5.18 it recommends *trained counsellors* in every school cluster. These provisions are likely to revive interest in vocational guidance over the coming years.

In its modern form, vocational guidance is understood as a *comprehensive process* that includes (a) the *self-appraisal* of the learner with the help of standardised tests of intelligence, aptitude, interest, personality and achievement; (b) *occupational information* relating to the nature of work, qualifications required, methods of entry, conditions of work, remuneration and prospects in various occupations; (c) *individual counselling* sessions with a trained counsellor; (d) *group guidance activities* such as career talks, career fairs, exposure visits and films; and (e) *follow-up services* to ascertain the success of the choice made.

The accurate measurement of *vocational interest* — the focus of the present study — is therefore one of the indispensable foundations on which the entire structure of vocational guidance must rest.

## 1.5 Secondary Education in India

Secondary education in India occupies the *middle position* between the elementary stage and the higher stage of education. According to the National Education Policy 2020, the school stage has been re-organised into four blocks — Foundational (3 + 2 years), Preparatory (3 years), Middle (3 years) and Secondary (4 years). The *Secondary stage* covers Grades 9 to 12, i.e., students from approximately 14 to 18 years of age. The earlier *10+2* pattern divided this into Classes IX–X (Secondary) and XI–XII (Senior Secondary).

The Secondary Education Commission (1952–53) emphasised four major aims of secondary education:

i. Development of democratic citizenship;

ii. Development of personality;

iii. Improvement of vocational efficiency; and

iv. Education for leadership.

Of these, *vocational efficiency* and *personality development* are most closely tied to the present study. The Commission recommended a *diversified curriculum* with seven streams (Humanities, Science, Commerce, Agriculture, Fine Arts, Home Science and Technical), so that every student could pursue an area of his or her interest. The Kothari Commission carried this forward by recommending the *vocationalisation of secondary education* with at least 50 per cent of students in Class XI – XII opting for vocational rather than purely academic courses. The actual achievement on this front has been disappointing — the percentage of students in vocational streams in India has rarely crossed 5 per cent.

The Secondary stage is significant from the present study's standpoint for three reasons:

a. It is the *final compulsory stage* for all those who do not proceed to higher education; the vocational decision taken at this point has lifelong consequences.

b. It coincides with the period of *adolescence*, during which the individual's vocational interest is in the most active stage of crystallisation.

c. It is the stage at which *streaming* (Science / Commerce / Arts) takes place at the end of Class X, and this stream choice is heavily dependent on the learner's vocational interest.

In the State of Uttar Pradesh, in which the present study has been conducted, secondary education is administered through the *Uttar Pradesh Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad* (U.P. Board of High School and Intermediate Education, Prayagraj), as well as through the central boards CBSE and ICSE. The two sample schools of the present study represent these two streams: *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur*, is affiliated with the U.P. Board, while *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh*, is affiliated with the Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE).

## 1.6 Adolescent Career Development

The Secondary School age coincides with the *adolescent stage* of human development, traditionally placed between 12 and 18 years. G. Stanley Hall (1904), the father of adolescent psychology, characterised adolescence as a "*period of storm and stress*". Modern psychologists such as Erik Erikson (1968) prefer to describe it as "*the stage of identity formation*", in which the individual asks the central question, "*Who am I?*" and in answering it, formulates a *vocational identity* alongside a personal identity.

Donald E. Super (1953, 1980) divided the lifelong process of vocational development into five major stages:

a. *Growth* (birth – 14 years) — the child develops a self-concept through identification with key figures in family and school. Vocational fantasies of "I will be a pilot / doctor / soldier" are typical.

b. *Exploration* (15 – 24 years) — the *crystallisation*, *specification* and *implementation* of a vocational choice. The Secondary School learner is at the early end of this stage.

c. *Establishment* (25 – 44 years) — the individual settles into a chosen occupation and seeks to make it secure.

d. *Maintenance* (45 – 64 years) — the individual works to retain the position attained.

e. *Decline* (65 + years) — gradual disengagement from work.

Eli Ginzberg (1951) identified three sub-periods within the adolescent stage of vocational development — *Tentative* (11–17 years), within which lie the *Interest stage* (11–12), the *Capacity stage* (13–14), the *Value stage* (15–16) and the *Transition stage* (17–18); and *Realistic* (17–early twenties).

It is evident from these formulations that *Class IX and X students*, who form the sample of the present study, are at the *Tentative* stage in Ginzberg's scheme and at the early *Exploration* stage in Super's scheme. They are precisely at the age at which their interests are sufficiently formed to be measured, but still flexible enough to be re-shaped through good guidance. This is precisely why the present investigation focusses on this age group.

## 1.7 Importance of Vocational Interests

The systematic identification of vocational interest at the secondary stage is significant on several counts:

i. **Academic stream choice.** At the end of Class X, the Indian secondary school learner has to choose between Science, Commerce, Arts and (in some schools) Vocational streams for Class XI–XII. An informed stream choice based on measured vocational interest is more likely to lead to academic success.

ii. **Subject and elective choice.** Even within a stream, electives such as Biology vs. Mathematics in the Science stream, or Accountancy vs. Economics vs. Computer Science in the Commerce stream, are best made on the basis of differential interest patterns.

iii. **Higher education planning.** Choices of college, course and university are simplified once the dominant interest area of the student is known.

iv. **Vocational satisfaction.** Persons working in occupations matching their measured interest are reported to be more satisfied, more productive and less prone to absenteeism and quitting (Holland, 1985).

v. **Reduction of dropout and failure.** A great deal of academic failure and school dropout has been traced to a *mismatch* between the student's interest and the course in which he or she is enrolled.

vi. **Mental health.** Vocational dissatisfaction is one of the major sources of adult anxiety, depression and psychosomatic illness. Early identification and respect for vocational interest can therefore have preventive mental-health value.

vii. **National manpower planning.** From the standpoint of the State, the systematic mapping of the vocational interests of the secondary school population provides important inputs for human resource planning, vocational education provisions and skill-development missions.

## 1.8 Areas of Vocational Interest

Different theorists have classified vocational interest into different sets of areas. Strong (1943) identified about 50 occupational scales; Holland (1985) reduced these to *six* RIASEC types; Indian researcher S.P. Kulshrestha, on the basis of factor analysis on the Indian occupational data, settled on *ten* areas, which are reproduced below with brief descriptions. (These ten areas are the basis of the *Vocational Interest Record* used in the present study.)

1. **Literary Interest.** Liking for occupations involving the use of language — reading, writing, editing, journalism, teaching of language and literature, writing scripts, etc. Persons high on this area enjoy poetry, novels, debates and creative writing.

2. **Scientific Interest.** Liking for occupations involving observation, experimentation and investigation of natural phenomena — research scientist, engineer, doctor, laboratory technician, science teacher, mathematician, etc. Such persons are intellectually curious and enjoy "finding out how and why".

3. **Executive Interest.** Liking for managerial, administrative and supervisory work — civil services, business management, banking, school administration, etc. Such persons enjoy planning, organising, decision-making and leading others.

4. **Commercial Interest.** Liking for trade, business and money-related transactions — accountancy, banking, marketing, retail business, finance, etc. Persons high on this area are practical, alert to economic opportunities and skilful in handling money.

5. **Constructive Interest** (also called *Technical* interest in some adaptations of the V.I.R.). Liking for working with tools, machines and material objects — mechanics, electrician, carpenter, motor-vehicle technician, computer hardware technician, civil construction work, etc. Persons high on this area enjoy "putting things together" with their hands.

6. **Artistic Interest.** Liking for occupations involving creative expression in colour, form, sound or movement — painting, music, dance, sculpture, fashion design, interior design, photography, etc. Such persons are imaginative, sensitive and aesthetically inclined.

7. **Agricultural Interest.** Liking for working with plants, soil, animals and the open environment — farming, horticulture, dairy, animal husbandry, agricultural extension, agricultural research, etc. Such persons enjoy the outdoors and rural life.

8. **Persuasive Interest.** Liking for occupations involving the influencing of other people — marketing and sales, advertising, public relations, law, politics, social leadership, etc. Such persons enjoy talking, convincing and motivating others.

9. **Social Interest.** Liking for occupations involving the welfare of human beings — teaching, nursing, social work, counselling, medical care, NGO work, child care, etc. Such persons are warm, helpful and interested in human service.

10. **Household Interest.** Liking for occupations and activities centred on the home — cooking, cleaning, child rearing, dietetics, home economics, hotel and catering services, fashion and textile design, interior decoration, etc. Such persons enjoy creating a comfortable home environment.

These ten areas, developed by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha, were derived through factor analysis of Indian occupational data and are known to provide an exhaustive coverage of the major Indian vocations. Their measurement has been the focus of the present investigation.

## 1.9 Gender Differences in Career Choices

Gender is one of the most pervasive social variables in human life, and it makes its presence felt in the area of career choice as much as in any other area. A large body of research from India and abroad has consistently reported significant differences in the vocational interest profiles of men and women, especially during the adolescent years. The principal patterns reported in the literature are:

a. *Boys* tend to score significantly higher than *girls* on Scientific, Constructive (Technical), Mechanical, Outdoor, Agricultural, Persuasive and Executive interest areas.

b. *Girls* tend to score significantly higher than *boys* on Literary, Artistic, Social, Clerical and Household interest areas.

c. *Commercial* interest, in many studies, has been found to be relatively gender-neutral.

These patterns, however, are not biologically fixed; they are shaped to a great extent by *socialisation*. In a society where the boy is encouraged from infancy to play with mechanical toys and is told that he is destined for a "real" career, while the girl is given dolls, kitchen sets and the message that her primary calling is the home, the resulting interest patterns become almost predictable. The challenge for educational research is therefore not merely to *describe* such patterns but to *understand* the social processes that produce them and to suggest *educational interventions* that can liberate the adolescent learner — both boy and girl — from limiting stereotypes.

The present study examines whether the patterns reported in the literature are also visible in a contemporary Meerut sample, and if so, how strongly they are visible.

## 1.10 Need and Significance of the Study

The need for the present study arises from the convergence of several considerations.

First, *adolescence* is the most critical stage of vocational development, and yet the vast majority of Indian secondary school students continue to make career decisions on the basis of *parental advice, peer influence and social fashion*, without any systematic measurement of their own vocational interests. A study that documents the vocational interest pattern of contemporary secondary school students adds to the empirical knowledge base required for an evidence-based career-guidance practice.

Second, the *gender differences* in vocational interest, although extensively studied in the Western literature, have not been adequately examined in the *Indian context* in recent years. Most Indian studies on the subject date from the 1970s and 1980s. The social position of girls in India has changed substantially since then, and a fresh empirical examination is overdue.

Third, the *Meerut District* of Western Uttar Pradesh — the field of the present investigation — is one of the rapidly urbanising districts of the country. It contains, side by side, deeply traditional rural communities and modern urban-CBSE schools that prepare students for international careers. A comparative picture of the vocational interests of boys and girls drawn from these contrasting milieus is of considerable contextual significance.

Fourth, the *findings* of the study can directly inform the work of class teachers, school administrators, parents and trained counsellors of the district. The educational implications and suggestions presented in Chapter V of the dissertation are intended to be of direct practical use.

Fifth, the National Education Policy 2020 has re-asserted the centrality of *career awareness, skill education and counselling* at the secondary stage. Empirical studies of the present type are necessary inputs for the implementation of these recommendations.

The investigator therefore considers that the proposed study is of *educational, social, theoretical and practical significance*, and that it will make a small but useful contribution to the field of vocational guidance in India.

## 1.11 Statement of the Problem

The problem of the present investigation has been precisely stated as:

> ***"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level."***

The study is descriptive in nature and proposes to compare the vocational interest profiles of secondary school boys and girls of Class IX and X, drawn from two schools of Meerut District, on the ten standardised areas of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record*.

## 1.12 Operational Definitions of the Terms Used

For the purposes of the present investigation, the principal terms used in the title and statement of the problem have been defined as follows:

**Comparative Study.** "Comparative study" in the present investigation refers to the *systematic comparison* of the vocational interest scores of two groups — boys and girls — on each of the ten areas of the Vocational Interest Record, using the *t*-test for independent samples and percentage analysis.

**Vocational Interest.** *Vocational interest*, for the purpose of the present investigation, has been operationally defined as the *score obtained by the student on each of the ten areas* of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record*. A higher score in an area indicates a stronger preference for vocations falling in that area.

**Boys.** "Boys" refers to the *male students* of Class IX and X drawn through random sampling from *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut*, whose age lies between 13 and 16 years.

**Girls.** "Girls" refers to the *female students* of Class IX and X drawn through random sampling from *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut*, whose age lies between 13 and 16 years.

**Secondary Level.** "Secondary level" in the present study refers to *Classes IX and X*, which constitute the secondary stage in the U.P. Board (10 + 2) and the CBSE pattern of school education.

**Government School.** A *Government school* is one which is owned, financed and managed by the Government of Uttar Pradesh through the U.P. Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad — in this study, *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur*.

**Private School.** A *Private school* is one which is privately managed under a registered society and is affiliated with the Central Board of Secondary Education — in this study, *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh*.

## 1.13 Objectives of the Study

The study has been designed to fulfil the following five major objectives:

**Objective 1.** To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and Standard Deviation) of secondary school *boys* on the ten areas of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record.

**Objective 2.** To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and Standard Deviation) of secondary school *girls* on the ten areas of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record.

**Objective 3.** To compare the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls on each of the ten areas of vocational interest by means of the *t*-test of significance.

**Objective 4.** To compute the percentage of boys and girls falling at the *high*, *average* and *low* levels of vocational interest in each of the ten areas, and to compare the two distributions.

**Objective 5.** To determine the *rank order* of the ten areas of vocational interest of boys and of girls, and to examine the extent of agreement between the two rank orders.

## 1.14 Hypotheses of the Study

In the light of the above objectives and the trends reported in the related literature, the following null hypotheses (H₀) have been formulated for empirical testing:

**H₀1.** There is no significant difference between the mean scores of boys and girls of secondary level on each of the ten areas of vocational interest, namely Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

**H₀2.** Boys and girls of secondary level do not differ significantly in the percentage distribution of their vocational interest at the *high*, *average* and *low* levels in any of the ten areas.

**H₀3.** Boys and girls of secondary level do not differ significantly in the rank order in which they place the ten areas of vocational interest.

In view of the literature reviewed in Chapter II, the corresponding *research* (alternative) hypotheses have also been kept in mind. They postulate that boys will obtain higher mean scores than girls on Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Agricultural and Persuasive areas, and that girls will obtain higher mean scores on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household areas. The empirical results presented in Chapter IV will determine whether the null hypotheses can be rejected in favour of these alternatives.

## 1.15 Delimitations of the Study

Every educational research project must necessarily restrict its scope so that the work can be completed within the time, financial and human resources available to the investigator. The present study is delimited as follows:

i. The study is delimited to the *Meerut District* of Western Uttar Pradesh.

ii. It is further delimited to *two schools* of the district — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur (Government, rural, U.P. Board) and K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh (Private, urban, CBSE).

iii. The sample is delimited to *40 students* — 20 boys and 20 girls — selected from Classes IX and X.

iv. The age of the sample is delimited to the range *13 – 16 years*.

v. The variable of *vocational interest* has been measured solely through *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)*. Other tools have not been used.

vi. The study is delimited to *ten* areas of vocational interest, viz. Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

vii. The statistical analysis is delimited to *Mean, Standard Deviation, t-test for two independent samples, and Percentage Analysis*. ANOVA, correlation and regression have not been used.

viii. The study is delimited to the academic *Session 2025–2026*.

## 1.16 Assumptions of the Study

The present investigation rests on the following assumptions:

i. The *Vocational Interest Record* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha is a valid and reliable instrument for the measurement of the vocational interests of Indian secondary school students.

ii. The students of the sample have responded to the items of the Record *honestly* and to the best of their understanding, on the assurance that their responses would be kept confidential and would not affect their academic standing.

iii. The 40 students drawn through random sampling are *fairly representative* of secondary level boys and girls of Meerut District.

iv. The scores obtained by the students on the Record are *interval-scale* data, and therefore parametric statistics (Mean, S.D., *t*-test) are appropriate.

v. Class IX and X students are at a stage at which their vocational interests are sufficiently *crystallised* to be reliably measured.

## 1.17 Organization of the Study

The present dissertation has been organised into *five chapters* in the manner customary for M.Ed. dissertations of Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut:

**Chapter I — Introduction.** The present chapter, which conceptually introduces the problem, presents the principal theories and definitions, formulates the problem, objectives, hypotheses, delimitations and operational definitions.

**Chapter II — Review of Related Literature.** Surveys the available Indian and foreign studies on vocational interest, identifies the dominant trends and the research gap that the present study seeks to fill.

**Chapter III — Research Methodology.** Describes the design of the study, the variables, population, sample and sampling technique, the description of the schools, the *Vocational Interest Record* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha, the procedure of data collection, the scoring procedure and the statistical techniques used.

**Chapter IV — Data Analysis and Interpretation.** Presents the area-wise scores of boys and girls, the *t*-tests, the percentage distributions, the rank orders and the corresponding interpretations.

**Chapter V — Summary, Findings, Conclusions and Suggestions.** Summarises the entire study, presents the major findings, draws conclusions, discusses educational implications, and offers suggestions for teachers, parents, school administrators, guidance counsellors and future researchers, along with a statement of limitations.

The dissertation closes with a list of *References / Bibliography* in APA 7th edition format and a set of *Appendices* containing the sample format of the *Vocational Interest Record*, the permission letter, raw data tables, sample calculations and student response sheets.
