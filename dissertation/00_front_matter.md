## PREFACE

The Secondary School stage in India coincides with the period of *adolescence* — a stage in human life that has been described in psychological literature as a "period of storm and stress" but also as a "period of self-discovery". It is during these years that the young learner first becomes aware of his or her own inclinations, talents and aspirations, and begins to translate these vague preferences into concrete educational and occupational decisions. The vocational choices made at this stage have far-reaching consequences for the individual, the family and society at large.

Educational psychologists are unanimous in their opinion that *vocational interest* is one of the strongest single predictors of vocational satisfaction and occupational success. An interest in a vocation, when supported by appropriate ability and opportunity, leads to engaged learning, sustained effort and a sense of fulfilment in adult life. Conversely, a wrong choice — made under the pressure of parents, peers or social fashion — frequently results in frustration, poor performance, low productivity and, in extreme cases, social withdrawal. The systematic and scientific identification of vocational interests at the secondary stage therefore assumes great educational and social significance.

In the Indian context, the National Education Policy 2020 has reaffirmed the importance of *vocational education and career counselling* in the secondary stage. It has recommended that every student should have access to systematic vocational exposure from Class 6 onwards and should be encouraged to develop a *career vision* by the end of Class 10. In this background, an investigation into the actual vocational interest pattern of secondary school boys and girls in a real Indian setting is both timely and necessary.

The present dissertation, *"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"*, is a humble attempt in this direction. It is based on a sample of 40 students of Classes IX and X drawn through random sampling technique from two schools of Meerut District — *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur* (a Government rural school) and *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh* (a Private urban CBSE-affiliated school). The vocational interest of the students has been measured by means of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record* and the data have been treated with appropriate descriptive and inferential statistical techniques.

The dissertation has been organised into five chapters. Chapter I introduces the problem and lays down the conceptual foundation. Chapter II surveys the related literature both in India and abroad. Chapter III gives a detailed account of the methodology adopted. Chapter IV presents the analysis and interpretation of the data with the help of suitable tables. Chapter V summarises the study, presents the major findings and offers educational implications and suggestions.

It is hoped that the present investigation will throw fresh light on the vocational interest pattern of secondary school boys and girls of Meerut and will be useful to teachers, school administrators, guidance counsellors, parents and future researchers in the field.

## ABSTRACT

The present investigation is entitled **"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"**. It has been undertaken in the academic district of Meerut (Uttar Pradesh) under the affiliation of Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut, during the academic session 2025–2026.

The Secondary School stage in India coincides with the developmental period of *early adolescence* during which an individual begins to crystallise his or her vocational choices. Choices made at this stage have far-reaching consequences not merely for the academic future of the learner but also for his or her later occupational adjustment, life satisfaction and contribution to society. In this background, a systematic enquiry into the comparative vocational interests of secondary school *boys* and *girls* in a real Indian setting has been considered both timely and educationally significant.

**Objectives of the Study.** The investigation was designed to fulfil the following five major objectives: (i) to study the area-wise vocational interest scores of secondary school boys; (ii) to study the area-wise vocational interest scores of secondary school girls; (iii) to compare the mean scores of boys and girls on each of the ten areas of vocational interest; (iv) to determine the percentage of boys and girls falling at the *high*, *average* and *low* levels of vocational interest in each area; and (v) to identify the rank order in which boys and girls place the ten vocational interest areas.

**Hypotheses.** Three principal null hypotheses were formulated. Hypothesis I postulated that there is no significant difference between the mean scores of boys and girls on the ten areas of vocational interest. Hypothesis II proposed that boys and girls do not differ significantly in the percentage distribution of their interest at the high, average and low levels. Hypothesis III stated that boys and girls do not differ in the rank order of vocational interest areas.

**Method and Sample.** The investigation followed the *descriptive survey method* of educational research. A total of **40 students** of Classes IX and X were selected through the *simple random sampling technique* — **20 boys** from Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur (a Government rural school under the U.P. Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad) and **20 girls** from K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh (a Private urban CBSE-affiliated school), both situated in Meerut District.

**Tool.** The *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* developed and standardised by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** was used. The Record measures interest in *ten* well-defined vocational areas, namely Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

**Statistical Techniques.** Mean, Standard Deviation, *t*-test for the difference between two independent means and Percentage Analysis were used.

**Major Findings.** The data analysis revealed: (i) Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls on the Scientific (t = 4.54, p < 0.01), Constructive/Technical (t = 5.96, p < 0.01) and Agricultural (t = 4.48, p < 0.01) areas. (ii) Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys on the Literary (t = 4.02, p < 0.01), Artistic (t = 5.07, p < 0.01), Social (t = 3.93, p < 0.01) and Household (t = 9.36, p < 0.01) areas. (iii) On the Executive (t = 1.91), Commercial (t = 1.17) and Persuasive (t = 1.31) areas the difference between boys and girls was *not* statistically significant. (iv) The most popular area of interest for boys was *Constructive/Technical* (rank 1) followed by *Scientific* (rank 2) and *Agricultural* (rank 3), whereas for girls it was *Household* (rank 1) followed by *Artistic* (rank 2) and *Social* (rank 3). The Spearman rank-order correlation between the two profiles is *ρ = −0.88*, a strong systematic reversal. (v) About 60 per cent of the boys reach the "high" level on the Constructive area and 60 per cent of the girls on the Household area, indicating fairly well-formed and sharply gender-typed vocational preferences at the secondary stage.

**Educational Implications.** The findings of the present investigation point clearly to the existence of a *gender-typed* pattern of vocational interest in Indian secondary schools. While such a pattern partly reflects genuine biological and psychological differences, it is also fed by social stereotyping, parental expectations and the limited exposure that girls — especially rural girls — receive to scientific and technical vocations. It is therefore strongly recommended that career guidance services in secondary schools should make conscious efforts to (a) expose girls to scientific, technical and entrepreneurial careers; (b) expose boys to artistic, social and care-giving vocations; and (c) provide every adolescent learner, regardless of gender, with an unbiased opportunity to identify and pursue his or her own line of vocational interest.

The dissertation thus contributes a small but useful body of empirical evidence on the comparative vocational interest pattern of secondary school boys and girls of Meerut and offers concrete suggestions for teachers, parents, school administrators, guidance counsellors and future researchers in the field.
