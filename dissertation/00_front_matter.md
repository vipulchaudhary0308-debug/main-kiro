## ABSTRACT

The present investigation is entitled **"A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students of Rural Government and Urban Private Schools of Meerut District"**. The study has been conducted in the district of Meerut (Uttar Pradesh) under the academic jurisdiction of Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut, during the session 2025–2026.

Adolescence is a crucial period in the life of a young person. It is during this stage that the boy or the girl first begins to think seriously about his or her future career. The Secondary School stage in India coincides with this exploratory period of vocational thinking. The choices made at this stage have far-reaching effects, not only on the academic future of the learner but also on his or her later happiness, satisfaction with work and contribution to society. The systematic and scientific identification of vocational interests at the secondary stage is, therefore, both an educational and a social necessity.

Two factors that often influence the vocational interest pattern of an adolescent are (a) the *type of locality* in which he / she lives — rural or urban — and (b) the *type of school* in which he / she studies — government or private. The present investigator has selected two schools that represent the two extremes of these two factors put together: one *Rural Government* school (Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut) and one *Urban Private CBSE-affiliated* school (K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut). *Gender* (boys / girls) and *class* (IX / X) are the two additional independent variables.

**Objectives.** The study has been planned with five major objectives, the most important of which are: (a) to study the area-wise vocational interest pattern of the total sample of secondary school students, (b) to find out the percentage distribution of high, average and low levels of interest in each of the ten areas, (c) to compare the vocational interests of boys and girls, (d) to compare the vocational interests of Rural Government and Urban Private school students, and (e) to compare the vocational interests of Class IX and Class X students.

**Hypotheses.** Three null hypotheses have been framed, postulating that no significant difference exists between (i) boys and girls, (ii) Rural Government and Urban Private school students, and (iii) Class IX and Class X students, on any of the ten areas of vocational interest.

**Method and Sample.** The investigation is *descriptive* in nature and has employed the *survey method* of educational research. A total of **60 students** has been selected through the *stratified random sampling technique* — **30 from each of the two schools** — with **15 students of Class IX** (7 boys + 8 girls) and **15 students of Class X** (8 boys + 7 girls) from each school. Hence the sample is balanced as **30 boys + 30 girls = 60 students** in all.

**Tool.** The *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* developed and standardised by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** has been used. The Record measures interest in ten well-defined vocational areas, namely Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

**Statistical Techniques.** *Mean* (to find the average level of interest), *Standard Deviation* (to study the spread or variability of the scores), *t-test* (to compare boys and girls, the two schools, and the two classes), and *percentages* (to show the distribution of the students into Low, Average and High levels of interest in each area) have been used.

**Major Findings.** The data analysis revealed that:

(i) On the whole sample, *Social* (M = 12.78), *Scientific* (M = 12.50) and *Household* (M = 12.40) areas attracted the greatest interest, while *Persuasive* (M = 11.17) and *Commercial* (M = 11.43) areas attracted the lowest interest.

(ii) The percentage analysis showed that 45.0 per cent of the students had a *high* level of interest in the Social area, 35.0 per cent in the Scientific area and 31.7 per cent each in the Executive and Household areas.

(iii) **Boys** scored significantly higher than **girls** on the Scientific (t = 2.77, p < .01), Constructive (t = 3.78, p < .01) and Persuasive (t = 1.90, NS — but trend present) areas; whereas **girls** scored significantly higher than **boys** on the Literary (t = 2.31, p < .05), Social (t = 3.70, p < .01) and Household (t = 3.69, p < .01) areas.

(iv) **Urban Private** school students scored significantly higher than **Rural Government** school students on the Scientific (t = 2.58, p < .05), Executive (t = 4.55, p < .01), Commercial (t = 4.96, p < .01), Artistic (t = 5.04, p < .01) and Persuasive (t = 3.09, p < .01) areas; whereas **Rural Government** school students scored significantly higher than **Urban Private** students on the Constructive (t = 2.36, p < .05), Agricultural (t = 3.66, p < .01) and Household (t = 3.43, p < .01) areas.

(v) **Class X** students scored significantly higher than **Class IX** students only on the Scientific and Agricultural areas; on the remaining eight areas, the difference between the two classes was *not* statistically significant.

**Implications.** On the basis of the findings, important educational implications have been drawn. The most important of these are: career-guidance services in rural government schools need urgent strengthening; vocational fairs, exposure visits and career-awareness talks should be regularly organised; the school curriculum at the secondary stage should provide specially designed enrichment activities for those interest areas in which the school has been found to be lagging; and parents need orientation regarding gender-equality in occupational choices.

Suggestions for further research and limitations of the study have also been incorporated in the last chapter.
