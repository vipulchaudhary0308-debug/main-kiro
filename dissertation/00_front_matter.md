## PREFACE

---

**PREFACE**

The years of adolescence are perhaps the most significant and transformative phase in a person's life. It is during this period that young minds begin to seriously wonder about their future — *Who am I? What do I want to become? What kind of work will bring me satisfaction and success?* These are not merely passing thoughts; they are the early stirrings of vocational awareness that will eventually shape life decisions of the greatest consequence.

As a student of Education at Meerut College, I have had the privilege of studying the psychological and sociological dimensions of learning and human development. When the time came to choose a topic for my M.Ed. dissertation, I found myself drawn naturally to the area of vocational guidance — particularly the question of whether boys and girls in Indian schools differ in their vocational interests, and whether the type of school (rural government vs. urban private) plays any role in shaping those interests.

This dissertation, titled **"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"**, is a modest attempt to explore these questions through systematic, evidence-based educational research. The study focuses on students from two schools in the Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut district — **Shri Sanskrit Inter College** (a rural government school) and **KP International School** (an urban private CBSE school). A sample of 60 students (30 boys and 30 girls) was selected through random sampling, and their vocational interests were assessed using the **S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record (VIR)**.

The present work has been organized into five chapters. The first chapter introduces the concept of vocational interest and establishes the context of the study. The second chapter presents a comprehensive review of related literature from both Indian and international researchers. The third chapter describes the research methodology in detail. The fourth chapter presents data analysis and interpretation, supported by statistical tables and colourful visual representations. The fifth and final chapter offers a summary of findings, conclusions, and practical suggestions for educators, parents, and policymakers.

I have made every sincere effort to ensure academic rigour, clarity of expression, and authenticity of data throughout this work. However, like all research endeavours, this study too has its limitations, which have been honestly acknowledged.

I hope this dissertation will serve as a useful resource for educators, guidance counsellors, and future researchers who are interested in the vocational development of adolescent students in the Indian educational context.
