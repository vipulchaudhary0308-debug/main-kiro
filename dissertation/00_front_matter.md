## PREFACE

Adolescence is the most formative stage of human life. It is during these few years — roughly between the ages of fourteen and seventeen — that the boy or the girl first begins to think seriously about *what to do in life*. The choice of an occupation is one of the most far-reaching decisions that a person ever makes, for it determines not only the level of income and the social status that the person will later enjoy but also, and more importantly, the level of personal satisfaction and the quality of life that he or she will lead. The Secondary School stage in India coincides exactly with this exploratory period of vocational thinking, and it is the duty of the Indian educational system to give the adolescent boy and girl every possible help in *understanding their own interests* and in *aligning their choices with those interests*.

The present investigation has grown out of the investigator's own concern with this very issue. During my visits to a number of secondary schools of Meerut District in connection with the M.Ed. course-work, I noticed that many of the Class IX and Class X students had only the *vaguest* ideas about their future careers. The boys, almost as a matter of course, talked of becoming engineers, doctors, soldiers or government officers; the girls, with surprising frequency, talked of becoming teachers, nurses, beauticians or housewives. These dreams seemed to flow not from any *personal exploration* of the self, but from the *traditional gender expectations* of the families and the neighbourhoods in which the children were growing up. The question that troubled me was — *Are these dreams genuinely the dreams of these adolescents, or are they merely the inherited preferences of their elders?*

The way to answer this question, I felt, was to administer a *standardised vocational interest tool* to a representative sample of secondary school boys and girls and to *compare* their measured interests area by area. The S.P. Kulshrestha *Vocational Interest Record* offered itself as the most suitable tool for this purpose — it has been standardised on the Indian high-school population, it is in Hindi (and is, therefore, suited to both medium-of-instruction groups in Meerut District), and it covers ten well-defined vocational areas which together encompass the major occupational fields available in the country.

In order to draw a balanced and reasonably representative sample, the investigator selected two contrasting secondary schools of Meerut District — one *Rural Government* school (Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur) and one *Urban Private* C.B.S.E. school (K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh). From each school, ten boys and ten girls of Class IX and ten boys and ten girls of Class X were selected through *random sampling*. The total sample of **80 students** (40 boys and 40 girls) thus obtained provided a sufficiently broad cross-section of the secondary school population of the district to permit a meaningful gender comparison.

The present work is a small but sincere attempt at such a study. It is offered, in all humility, as my contribution to the slowly growing body of empirical research on the *career-related psychology* of Indian adolescents. If the findings of the study help even a few teachers, parents and counsellors of Meerut District to plan more *informed*, *gender-sensitive* and *evidence-based* career-guidance services for their children, the labour of writing this dissertation will have been amply rewarded.

The dissertation has been organised into five chapters. Chapter I introduces the problem and defines its boundaries. Chapter II surveys the related literature both in India and abroad. Chapter III describes the methodology. Chapter IV presents the analysis and the interpretation of the data. Chapter V brings together the major findings, draws conclusions, lists the educational implications and offers suggestions for further work. References (in A.P.A. 7th edition style) and appendices are provided at the end.

I am conscious of the limitations of the present study, the most important of which is the *small size of the sample* (80 students drawn from only two schools). The findings should, therefore, be treated as *exploratory* rather than as *definitive*. It is hoped, however, that the work, in its modest way, will encourage other researchers to take up larger and deeper investigations of the same area.

## ABSTRACT

The present investigation is entitled **"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"**. The study has been conducted in Meerut District of Uttar Pradesh, in partial fulfilment of the requirements for the award of the degree of *Master of Education (M.Ed.)* of Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut, through the Department of Education, Meerut College, Meerut, during the academic session 2025–2026.

**Background.** The Secondary School stage (Classes IX and X) coincides with the *Exploration stage* of Super's life-stage model of vocational development. It is at this stage that the adolescent boy or girl first thinks seriously about a future career and begins to align his / her academic and co-curricular choices with that thinking. *Gender* has long been recognised as one of the most important variables that shape the vocational interest pattern of adolescents. The present study examines whether the gender-based differentiation of vocational interests, reported by Indian researchers since the 1970s, continues to hold in the secondary schools of Meerut District in the year 2025.

**Objectives.** The investigation has been planned with three objectives:

(i) to study the area-wise vocational interest pattern of secondary school students of Meerut District;

(ii) to compare the mean vocational interest scores of *boys* and *girls* on each of the ten areas of the *Vocational Interest Record* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha;

(iii) to find out the percentage distribution of boys and girls in the *low*, *average* and *high* levels of interest on each of the ten areas.

**Hypothesis.** The principal null hypothesis of the study is — *"There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls of secondary school stage on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R."*

**Method.** The study employed the *descriptive survey method* of educational research. A sample of **80 students** (40 boys + 40 girls) of Class IX and Class X was selected through *random sampling* from two contrasting secondary schools of Meerut District —

(a) **Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut** (Rural Government, U.P. Board) — 10 boys + 10 girls of Class IX and 10 boys + 10 girls of Class X = 40 students; and

(b) **K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut** (Urban Private, C.B.S.E.) — 10 boys + 10 girls of Class IX and 10 boys + 10 girls of Class X = 40 students.

The standardised *Vocational Interest Record (V.I.R.)* developed by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha was used as the principal tool of measurement. The data were analysed by *Mean*, *Standard Deviation*, *t-test for independent samples* and *Percentage Analysis*.

**Major Findings.** The principal findings of the study are:

(i) The total sample (N = 80) showed the highest mean interest in *Household* (M = 12.27), *Scientific* (M = 12.22) and *Artistic* (M = 11.89) areas, and the lowest mean interest in *Persuasive* (M = 10.75) and *Agricultural* (M = 11.39) areas.

(ii) **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the *Scientific* (t = 5.15, p < .01), *Executive* (t = 3.00, p < .01), *Constructive / Technical* (t = 3.16, p < .01), *Agricultural* (t = 3.14, p < .01) and *Persuasive* (t = 1.99, p < .05) areas.

(iii) **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the *Artistic* (t = 3.32, p < .01), *Social* (t = 3.49, p < .01) and *Household* (t = 6.93, p < .01) areas.

(iv) On the *Literary* and *Commercial* areas, the difference between boys and girls was *not statistically significant*.

(v) The percentage analysis confirmed the trend obtained from the *t*-tests. For example, in the *high-interest* range (score 14 – 20), 47.5 per cent of the girls fell on the Household area as against only 7.5 per cent of the boys; and 30.0 per cent of the boys fell on the Scientific area as against only 12.5 per cent of the girls.

**Conclusion.** The null hypothesis was *partly rejected* (on eight of the ten areas) and *partly retained* (on two of the ten areas). The study, accordingly, supports the long-standing finding of Indian and foreign research that *gender remains a robust predictor of the vocational interest pattern of secondary school adolescents*, especially on the *People–Things* dimension proposed by Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009).

**Implications.** Career-guidance services in the secondary schools of Meerut District should be *gender-sensitive*. Special programmes are needed to (a) encourage girls to take up *Scientific*, *Constructive / Technical*, *Agricultural* and *Executive* careers, and (b) encourage boys to consider *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household / Service* careers, so that the gender-based occupational segregation of the Indian work-force is gradually reduced.
