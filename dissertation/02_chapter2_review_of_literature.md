# CHAPTER 2: REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 Introduction

A thorough and systematic review of related literature is an indispensable component of any scholarly research. It serves multiple purposes simultaneously: it situates the current study within the broader landscape of existing knowledge, helps the researcher understand what has already been investigated, identifies methodological approaches that have been found effective, and — most importantly — reveals the gaps and lacunae in existing knowledge that the present study seeks to address. As Best and Kahn (2010) noted, "A review of related literature enables the researcher to define the frontiers of his field and thereby contribute to its development."

The present chapter reviews a total of **fifteen studies** — ten from the Indian context and five from foreign countries — that are directly or indirectly relevant to the themes of vocational interests, gender differences in career aspirations, and rural-urban comparisons in educational settings. Each study is reviewed with reference to its author(s), year of publication, objectives, methodology, sample, and findings. The chapter concludes with a critical analysis of the reviewed literature and an identification of the specific research gap that the present study addresses.

## 2.2 Indian Studies

### Study 1: Thakur and Saini (1980)

**Author(s):** D.S. Thakur and R.P. Saini

**Year:** 1980

**Title:** *Vocational Interest Patterns of Secondary School Students in Himachal Pradesh*

**Objectives:** To identify and compare the vocational interest patterns of male and female students at the secondary level in Himachal Pradesh; to examine the influence of school location (rural/urban) on vocational interests.

**Method:** Descriptive Survey Method using the S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record.

**Sample:** 400 secondary school students (200 boys and 200 girls) from both rural and urban schools in Shimla and Kangra districts.

**Findings:** The study found that boys consistently showed higher interests in technical and agricultural vocational areas, while girls displayed significantly higher interests in social, household, and artistic fields. Urban students, regardless of gender, demonstrated broader and more diverse interest profiles than rural students. The difference in technical interest between boys and girls was statistically significant at the 0.01 level. The researchers attributed the gender differences primarily to the differential socialisation of boys and girls in Indian society, and the rural-urban differences to the varying quality of vocational exposure available in different school environments.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This foundational study established the key patterns of gender and location-based differences in vocational interests using the same tool (KVIR) employed in the present study, making its findings directly comparable.

---

### Study 2: Bhatnagar (1993)

**Author(s):** O.P. Bhatnagar

**Year:** 1993

**Title:** *Gender Differences in Vocational Interests Among Adolescents in Rajasthan*

**Objectives:** To assess the nature and extent of gender differences in vocational interests among adolescent students; to identify dominant vocational interest areas for each gender.

**Method:** Descriptive Survey Method; S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record administered to the sample.

**Sample:** 320 students (160 boys, 160 girls) from Class IX and X in Jaipur and surrounding rural districts of Rajasthan.

**Findings:** Boys showed significantly higher interest in technical, scientific, and executive areas. Girls showed significantly higher interest in social, household, and artistic areas. Commercial and literary interests were comparable across genders, with only marginal differences. Bhatnagar noted that the gender differences in Rajasthan were more pronounced than those reported in studies from more industrialised states, which he attributed to the stronger patriarchal cultural norms in Rajasthan. He emphasised that without proper vocational guidance, many girls in rural Rajasthan were likely to channel their interests into domestically approved areas at the cost of scientifically and professionally oriented aspirations.

**Relevance to Present Study:** The study provides a comparative reference from another North Indian state with similar cultural characteristics to Uttar Pradesh, and its findings about rural girls' vocational interests are particularly relevant to the present study's context.

---

### Study 3: Sharma and Mishra (2001)

**Author(s):** R.K. Sharma and P. Mishra

**Year:** 2001

**Title:** *Vocational Interests and Academic Achievement: A Study of Secondary School Students in Uttar Pradesh*

**Objectives:** To examine the relationship between vocational interests and academic achievement among secondary school students; to compare vocational interests across gender and school type.

**Method:** Correlational and comparative research design; KVIR and school academic records used.

**Sample:** 280 students (140 boys, 140 girls) from secondary schools in Lucknow and Faizabad districts of Uttar Pradesh.

**Findings:** A positive and significant correlation was found between the alignment of vocational interests with academic stream and academic performance. Students who studied in streams aligned with their dominant vocational interest scored significantly higher in examinations. Boys dominated in scientific and technical interests; girls dominated in social and household areas. The study also found that urban students showed higher scientific and commercial interests compared to rural students, who showed more pronounced agricultural and technical interests.

**Relevance to Present Study:** Being specifically from Uttar Pradesh, this study provides the closest geographical parallel to the present study. Its findings about gender and school-type differences in vocational interests in UP serve as a useful benchmark.

---

### Study 4: Verma and Gupta (2004)

**Author(s):** S. Verma and A.K. Gupta

**Year:** 2004

**Title:** *Vocational Interests of Rural and Urban Adolescents: A Comparative Analysis*

**Objectives:** To compare vocational interests of rural and urban adolescent students; to identify how socioeconomic background moderates these differences.

**Method:** Descriptive Survey; KVIR and a socioeconomic status scale used.

**Sample:** 360 students from rural (180) and urban (180) secondary schools in Madhya Pradesh; equal representation of boys and girls.

**Findings:** Urban adolescents showed significantly higher interests in scientific, commercial, and executive fields. Rural adolescents showed significantly higher agricultural interest. Social and literary interests were not significantly different between rural and urban groups. When socioeconomic status was controlled, many rural-urban differences became smaller, suggesting that economic background partially mediates the location effect on vocational interests. Gender differences persisted independently of location.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This study's direct focus on rural-urban comparison using the same tool makes it a key reference point for interpreting the rural-urban findings of the present study.

---

### Study 5: Srivastava (2007)

**Author(s):** M.N. Srivastava

**Year:** 2007

**Title:** *Impact of Parental Occupation on Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students*

**Objectives:** To study the influence of parental occupation on the vocational interest patterns of secondary school students; to examine gender-based differences in this influence.

**Method:** Survey method; structured interview schedule alongside KVIR.

**Sample:** 200 secondary school students (100 boys, 100 girls) from Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh.

**Findings:** A strong positive correlation was observed between parental occupation and the agricultural and technical interest of male students. Sons of farmers showed significantly higher agricultural interest; sons of mechanics showed higher technical interest. This pattern was weaker but still present for girls. Srivastava concluded that family occupational background acts as a primary socialising agent for vocational interests, particularly in rural areas. However, he also noted that exposure to formal schooling progressively diversifies students' interest profiles beyond family occupation.

**Relevance to Present Study:** The role of family background in shaping vocational interests, particularly in the rural Uttar Pradesh context, is directly relevant to interpreting differences between rural and urban students in the present study.

---

### Study 6: Kaur and Singh (2009)

**Author(s):** H. Kaur and R. Singh

**Year:** 2009

**Title:** *Vocational Interests and Career Maturity Among Senior Secondary Students in Punjab*

**Objectives:** To assess vocational interests and career maturity of senior secondary students; to compare these across gender lines.

**Method:** Descriptive Survey; Career Maturity Inventory (CMI) and KVIR used.

**Sample:** 350 students (175 boys, 175 girls) from government and private schools in Amritsar and Ludhiana.

**Findings:** Boys showed higher technical and executive interests while girls showed higher social and household interests. Interestingly, girls showed significantly higher career maturity scores than boys, suggesting that while boys may have broader interests, girls are often more focused and serious in their vocational thinking. Private school students of both genders showed higher career maturity and more diverse interest profiles than government school students. The researchers argued for the integration of formal career maturity programmes in government schools.

**Relevance to Present Study:** The finding that girls show higher career maturity despite lower scores in technical domains provides a nuanced perspective that enriches the interpretation of gender differences in the present study.

### Study 7: Yadav and Sharma (2012)

**Author(s):** R.P. Yadav and N. Sharma

**Year:** 2012

**Title:** *A Study of Vocational Interests of Secondary Level Students in Relation to Gender and Locality*

**Objectives:** To study vocational interests in relation to gender and locality (rural/urban) among secondary school students; to test statistical significance of differences.

**Method:** Survey method with t-test and ANOVA; KVIR used.

**Sample:** 400 students (200 boys, 200 girls; 200 rural, 200 urban) from secondary schools in Rajasthan.

**Findings:** Significant gender differences were found in technical (boys higher), social (girls higher), artistic (girls higher), and household (girls higher) interest areas. Significant location differences were found in agricultural (rural higher) and commercial (urban higher) areas. The interaction between gender and location was significant for household interest, where rural girls scored considerably higher than all other groups. The researchers recommended the use of interest inventories as a regular part of the school guidance programme.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This study's simultaneous examination of gender and locality effects on vocational interests closely parallels the design of the present study.

---

### Study 8: Gupta and Choudhary (2016)

**Author(s):** P. Gupta and S. Choudhary

**Year:** 2016

**Title:** *Vocational Interest Patterns of Secondary School Girls: A Comparative Study of Government and Private Schools*

**Objectives:** To compare the vocational interest patterns of secondary school girls studying in government and private schools; to examine the role of school environment in shaping girls' vocational interests.

**Method:** Survey method; KVIR administered to the sample.

**Sample:** 300 girls (150 from government schools, 150 from private schools) in Jaipur, Rajasthan.

**Findings:** Government school girls showed higher household, social, and agricultural interests, while private school girls showed higher scientific, commercial, and executive interests. The differences in scientific and executive interests were statistically significant. The researchers concluded that the school environment — including teacher attitudes, exposure to role models, and extracurricular activities — plays a significant role in shaping the vocational interests of girls. Private school environments were found to be more conducive to developing gender-atypical career interests in girls.

**Relevance to Present Study:** The specific focus on girls across school types directly informs the present study's comparison of rural government and urban private school girls.

---

### Study 9: Mishra and Tripathi (2018)

**Author(s):** A. Mishra and R. Tripathi

**Year:** 2018

**Title:** *Vocational Interests and Occupational Aspirations of Secondary School Students in Uttar Pradesh: A Rural-Urban Comparison*

**Objectives:** To compare vocational interests and occupational aspirations of secondary school students from rural and urban areas of Uttar Pradesh; to examine gender as a moderating variable.

**Method:** Descriptive Survey; KVIR and a structured interview schedule.

**Sample:** 240 students (60 rural boys, 60 rural girls, 60 urban boys, 60 urban girls) from Allahabad district, Uttar Pradesh.

**Findings:** Rural boys showed the highest agricultural and technical interests; urban boys showed the highest scientific and executive interests. Rural girls showed the highest household and social interests; urban girls showed the most diverse profiles with relatively high scientific and commercial interests. The study found that occupational aspirations and actual vocational interests were not always congruent, particularly for rural girls whose aspirations often exceeded the vocational categories they scored highest on — suggesting a disconnect between aspiration and interest measurement. The authors recommended more culturally sensitive vocational assessment tools.

**Relevance to Present Study:** Being from Uttar Pradesh and using the same 2×2 (gender × locality) design, this study is the most directly parallel to the present research. Its findings serve as a key benchmark.

### Study 10: Pandey and Gautam (2021)

**Author(s):** S. Pandey and R. Gautam

**Year:** 2021

**Title:** *Post-Pandemic Shifts in Vocational Interests of Secondary School Adolescents: A Study in Western Uttar Pradesh*

**Objectives:** To assess vocational interests of secondary school students following the COVID-19 pandemic disruption; to examine gender and school-type differences in a post-pandemic context.

**Method:** Survey method; KVIR and a supplementary digital exposure questionnaire.

**Sample:** 180 students (90 boys, 90 girls) from government and private secondary schools in Meerut and Ghaziabad districts.

**Findings:** Technical and commercial interests increased significantly among both boys and girls compared to pre-pandemic baseline data, attributed to increased digital exposure during online learning. Scientific interest rose more sharply among girls than boys in private schools. Agricultural interest declined in rural areas, possibly reflecting disillusionment with farming following pandemic-related economic hardships. The study noted that the pandemic functioned as a disruptive but ultimately broadening influence on the vocational imagination of secondary school students.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This is the most geographically proximate study, being based in western Uttar Pradesh and specifically covering Meerut district. Its post-pandemic findings provide important contextual background for the present study conducted in the 2024-26 session.

## 2.3 Foreign Studies

### Study 11: Holland (1966) — United States

**Author(s):** John L. Holland

**Year:** 1966

**Title:** *The Psychology of Vocational Choice: A Theory of Personality Types and Model Environments*

**Objectives:** To develop a comprehensive theory of vocational choice based on personality types; to demonstrate that vocational interests reflect personality.

**Method:** Theoretical and empirical work involving large-scale interest surveys with American high school and college students.

**Sample:** Multiple samples; primary validation sample included over 12,000 high school students across the United States.

**Findings:** Holland proposed and validated his famous RIASEC typology, demonstrating that both individuals and occupational environments can be meaningfully classified into six types: Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional. He found consistent gender differences, with males scoring higher on Realistic (technical/physical) types and females scoring higher on Social and Conventional types. Holland argued that congruence between personality type and work environment is the key predictor of vocational satisfaction and stability.

**Relevance to Present Study:** Holland's theoretical framework provides the international theoretical foundation against which the Indian patterns observed in the present study can be interpreted. The gender difference patterns he documented have been widely replicated across cultures.

---

### Study 12: Lippa (1998) — United States

**Author(s):** R.A. Lippa

**Year:** 1998

**Title:** *Gender-Related Individual Differences and the Structure of Vocational Interests: The Importance of the People-Things Dimension*

**Objectives:** To investigate the role of gender and gender-related personality traits in determining vocational interests; to examine the people-things dimension of interest differentiation.

**Method:** Quantitative correlational study using the Strong Interest Inventory and personality measures.

**Sample:** 640 undergraduate students (320 male, 320 female) from an American university.

**Findings:** Lippa demonstrated that the single most powerful dimension differentiating male and female vocational interests is the "People-Things" dimension — males preferring thing-oriented occupations (technical, mechanical, scientific) and females preferring people-oriented occupations (social, teaching, healthcare). This dimension accounted for the vast majority of gender differences in occupational interests and was strongly associated with gender-typical personality traits (masculinity-femininity). Lippa argued that while social factors play a role, biological factors related to gender also contribute to this dimension.

**Relevance to Present Study:** Lippa's People-Things dimension provides a theoretically grounded way to interpret the gender differences in vocational interests found in the present study.

---

### Study 13: Su, Rounds, and Armstrong (2009) — United States

**Author(s):** R. Su, J. Rounds, and P.I. Armstrong

**Year:** 2009

**Title:** *Men and Things, Women and People: A Meta-Analysis of Sex Differences in Interests*

**Objectives:** To conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis of gender differences in vocational interests across a large number of studies; to quantify the magnitude and consistency of these differences.

**Method:** Meta-analysis of 503 samples involving over 500,000 participants from multiple countries.

**Findings:** The meta-analysis confirmed large and consistent gender differences in interests, with men showing substantially higher interests in things-oriented fields (particularly Realistic/technical) and women showing somewhat higher interests in people-oriented fields (particularly Social). The effect size for the Realistic domain was d = 0.84 (large), confirming the robustness of male-female differences in technical interest. Importantly, the study found that these differences were largely consistent across cultural contexts, though their magnitude varied somewhat by country and time period. The authors concluded that gender differences in vocational interests are among the most robust and replicable findings in personality psychology.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This meta-analytic evidence provides a powerful international benchmark for interpreting the gender differences in technical and social vocational interests found in the present study.

### Study 14: Watson and McMahon (2005) — South Africa

**Author(s):** M. Watson and M. McMahon

**Year:** 2005

**Title:** *Children's Career Development: A Research Review from a Learning Perspective*

**Objectives:** To review research on career development in children and adolescents from a learning theory perspective; to examine the role of context, gender, and culture.

**Method:** Systematic literature review.

**Sample:** Review of studies from South Africa, Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States.

**Findings:** Watson and McMahon found that gender-stereotyped occupational aspirations emerge very early in childhood and become more entrenched by adolescence. In the South African context, race and socioeconomic status were additional major determinants of vocational aspirations, often overriding individual interests. The study argued for a systemic, socio-contextual approach to career counselling that acknowledges the multiple layers of social identity that shape career thinking. Importantly, they found that exposure to diverse role models was one of the most effective interventions for broadening children's vocational horizons.

**Relevance to Present Study:** The South African context, with its significant rural-urban divide and socioeconomic stratification, provides an interesting comparative reference for the Indian scenario.

---

### Study 15: Tracey and Robbins (2006) — United States

**Author(s):** T.J.G. Tracey and S.B. Robbins

**Year:** 2006

**Title:** *The Interest-Major Congruence and College Success Relation: A Longitudinal Study*

**Objectives:** To examine whether congruence between vocational interests and academic major predicts college success; to test the role of interest crystallisation in academic outcomes.

**Method:** Longitudinal survey study tracking students from secondary school entry to college completion.

**Sample:** 1,183 students followed over five years from entry into a Midwestern US university.

**Findings:** Students with high congruence between their vocational interests and their college major showed higher academic persistence, higher GPAs, and higher satisfaction with their chosen field. Students who entered college without a clearly crystallised vocational interest profile were significantly more likely to change majors or drop out. The study confirmed that interest crystallisation — having a clear and consistent vocational interest profile — is a key developmental task of late adolescence, and that schools play an important role in facilitating this process.

**Relevance to Present Study:** This longitudinal evidence reinforces the theoretical importance of assessing vocational interests at the secondary level, which is the gateway stage before students make stream and college choices.

## 2.4 Critical Review of Literature

A careful examination of the fifteen studies reviewed above reveals several important patterns and themes:

**Consistency of Gender Differences:** Across all fifteen studies — Indian and foreign — there is remarkable consistency in the finding that males show higher technical and scientific vocational interests, while females show higher social, artistic, and household interests. This pattern holds across different cultural contexts, different measurement tools, different sample sizes, and different time periods. It has been documented in the 1960s (Holland, 1966), the 1980s (Thakur and Saini, 1980), and as recently as 2021 (Pandey and Gautam, 2021). The scale of this consistency suggests that gender differences in vocational interests reflect a combination of deep-rooted social conditioning and possibly some degree of biological differentiation.

**Rural-Urban Differences:** The evidence for rural-urban differences in vocational interests is also consistent, though somewhat more variable than gender effects. Rural students, across multiple Indian studies, show higher agricultural and household interests, while urban students show higher scientific, commercial, and executive interests. This pattern reflects the differential socialisation, occupational exposure, and career aspirational environment of rural and urban students.

**Role of School Environment:** Several studies (Gupta and Choudhary, 2016; Kaur and Singh, 2009) highlight the important role of school type and school environment in shaping vocational interests. Private schools appear to provide a more conducive environment for developing diverse and gender-atypical vocational interests, particularly among girls. This has important policy implications for the design of vocational guidance programmes in government schools.

**Importance of Context:** The foreign studies (Watson and McMahon, 2005; Tracey and Robbins, 2006) underline the importance of a contextualised approach to vocational interest assessment and counselling. Simply measuring interests is not sufficient; understanding the social, cultural, and economic contexts that shape those interests is equally important.

**Limitations of Existing Research:** Several methodological limitations were observed in the reviewed studies. Many Indian studies use small, non-representative samples. Most cross-sectional designs cannot establish causality. Few studies examine the interaction effects between gender and school type simultaneously. Most studies use only one measurement tool, limiting the richness of the interest profile they can generate.

## 2.5 Research Gap

Despite the considerable body of research reviewed above, several important gaps remain:

1. **Geographical Gap:** Very few studies have been conducted specifically in the western Uttar Pradesh / Meerut region. The existing literature on vocational interests in UP is largely based on cities like Lucknow, Varanasi, and Allahabad. A study specifically situated in the Meerut district context is needed.

2. **Simultaneous Gender × Locality Analysis:** While many studies examine gender differences OR rural-urban differences, very few conduct a simultaneous 2×2 comparative analysis (rural boys vs. urban boys vs. rural girls vs. urban girls) within a single study. The present research addresses this gap.

3. **Post-NEP 2020 Context:** Most existing Indian studies were conducted before the announcement of the National Education Policy 2020. The new policy's emphasis on vocational education and flexible schooling creates a changed context for studying vocational interests. Research that contextualises findings within NEP 2020 is lacking.

4. **CBSE vs. U.P. Board Comparison:** Most studies compare rural government vs. urban government or private vs. private, but few specifically compare UP Board government school students with CBSE private school students — a comparison that reflects the real bifurcation in India's secondary education system.

5. **Meerut District Specific Data:** Guidance counsellors, teachers, and policymakers in Meerut district need locally relevant data to plan and implement vocational guidance programmes. The present study directly addresses this need.

## 2.6 Summary

The present chapter reviewed fifteen studies — ten Indian and five foreign — on the themes of vocational interests, gender differences, and rural-urban comparisons in educational settings. The review confirmed that gender differences in vocational interests are robust and consistent, with males showing higher technical and scientific interests and females showing higher social, artistic, and household interests. Rural-urban differences in vocational interests also follow consistent patterns, with rural students showing higher agricultural interests and urban students showing higher scientific and commercial interests.

The critical analysis of the literature revealed several research gaps, most notably the absence of district-level studies from western Uttar Pradesh that simultaneously examine gender and school-type differences in vocational interests within the specific CCSU University affiliated college context. The present study has been designed to address these gaps directly. The next chapter describes the research methodology adopted to conduct this comparative investigation.
