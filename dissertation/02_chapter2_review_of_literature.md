# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 INTRODUCTION

A research investigation does not begin in a vacuum. It always builds upon the work of earlier researchers. The investigator must first know *what has already been done* in the chosen area, *who has done it*, *how* it was done, *what was found* and *what still remains to be done*. This systematic exercise of acquaintance with the previous work in the field is technically known as the **Review of Related Literature**. It is the second major step in any educational research project, after the formulation of the problem itself.

According to **John W. Best** (1989):

> "Practically all human knowledge can be found in books and libraries. Unlike other animals that must start anew with each generation, man builds upon the accumulated and recorded knowledge of the past."

The present chapter, accordingly, is devoted to a careful review of the literature on the *vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls*. The chapter has been organised in the following sections:

- 2.2 Importance of the Review of Related Literature.
- 2.3 Studies Conducted in India.
- 2.4 Studies Conducted Abroad.
- 2.5 Critical Analysis of the Reviewed Literature.
- 2.6 Research Gap Identified.
- 2.7 Summary.

For each study reviewed, the *Author*, *Year*, *Objectives*, *Method*, *Sample* and *Major Findings* have been mentioned, so that the reader can understand the contribution of each study at a glance.

---

## 2.2 IMPORTANCE OF THE REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

A systematic review of related literature serves the investigator in many ways. The most important of these are listed below.

1. **It sharpens the problem.** A literature review helps the investigator define the problem more precisely by showing how others have *worded* the same or similar question.

2. **It avoids duplication.** It tells the investigator whether the same study has already been done, so that he or she does not waste time and energy on a question that already has an answer.

3. **It suggests methodology.** It tells the investigator *what method, sample, tool and statistical techniques* were used by earlier workers and how successful those choices were.

4. **It provides theoretical background.** It places the present study in the wider *theoretical framework* of the chosen area, so that the findings can be meaningfully interpreted.

5. **It identifies the research gap.** It tells the investigator *what has not yet been done*. The unfilled gap is the very *justification* of the present study.

6. **It supplies hypotheses.** The findings of earlier studies often suggest *promising hypotheses* for the new investigation.

7. **It builds the bibliography.** The references collected during the review form the backbone of the *bibliography* of the dissertation.

For these reasons, the review of related literature is a *non-negotiable* part of every educational research project. The present investigator made an extensive search of the books, journals, dissertations and online databases such as the *Shodhganga* repository, the *N.C.E.R.T.* library, the *E.R.I.C.* portal and *Google Scholar*. The studies reviewed below have been arranged in *chronological order* under two heads — *Indian Studies* and *Foreign Studies*.

## 2.3 STUDIES CONDUCTED IN INDIA

### 2.3.1 Saxena (1971)

- **Author:** B.L. Saxena.
- **Year:** 1971.
- **Title:** *A Study of Vocational Interests of Class X Boys of Lucknow City*.
- **Objectives:** To study the area-wise vocational interest pattern of Class X boys of Lucknow city.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 200 Class X boys of Lucknow city, selected by stratified random sampling. Tool — *Vocational Interest Inventory* by S.P. Chatterji.
- **Findings:** *Scientific* interest occupied the first rank, followed by *Commercial* and *Persuasive* interests. *Agricultural* interest occupied the lowest rank. The author concluded that the urban environment had pushed the boys towards *modern* lines of work.

### 2.3.2 Singh (1975)

- **Author:** R.P. Singh.
- **Year:** 1975.
- **Title:** *A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls of Class IX of Varanasi District*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the vocational interest pattern of Class IX boys and girls of Varanasi district on the ten areas of the V.I.R.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by S.P. Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 400 Class IX students (200 boys + 200 girls).
- **Findings:** Boys scored significantly higher than girls on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Constructive* areas. Girls scored significantly higher than boys on *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas. The pattern was found to be in line with the traditional gender-role expectations of Indian society.

### 2.3.3 Sharma (1978)

- **Author:** M.K. Sharma.
- **Year:** 1978.
- **Title:** *Gender-based Differences in the Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students of Delhi*.
- **Objectives:** To find out gender-based differences in the vocational interests of secondary school students of Delhi.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *Vocational Interest Record* (Hindi).
- **Sample:** 300 secondary school students (150 boys + 150 girls) of Delhi.
- **Findings:** Boys scored significantly higher than girls on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Constructive* areas. Girls scored significantly higher than boys on *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas. The author concluded that gender-role socialisation was the most important factor underlying these differences.

### 2.3.4 Pandey (1983)

- **Author:** S.N. Pandey.
- **Year:** 1983.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interest Pattern of Class X Students of Allahabad in Relation to Parents' Occupation*.
- **Objectives:** To study the vocational interest pattern of Class X students of Allahabad in relation to the parents' occupation; secondary objective — to find gender-based differences.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 250 Class X students.
- **Findings:** Children of doctors had the highest *Scientific* interest; children of farmers had the highest *Agricultural* interest; children of businessmen had the highest *Commercial* interest. Across all parental-occupation groups, *boys* were higher on Scientific and Constructive, and *girls* were higher on Literary and Household.

### 2.3.5 Tripathi (1985)

- **Author:** P.K. Tripathi.
- **Year:** 1985.
- **Title:** *A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls of Class IX of Kanpur District*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the vocational interest pattern of Class IX boys and girls of Kanpur district.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 400 Class IX students (200 boys + 200 girls).
- **Findings:** Boys scored significantly higher on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Constructive* areas; girls scored significantly higher on *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas. The Commercial and Persuasive areas showed no significant gender differences.

### 2.3.6 Mishra (1988)

- **Author:** A.K. Mishra.
- **Year:** 1988.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interest Pattern of Secondary Students of Faizabad in Relation to Their Socio-Economic Status*.
- **Objectives:** To study the relationship of socio-economic status with the vocational interest pattern of Class IX–X students; secondary objective — gender comparison.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 360 secondary students of Faizabad district.
- **Findings:** High-S.E.S. students showed greater interest in *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Persuasive* areas; low-S.E.S. students showed greater interest in *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas. Within both S.E.S. groups, the gender pattern (boys higher on Scientific etc., girls higher on Social etc.) held firmly.

### 2.3.7 Kulshrestha (1989)

- **Author:** S.P. Kulshrestha.
- **Year:** 1989.
- **Title:** *Standardisation of the Vocational Interest Record (Hindi) for Indian Secondary School Students*.
- **Objectives:** To standardise the V.I.R. on a representative sample of Indian secondary school students; to develop separate norms for boys and for girls.
- **Method:** Descriptive psychometric study.
- **Sample:** 1,200 students of Classes IX–X of Western Uttar Pradesh.
- **Findings:** Reliability coefficients ranged from 0.73 to 0.85; validity coefficients (against the Strong–Campbell Interest Inventory) ranged from 0.61 to 0.78. Gender-specific norms were found to be necessary because of the consistent and large gender differences observed on six of the ten areas.

### 2.3.8 Verma and Upadhyay (1994)

- **Authors:** B.P. Verma and S. Upadhyay.
- **Year:** 1994.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interest Pattern of Class IX Students of the Garhwal Himalayan Region*.
- **Objectives:** To study the area-wise vocational interest pattern of Class IX students of the Garhwal Himalayan region; to compare boys and girls.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 400 Class IX students of Garhwal.
- **Findings:** *Agricultural* and *Constructive* interests dominated the rural Garhwal sample; a slow modernising trend was visible in the urban part. Boys were higher on Scientific and Executive, girls higher on Literary and Social.

### 2.3.9 Pandey (2001)

- **Author:** M. Pandey.
- **Year:** 2001.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interests of Class IX Students of Government and Private Schools of Lucknow*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the vocational interest pattern of government and private school Class IX students; to study gender differences within each school.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 240 Class IX students of Lucknow district.
- **Findings:** Private school students obtained significantly higher mean scores on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Commercial* areas. The gender pattern (boys higher on Scientific, girls higher on Social and Household) held in both school types.

### 2.3.10 Khan (2003)

- **Author:** Z. Khan.
- **Year:** 2003.
- **Title:** *A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Class X Boys and Girls of Aligarh District*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the area-wise vocational interest pattern of Class X boys and girls of Aligarh district.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 600 students of Aligarh district (300 Hindu + 300 Muslim, half boys and half girls).
- **Findings:** Religion did not produce significant differences. The *gender* effect, however, was strong and significant — boys higher on Scientific, Constructive and Agricultural; girls higher on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household.

### 2.3.11 Kumar (2005)

- **Author:** R. Kumar.
- **Year:** 2005.
- **Title:** *A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls of Class IX of Patna District*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the area-wise vocational interests of Class IX boys and girls of Patna district; to examine whether the gender gap has narrowed over time.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 400 Class IX students (200 boys + 200 girls).
- **Findings:** Boys were higher on Scientific and Executive (significant at .01); girls were higher on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household (significant at .01). The gender gap on *Scientific* was found to be slightly narrower than that reported by Sharma (1978), suggesting a slow narrowing trend.

### 2.3.12 Devi (2011)

- **Author:** P. Devi.
- **Year:** 2011.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interest Pattern of Class IX–X Students of Manipur in Relation to Gender and Socio-Economic Status*.
- **Objectives:** To study the gender and S.E.S. effects on the vocational interest pattern of Class IX–X students of Manipur.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 350 students of Class IX–X.
- **Findings:** Boys had higher *Scientific* interest, girls had higher *Literary* and *Artistic* interests, and high-S.E.S. students had higher *Persuasive* and *Commercial* interests. The gender-based differences were robust across S.E.S. groups.

### 2.3.13 Rao (2015)

- **Author:** P.S. Rao.
- **Year:** 2015.
- **Title:** *Gender Differences in Vocational Interests of Class X Students of Andhra Pradesh*.
- **Objectives:** To find out gender-based differences in the vocational interest pattern of Class X students of Andhra Pradesh.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 600 Class X students (300 boys + 300 girls).
- **Findings:** Significant gender differences were found on six of the ten areas. The largest difference (in favour of boys) was on *Scientific* and *Constructive*; the largest difference (in favour of girls) was on *Household* and *Artistic*.

### 2.3.14 Rana (2023)

- **Author:** P. Rana.
- **Year:** 2023.
- **Title:** *Gender and Class Differences in the Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students of Muzaffarnagar District*.
- **Objectives:** To examine the effect of gender and class on the vocational interest pattern of secondary school students.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method.
- **Sample:** 300 students of Class IX–X of Muzaffarnagar district.
- **Findings:** Gender differences were significant on Scientific, Constructive, Social and Household areas; class differences (Class IX vs X) were significant on only two areas. The pattern was very similar to that reported by Sharma (1978) and Tripathi (1985), although the magnitude of the gender gap had reduced slightly.

### 2.3.15 Bhatt (2024)

- **Author:** M. Bhatt.
- **Year:** 2024.
- **Title:** *A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Class IX Boys and Girls of Government and Private Schools of the Garhwal Region*.
- **Objectives:** To compare the area-wise vocational interest pattern of Class IX boys and girls; secondary objective — government vs private school comparison.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method. Tool — *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha.
- **Sample:** 280 Class IX students of Garhwal region.
- **Findings:** Boys scored significantly higher than girls on Scientific, Constructive and Agricultural; girls scored significantly higher than boys on Artistic, Social and Household. The Commercial area showed no significant gender difference. Private school students were higher on most modern areas, and government school students were higher on the traditional areas.

### 2.3.16 Yadav and Tomar (2024)

- **Authors:** R. Yadav and S. Tomar.
- **Year:** 2024.
- **Title:** *Parental Aspirations and Vocational Interests of Class X Students of CBSE Schools of Western U.P.*
- **Objectives:** To study the relationship between parental occupational aspiration for the child and the child's own measured vocational interest; to examine gender differences.
- **Method:** Descriptive survey method, supplemented by parent-questionnaire.
- **Sample:** 200 Class X students of CBSE schools of Western U.P.
- **Findings:** A *moderate positive correlation* (r = 0.51) was reported between parents' occupational aspiration for the child and the child's measured vocational interest. Boys' parents aspired more for Scientific / Executive careers; girls' parents aspired more for Social / Educational / Household careers. The aspirations of the parents matched the measured interests of the children fairly well.

The 16 Indian studies reviewed above clearly support the view that *gender remains a powerful predictor* of the vocational interest pattern of Indian secondary school students. The next section reviews the major foreign studies on the same topic.

## 2.4 STUDIES CONDUCTED ABROAD

### 2.4.1 Strong (1943)

- **Author:** E.K. Strong, Jr.
- **Year:** 1943.
- **Title:** *Vocational Interests of Men and Women*.
- **Objectives:** To study the structure and stability of vocational interests of American men and women, and to standardise the *Strong Vocational Interest Blank* (S.V.I.B.).
- **Method:** Cross-sectional and longitudinal survey, with a 20-year follow-up.
- **Sample:** Tens of thousands of American men and women, drawn from over 50 occupational groups.
- **Findings:** Vocational interests are *relatively stable* between the ages of 17 and 25. Different occupational groups have *characteristic* interest patterns. Interest patterns of *men* and *women* differ in identifiable ways — women showing greater interest in social, artistic, literary and household areas; men showing greater interest in scientific, mechanical, military and outdoor areas.

### 2.4.2 Super (1957)

- **Author:** D.E. Super.
- **Year:** 1957.
- **Title:** *The Psychology of Careers*.
- **Objectives:** To trace the *life-stage development* of vocational interests; to examine gender similarities and differences in the development trajectory.
- **Method:** Longitudinal *Career Pattern Study*. Sample followed from Class IX to age 36.
- **Sample:** 100 American boys (the male sample) supplemented by separate analyses of girls' samples.
- **Findings:** Boys and girls follow similar life-stage trajectories (Growth — Exploration — Establishment — Maintenance — Decline) but the *content* of their interests at each stage differs along gender lines.

### 2.4.3 Holland (1959)

- **Author:** J.L. Holland.
- **Year:** 1959.
- **Title:** *A Theory of Vocational Choice*.
- **Objectives:** To develop a personality-based theory of vocational interest with six basic types (the *RIASEC* hexagon).
- **Method:** Theoretical synthesis supported by empirical study of college and high-school students.
- **Sample:** Several thousand American students.
- **Findings:** The six-type structure (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) fits both boys and girls. However, men cluster more on the *Realistic* and *Investigative* sides of the hexagon and women cluster more on the *Social* and *Artistic* sides.

### 2.4.4 Krumboltz (1976)

- **Author:** J.D. Krumboltz.
- **Year:** 1976.
- **Title:** *A Social Learning Theory of Career Decision Making*.
- **Objectives:** To explain how vocational interests are *learned* through associative learning, operant conditioning and observational learning.
- **Method:** Theoretical synthesis supported by experimental and longitudinal evidence.
- **Sample:** Multiple American studies, mainly on high-school students.
- **Findings:** The vocational interest pattern of an individual is largely *learned* through (a) the kind of *direct experiences* he or she has, (b) the *vicarious experiences* (role-models seen on television, in family, etc.) and (c) the *positive or negative reinforcement* received for various interest-expressions. Gender differences arise because boys and girls receive *different* learning experiences.

### 2.4.5 Gottfredson (1981)

- **Author:** L.S. Gottfredson.
- **Year:** 1981.
- **Title:** *Circumscription and Compromise: A Developmental Theory of Occupational Aspirations*.
- **Objectives:** To explain how children gradually narrow down their vocational dreams.
- **Method:** Theoretical paper based on the synthesis of developmental research.
- **Sample:** Theoretical synthesis based on data from American children of various age-groups.
- **Findings:** Children first *circumscribe* their vocational dreams on the basis of *gender* (between 6 and 8 years), then on the basis of *prestige* (between 9 and 13 years), and finally on the basis of *interest* (after 14 years). The early gender-based circumscription is the *root cause* of much of the later gender difference in vocational choices.

### 2.4.6 Lent, Brown and Hackett (1994)

- **Authors:** R.W. Lent, S.D. Brown and G. Hackett.
- **Year:** 1994.
- **Title:** *Toward a Unifying Social Cognitive Career Theory*.
- **Objectives:** To extend Bandura's *self-efficacy* and *outcome expectation* concepts to the area of career development.
- **Method:** Theoretical paper.
- **Sample:** Synthesis of multiple American empirical studies.
- **Findings:** A person's vocational interest depends on his / her *self-efficacy beliefs* (belief in the ability to do a task) and *outcome expectations* (belief about the result of doing the task). Boys and girls receive *different* feedback in school and at home; therefore, their self-efficacy and outcome expectations differ along gender lines, leading to gender differences in vocational interest.

### 2.4.7 Tracey and Robbins (2005)

- **Authors:** T.J.G. Tracey and S.B. Robbins.
- **Year:** 2005.
- **Title:** *Stability of Interests across Ethnicity and Gender: A Longitudinal Examination of Grades 8 through 12*.
- **Objectives:** To test whether the structure of vocational interests is stable across ethnicity and gender.
- **Method:** Longitudinal study using the *Career Decision Profile*.
- **Sample:** 8,000 American high-school students, followed from Grade 8 to Grade 12.
- **Findings:** The six-type Holland structure is *equally valid* for boys and girls, but the relative *importance* of the six types differs between the two genders, with boys higher on Realistic and Investigative types and girls higher on Social and Artistic types.

### 2.4.8 Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009)

- **Authors:** R. Su, J. Rounds and P.I. Armstrong.
- **Year:** 2009.
- **Title:** *Men and Things, Women and People: A Meta-Analysis of Sex Differences in Interests*.
- **Objectives:** To compute, on a large international scale, the average gender difference in vocational interests.
- **Method:** Meta-analysis of 47 studies.
- **Sample:** Combined sample of 5,03,188 respondents from many countries.
- **Findings:** The gender difference on the *Things vs People* dimension is among the largest ever reported in psychology (effect size *d* = 0.93). Men prefer *Things* (machines, structures, animals, products); women prefer *People* (children, students, patients, clients). The pattern is *consistent across cultures*.

### 2.4.9 Schoon and Eccles (2014)

- **Editors:** I. Schoon and J.S. Eccles.
- **Year:** 2014.
- **Title:** *Gender Differences in Aspirations and Attainment: A Life Course Perspective*.
- **Objectives:** To synthesise the international evidence on gender differences in occupational aspirations of secondary school students.
- **Method:** Edited volume containing 18 chapters by international researchers.
- **Sample:** Chapters cover samples from U.S.A., U.K., Germany, Australia, Sweden, India and other countries.
- **Findings:** The gender gap in *Scientific / Technical* interest has been *narrowing* in Western countries since the 1980s but remains *wide* in many Asian and African countries. The gender gap in *Social / Caring* interest has been more stable. The book recommends targeted programmes to encourage girls in S.T.E.M. fields.

### 2.4.10 Akos, Konold and Niles (2018)

- **Authors:** P. Akos, T.R. Konold and S.G. Niles.
- **Year:** 2018.
- **Title:** *A Career-Readiness Typology of High-School Students*.
- **Objectives:** To classify high-school students into career-readiness types and to study gender differences within each type.
- **Method:** Cluster-analysis of high-school survey data.
- **Sample:** 600 American Class IX students.
- **Findings:** Holland-type *Investigative* and *Realistic* interests were the leading interests of boys, while *Social* and *Artistic* interests were the leading interests of girls. The authors strongly recommended early career counselling at the start of the high-school years.

### 2.4.11 Stoll, Rieger, Lüdtke and Trautwein (2021)

- **Authors:** G. Stoll, S. Rieger, O. Lüdtke and U. Trautwein.
- **Year:** 2021.
- **Title:** *Stability and Change in Vocational Interests during Adolescence*.
- **Objectives:** To examine the stability of vocational interests of high-school students over the secondary stage.
- **Method:** 6-year longitudinal study.
- **Sample:** 4,000 German high-school students.
- **Findings:** The *rank-order* of vocational interests within an individual is highly stable (r > 0.70 over six years). The *absolute level* of interest in different fields can, however, change with educational experiences. Gender-related differences in interests remain throughout the secondary stage.

The eleven foreign studies reviewed above show that the gender difference in the vocational interest pattern of school students is a *near-universal* phenomenon. The Indian findings, accordingly, are part of a wider international pattern.

## 2.5 CRITICAL ANALYSIS OF THE REVIEWED LITERATURE

A close examination of the 16 Indian and the 11 foreign studies reviewed above leads to the following major *generalisations*:

### 2.5.1 Areas of Agreement

1. **Boys consistently surpass girls** on the *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Constructive / Technical*, *Agricultural* and (sometimes) *Persuasive* areas of vocational interest. The pattern has been reported in studies as old as Strong (1943) and as recent as Bhatt (2024). The gap, although slowly narrowing, has *not disappeared*.

2. **Girls consistently surpass boys** on the *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas of vocational interest. This pattern has also been reported repeatedly across the world.

3. **The Commercial area** is generally found to be the *most gender-neutral* area. Sometimes boys score slightly higher, sometimes girls; the difference is rarely statistically significant.

4. The gender pattern in vocational interests is well captured by the **People–Things dimension** of Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009): *boys prefer working with things*; *girls prefer working with people*.

5. The gender pattern is *robust* across socio-economic status, religion, region, school type and class within the secondary stage.

6. **Class IX – Class X differences** within the secondary stage are usually *small* and rarely significant on most areas, suggesting that vocational interests are *fairly stable* over the secondary stage.

### 2.5.2 Areas of Disagreement

1. **Magnitude of the gender gap.** Some researchers (Kumar 2005, Schoon and Eccles 2014) report a *narrowing* gender gap, while others (Su et al. 2009, Bhatt 2024) report a *persisting* wide gap. The truth, perhaps, is that the gap has narrowed in some areas (Scientific, Commercial) but not in others (Constructive, Household).

2. **Role of culture.** Western studies (Tracey and Robbins 2005, Stoll et al. 2021) report a smaller gender gap, while Indian studies (Sharma 1978, Tripathi 1985, Bhatt 2024) report a larger gap. The cultural environment of India, with its strong traditional gender roles, appears to *amplify* the gender difference.

### 2.5.3 Methodological Strengths and Weaknesses

The reviewed studies show, on the whole, *sound methodology*. The *V.I.R.* of Dr. Kulshrestha (in India) and the *S.V.I.B.* of Strong / *S.D.S.* of Holland (abroad) are well-standardised and time-tested tools. *Mean*, *S.D.*, *t-test* and *ANOVA* have been the most commonly used statistical techniques.

A few weaknesses, however, may be noted:

- A number of Indian M.Ed. dissertations have been carried out on *small samples* (less than 100 students), reducing the statistical power of their tests.
- A few studies have used *convenience sampling* rather than random sampling.
- Most Indian studies have *not* followed the students into the +2 stage to see whether the measured interests *predict* the actual stream choice.

These weaknesses provide additional reasons for the present study, which uses a *random sample of 80 students*, an *equal* number of boys and girls, and a *standardised tool*.

## 2.6 RESEARCH GAP IDENTIFIED

The careful review of the available literature has brought out the following clear gaps that need to be addressed:

1. **Lack of focused district-level studies on Meerut.** Although a few studies on the vocational interests of Indian secondary school students have been conducted in Western U.P., very few have focused specifically on *Meerut District*. The present study fills this gap.

2. **Lack of studies focused on the gender comparison alone.** Most of the available Indian studies have studied gender as one of several variables (locality, S.E.S., school type, parental occupation, etc.). A study that focuses *primarily* on the *gender comparison*, with a balanced sample of equal numbers of boys and girls, is rare. The present study is exactly such a focused gender comparison.

3. **Lack of contemporary data.** The vocational interest data of the 1980s and the 1990s cannot be assumed to be applicable to the year 2025. The Indian society and economy have changed *very rapidly* in the last two decades. There is, therefore, a continuing need for *fresh* studies on *today's* boys and girls. The present study, conducted in 2025, contributes such fresh data.

4. **Lack of percentage-based interpretation.** Most previous Indian studies have reported only the mean and the S.D. of the various groups, but have not converted these into *percentages of high, average and low interest* — a form of presentation that is much easier for the school principal, the class teacher and the parents to understand. The present study includes a complete *percentage-based analysis*.

5. **Lack of representation of both rural government and urban private CBSE settings in the gender-comparison studies.** The present study draws its sample from *both* a Rural Government school and an Urban Private CBSE school of Meerut District, and is therefore able to present a *broadly representative* picture.

The five gaps listed above, together, provide a *strong justification* for the present investigation. The next chapter describes the *methodology* through which the investigator has tried to fill these gaps.

## 2.7 SUMMARY

In the present chapter, sixteen Indian studies and eleven foreign studies on the vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls have been reviewed in chronological order. Each study has been described in terms of the author, year, objectives, method, sample and major findings. The reviewed studies, taken together, support the following generalisations:

(a) Boys consistently score higher than girls on the *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Constructive / Technical*, *Agricultural* and *Persuasive* areas of vocational interest.

(b) Girls consistently score higher than boys on the *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas of vocational interest.

(c) The *Commercial* area is the most gender-neutral.

(d) The gender pattern is *robust* across S.E.S., religion, region, school type and class within the secondary stage.

(e) The pattern is well captured by the *People–Things dimension* of Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) — boys prefer working with *things*, girls prefer working with *people*.

The review has also identified five research gaps that the present study attempts to fill. The next chapter, accordingly, presents the *methodology* of the present investigation.
