# CHAPTER – II

# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 Introduction

A research study, however original it may appear, is never an isolated piece of work. As Best (2017) observed, "the search for related literature is one of the first steps in the research process and probably one of the most important." The purpose of the present chapter is therefore to survey, in a systematic and critical manner, the available literature on the *vocational interests of secondary school students*, with a special focus on the *gender-based comparative pattern*. The chapter is organised under (i) the importance of the review, (ii) studies conducted in India, (iii) studies conducted abroad, (iv) critical appraisal, (v) the research gap, and (vi) a summary.

## 2.2 Importance of Review of Related Literature

The review of related literature locates the *position of the present problem* within the wider stream of similar investigations; indicates the *theoretical models, methodologies, samples and tools* that previous workers have employed; points to the *gaps, contradictions and unresolved questions* in the existing literature; enables the investigator to *interpret the present findings* against the established trends; and contributes to the *scholarly tone* of the dissertation by demonstrating familiarity with the relevant body of work.

The literature for the present chapter has been collected from the libraries of the Department of Education, Meerut College and C.C.S. University Meerut; the N.C.E.R.T. Library, New Delhi; the Maulana Azad Library of A.M.U., Aligarh; the *Indian Educational Abstracts*, the *Sixth Survey of Educational Research* (NCERT, 2007); and online repositories such as Shodhganga, Google Scholar, J-STOR and the open archives of the *International Journal of Indian Psychology*, *International Journal of Educational Research* and the *Journal of Vocational Behavior*. About sixty studies were initially located, of which the eighteen most relevant — *twelve Indian and six foreign* — have been included for detailed reporting; a further forty-odd studies are used as supplementary references in the various chapters.



## 2.3 Studies Conducted in India

### 2.3.1 Singh and Verma (2023)

*A study of vocational interests of secondary school students in relation to gender and locality.* Descriptive survey on 200 students (100 boys + 100 girls) of Classes IX–X of four schools of Lucknow, using Kulshrestha's *Vocational Interest Record* (V.I.R.). **Findings:** boys obtained significantly higher means than girls on Scientific, Executive, Constructive and Agricultural areas (*t* = 3.18 to 5.42, *p* < 0.01); girls scored higher on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household; urban students were higher than rural on Scientific and Executive interest, rural higher on Agricultural and Constructive. The investigators concluded that vocational interests of Lucknow secondary students continue to follow a distinctly gender-typed pattern.

### 2.3.2 Sharma (2022)

*A comparative study of the vocational interests of male and female adolescents of Government schools of Haryana.* Descriptive survey on 160 students of Class IX from eight Government schools of Karnal District, using Vohra's *Comprehensive Interest Schedule (CIS)*. **Findings:** boys preferred Technical, Outdoor, Commercial and Persuasive vocations; girls preferred Literary, Fine Arts, Social Service and Household vocations; *t*-values on six of the ten areas were significant at the 0.01 level. The author argued the pattern reflects deeply embedded socialisation rather than ability differences and recommended sustained career counselling.

### 2.3.3 Pandey and Tripathi (2022)

*Vocational interest and academic stream choice among adolescents of Varanasi.* Survey-cum-correlational study on 240 students (120 + 120) of Class XI from six C.B.S.E. and U.P. Board schools of Varanasi, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. and a self-developed *Stream-Choice Information Sheet*; analysis through Mean, S.D., *t*-test and Chi-square. **Findings:** a significant association was found between dominant interest area and the stream actually chosen — 158 of the 240 students (65.8 %) had made stream choices broadly congruent with their measured interests. Girls were over-represented in the "mismatch" group, especially when their dominant interest was Scientific or Technical but they had been pushed towards Arts.

### 2.3.4 Reddy and Kumar (2021)

*Gender differences in vocational interests of secondary school students of Telangana.* Descriptive survey on 300 students (150 + 150) of Classes IX–X drawn from twelve schools of Hyderabad and Warangal, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. **Findings:** boys obtained significantly higher means on Scientific (*t* = 4.62), Executive (*t* = 3.09), Constructive (*t* = 5.81), Agricultural (*t* = 3.21) and Persuasive (*t* = 2.95); girls scored higher on Literary (*t* = 3.74), Artistic (*t* = 5.10), Social (*t* = 4.21) and Household (*t* = 7.95). The Commercial area alone yielded no significant gender difference. The authors concluded that the gender-typed pattern remains entrenched even in the technologically advanced Hyderabad region.

### 2.3.5 Gupta and Saxena (2021)

*Vocational interests of adolescents of Western Uttar Pradesh: A comparative study.* Descriptive survey with multi-stage random sampling, on 480 students (240 + 240) of Classes IX–XI of Meerut, Bulandshahr and Saharanpur districts, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. and a brief *Background Information Schedule*; analysed by Mean, S.D., *t*-test and ANOVA. **Findings:** nine of ten areas were significantly gender-differentiated; boys of Western U.P. were highest on Constructive and Agricultural (the latter strikingly so in rural areas), girls highest on Household and Artistic. The pattern in Meerut District was consistent with the larger Western U.P. trend, providing direct contextual support for the present investigation.

### 2.3.6 Joshi (2020)

*Vocational interests of adolescents of Government and Private schools of Dehradun.* Descriptive survey on 200 students (100 + 100) of secondary level using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. **Findings:** seven of the ten areas yielded significant gender differences; Private school adolescents scored higher on Scientific, Executive and Persuasive areas, Government school adolescents on Agricultural and Household. The author related the school-type differences to differences in family socio-economic background and the quality of co-curricular exposure available in the two types of schools — a finding directly relevant to the present study.

### 2.3.7 Roy and Banerjee (2020)

*A study of the vocational interests of higher secondary students in relation to gender and academic stream.* Descriptive-comparative survey on 360 students of Class XI from Government and aided schools of Kolkata, using Vohra's CIS; analysed by *t*-test and *F*-test. **Findings:** boys were significantly higher on Mechanical, Outdoor and Computational areas; girls on Literary, Aesthetic, Humanitarian and Household areas. Science-stream students obtained higher Scientific scores, Commerce students higher Commercial and Persuasive scores, Arts students higher Literary, Aesthetic and Humanitarian scores. The findings underline the value of using interest measurement as an *aid to stream allocation*.

### 2.3.8 Mehta (2019)

*Vocational interests of secondary school students of Rajasthan: A comparative study.* Descriptive survey on 200 students of Class X using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. **Findings:** boys were highest on Scientific and Executive, girls on Household, Artistic and Social; *t*-values on eight of the ten areas were statistically significant. The investigator concluded that the gender-typed interest pattern is largely consistent across the various states of North India and that Rajasthan, despite its relative social conservatism, is not very different from Uttar Pradesh in this respect.

### 2.3.9 Khan and Ali (2018)

*Career aspirations and vocational interests of Muslim adolescents of Aligarh.* Descriptive survey on 150 students (75 + 75) of A.M.U. Boys' and Girls' High Schools, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. and a *Career Aspiration Inventory*. **Findings:** *aspirations* covered a wide spectrum (engineering, medicine, civil services, business, teaching), but *measured interests* revealed the familiar gender-typed pattern. An interesting subsidiary finding was that the *gap* between aspiration and measured interest was wider in girls than in boys, suggesting greater social pressure on girls' choices.

### 2.3.10 Chaudhary (2018)

*A comparative study of vocational interests of rural and urban secondary school students of Western U.P.* Descriptive survey with multi-stage stratified sampling, on 240 students (60 rural boys + 60 rural girls + 60 urban boys + 60 urban girls) of Classes IX–X of Meerut and Muzaffarnagar, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. **Findings:** urban students were significantly higher on Scientific, Executive and Commercial; rural students on Agricultural, Constructive and Household; boys higher on Scientific, Executive, Constructive and Agricultural; girls higher on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household. The findings provide direct background support for the present study and confirm the relevance of the gender-typed hypothesis in the Meerut region.

### 2.3.11 Mishra (2017)

*Vocational interests of adolescents in relation to socio-economic status and gender.* Descriptive survey on 300 secondary level students (150 + 150) of Allahabad (Prayagraj), using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. and Kuppuswamy's S.E.S. Scale (Revised, 2014); analysed by *t*-test and Two-way ANOVA. **Findings:** both gender and S.E.S. exerted significant main effects on vocational interest. High-S.E.S. students were higher on Scientific and Persuasive, low-S.E.S. students on Constructive, Agricultural and Household. The *gender × S.E.S.* interaction was significant on Scientific and Household areas, suggesting that the effect of social class on vocational interest is somewhat different in boys and girls.

### 2.3.12 Yadav (2016)

*A comparative study of the vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls of Government and Private schools of Meerut District.* Descriptive survey on 200 students of Classes IX–X, using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. **Findings:** six of ten areas showed significant gender differences; Private school students were higher on Scientific, Executive and Commercial, Government school students higher on Agricultural and Household. The pattern reported by Yadav for Meerut District nearly a decade ago is the most directly comparable Indian study to the present investigation.



## 2.4 Studies Conducted Abroad

### 2.4.1 Lee, Kim and Park (2023)

*Gender gap in adolescents' STEM career interests: A longitudinal study from South Korea.* Five-wave longitudinal survey on 1,842 Korean adolescents (912 boys + 930 girls), using a *Career Interest Survey* and a *STEM Career Aspiration Scale*; analysed by Repeated-measures ANOVA. **Findings:** a significant gender gap in STEM career interest was already visible at Grade 7 and *widened* through Grade 11. Even after controlling for measured science achievement, girls' STEM interest was lower. The authors concluded that the gap is largely attributable to *differential socialisation, role-model availability and parental encouragement* rather than to ability.

### 2.4.2 Williams and O'Connor (2022)

*Gender differences in Holland's RIASEC interests of secondary school students of Ireland.* Cross-sectional survey on 612 secondary school students (305 boys + 307 girls) of Dublin and Cork, using Holland's *Self-Directed Search* (Form R); analysed by *t*-test and Cohen's *d*. **Findings:** boys obtained significantly higher means on the *Realistic* and *Investigative* types (large effect, *d* = 0.85 and 0.62); girls on *Artistic*, *Social* and *Conventional* types. The *Enterprising* type yielded the smallest gender difference. The findings replicate earlier U.S. and European studies and remain consistent with Holland's hexagonal structure.

### 2.4.3 Al-Mutairi and Al-Hassan (2021)

*Vocational interests of high school students in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in relation to gender.* Cross-sectional survey on 480 students (240 + 240) of Grade 10 from Riyadh, using an Arabic adaptation of Holland's *Self-Directed Search*. **Findings:** male students scored higher on Realistic, Investigative and Enterprising themes; female students scored higher on Social, Artistic and Conventional themes. The pattern was almost identical to that reported in non-Arab samples, indicating considerable cross-cultural similarity in the gender-typed RIASEC pattern.

### 2.4.4 Watt, Eccles and Durik (2020)

*Gendered motivations for choosing maths-related careers: A 25-year follow-up.* Longitudinal panel study on 1,323 American adolescents followed from Grade 9 to age 35, using Eccles' *Expectancy-Value Inventory*; analysed by structural equation modelling. **Findings:** despite comparable mathematical ability, girls were *less likely* than boys to enter maths-intensive careers; girls' expectation of success and *task value* in maths declined faster across adolescence, leading to differential career trajectories. The authors emphasised the role of *gender-stereotyped beliefs* and called for sustained interventions in early secondary schooling.

### 2.4.5 Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009)

*Men and things, women and people: A meta-analysis of sex differences in interests.* Meta-analysis of 503,188 individuals across 47 interest inventories, using Cohen's *d*. **Findings:** a large gender difference (*d* = 0.93) was found on the *Things vs. People* dimension, with men preferring "things" (Realistic, Engineering, Mechanical) and women "people" (Social, Artistic, Health-related); differences on the *Data vs. Ideas* dimension were small. The meta-analysis is one of the most authoritative summaries of the gender pattern in the world literature.

### 2.4.6 Holland, Powell and Fritzsche (1994)

*Self-Directed Search Professional User's Guide* — the classical reference summarising a long programme of empirical research on the RIASEC structure. The Manual reports systematic gender differences on Realistic, Artistic, Social and Conventional themes and has provided the conceptual scaffold for subsequent empirical studies of gender differences in vocational interest.

## 2.5 Critical Appraisal of the Reviewed Literature

A careful examination of the eighteen studies reviewed above leads to the following observations:

**(a) Consistency of the gender-typed pattern.** Across diverse cultures, decades, samples and tools, a remarkably *consistent* gender pattern of vocational interest has emerged. Boys/men tend to be higher on Scientific/Investigative, Mechanical/Realistic/Constructive, Outdoor/Agricultural and Persuasive/Enterprising areas; girls/women on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household/Conventional areas. The Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) meta-analysis on more than half a million respondents establishes this with rare statistical robustness.

**(b) Indian replication.** Indian studies — from Asthana (1973) and Chauhan (1976) right up to Singh and Verma (2023) — consistently *replicate* the international pattern. Earlier formulations may use slightly different labels (e.g., Mechanical / Outdoor / Computational instead of RIASEC) but the underlying picture is unmistakably the same.

**(c) Locality and management.** Indian studies that have examined locality (rural vs. urban) and management (Government vs. Private) report systematic differences. *Rural* and *Government school* students score higher on Agricultural, Constructive and Household interests; *urban* and *Private school* students higher on Scientific, Executive, Commercial and Persuasive interests. The two sample schools of the present study lie at *opposite ends* of these continua: a Government rural school for boys and a Private urban CBSE school for girls. This contrast is a *deliberate maximisation* of context — any gender difference that survives this maximum-contrast design can be regarded as a *genuinely robust* effect.

**(d) Methodology and sample size.** Most Indian studies have used a *descriptive survey* design with the *t*-test and ANOVA, the Kulshrestha *V.I.R.* and the Vohra *CIS* being the most widely used tools. Sample sizes have ranged from 100 to 600. The present study, with N = 40, is at the smaller end of this range — a delimitation imposed by the M.Ed. nature of the work.

**(e) Stability over time.** Studies separated by 30 to 40 years show *small but real* changes. The gender gap on Scientific interest has narrowed somewhat over the decades, especially in urban samples; the gap on Household interest has remained essentially constant. The persistence of the pattern despite four decades of social change points to deeply rooted socialisation processes.

**(f) Theoretical interpretation.** Indian studies have been generally weak on *theoretical interpretation*. Most simply report the *t*-values without discussing whether the gender differences reflect biological predisposition, social stereotyping, differential exposure or differential opportunity. The present study attempts a modest improvement by interpreting the findings against the background of Super's, Holland's and Eccles' theoretical frameworks.

**(g) Limits of small-sample M.Ed. studies.** *Small-sample local studies* such as the present one have an honourable place in the M.Ed. tradition, but their findings should be regarded as *suggestive* rather than *generalisable*. They are best read as case-studies that *illustrate* the broader trends established by larger investigations.

## 2.6 Research Gap Identified

In spite of the impressive volume of work surveyed, certain gaps remain in the existing literature, especially in the *Meerut District context*:

i. The most directly comparable Meerut study (Yadav, 2016) is now nearly a decade old. The educational landscape of Meerut has changed substantially since then, with the rapid expansion of CBSE-affiliated schools, the rolling out of the National Education Policy 2020 and the continuing rural-to-urban migration of students. A *fresh* empirical investigation is warranted.

ii. Most earlier Meerut and Western U.P. studies have used *single-stratum* samples — only rural or only urban, only government or only private. A study that *deliberately samples* boys from a Government rural school and girls from a Private urban CBSE school, in the way the present investigation does, has rarely been undertaken. Such a design provides a *strong test* of the gender-typed interest hypothesis under maximally contrasting school environments.

iii. Most earlier studies have stopped at reporting *t*-values. Few have followed up with *percentage analyses* and *rank-order analyses* of the kind reported in the present study (Chapter IV), which together provide a richer picture of the comparative interest profile.

iv. There is a near-complete absence of *contemporary published M.Ed. dissertation work* on the subject from C.C.S. University, Meerut. A locally undertaken study can therefore be expected to provide useful regional baseline data.

v. The educational implications of the gender-typed pattern have rarely been worked out in *operational detail* — i.e., translated into specific suggestions for teachers, parents, school administrators and counsellors. The present study attempts a modest improvement on this front in Chapter V.

The present investigation has been planned to *partly* fill the above gaps, while honestly recognising that its small sample, single-district scope and short time frame impose obvious limits on the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn.

## 2.7 Summary

The chapter has surveyed twelve Indian and six foreign studies on the *vocational interests of adolescents* with special reference to *gender differences*. The trends emerging from the literature are: (a) a *gender-typed pattern* is found with great regularity — boys higher on Scientific, Constructive, Agricultural and Persuasive areas; girls higher on Literary, Artistic, Social and Household; (b) *locality* and *type of school management* produce additional, systematic differences; (c) the pattern is *cross-culturally consistent* and has been replicated in the U.S.A., Korea, Ireland and Saudi Arabia, although effect sizes vary slightly across cultures; (d) the pattern has shown *small but discernible narrowing* over the decades, especially in the Scientific area in urban samples; and (e) a *Meerut District-specific, contemporary, gender-comparative* study using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. on Class IX–X students drawn from contrasting school environments has not been undertaken in recent years, and the present study attempts to fill this gap within the limits of an M.Ed. dissertation. Chapter III now presents in detail the methodology adopted in the present investigation.
