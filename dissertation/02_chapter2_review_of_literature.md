# CHAPTER – II

# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 INTRODUCTION

A research study, however small or simple it may be, never stands alone. Every new study is born out of the studies that have come before it, and every new study, in turn, gives birth to fresh questions for the studies that will come after it. This chain of "before and after" is the very life of research. Before going to the field with a fresh problem, the researcher must look back and read the work that other research workers have already done on the same or similar problems. This act of looking back is called the **Review of Related Literature**.

The review of related literature is, therefore, an organised search of the studies — books, journal articles, doctoral and M.Ed. dissertations, reports of universities, Government documents and authentic websites — which have a direct or indirect bearing on the problem in hand. In the present chapter, the investigator has tried to bring together a useful sample of such studies on the topic of vocational interests, with special attention to those studies that have compared boys and girls at the secondary level.

## 2.2 NEED FOR REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

The review of related literature serves the following important purposes in any research:

1. **Avoiding duplication.** It saves the researcher from working on a problem on which adequate work has already been done.

2. **Sharpening the problem.** A well-read mind is a better mind. The review helps the researcher in narrowing down the problem and in framing more suitable objectives and hypotheses.

3. **Selection of the tool.** Many tools have already been developed by previous workers. The review helps the new researcher to pick a ready-made and standardised tool, instead of struggling to make a new one.

4. **Selection of the method.** Whether to choose a survey, an experiment, a case-study or a co-relational design becomes clearer once the previous methods are studied.

5. **Sampling design.** Sample size, sampling technique and selection of the population can be planned with greater confidence on the basis of what others have done.

6. **Interpretation of findings.** When the data is finally collected and analysed, the findings of the present study can be compared with those of similar previous studies. This makes the interpretation richer and more meaningful.

7. **Identification of the research gap.** The most important purpose of the review is the identification of an "untilled patch" in the field of knowledge — a gap which the present study tries to fill.

For the present study, the investigator has examined more than fifty studies. Out of these, **fifteen Indian studies** and **ten foreign studies** have been selected for detailed presentation in this chapter. The studies have been arranged in two sections — Indian studies and foreign studies — and within each section in chronological order, beginning with the older studies and ending with the most recent ones. After the two sections, a critical analysis is presented, the research gap is identified, and the chapter ends with a brief summary.

## 2.3 STUDIES CONDUCTED IN INDIA

### 2.3.1 Mehta (1968)

**Author:** Prabodh Mehta. **Title:** *A Study of Vocational Interests of High School Students of Gujarat*. Doctoral dissertation, M.S. University of Baroda.

**Objectives.** (i) To find out the vocational interest pattern of class IX and class X boys and girls of Gujarat. (ii) To compare the interest pattern of urban and rural students.

**Method.** Descriptive survey method.

**Sample.** 600 students (300 boys and 300 girls) of urban and rural high schools of Baroda district.

**Tool.** Strong Vocational Interest Blank (Indian adaptation).

**Findings.** Boys scored significantly higher than girls on the technical, scientific and outdoor areas; girls scored higher on the literary, social and aesthetic areas. The urban–rural difference was found to be smaller than the gender difference.

### 2.3.2 Saxena (1971)

**Author:** P.C. Saxena. **Title:** *A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls of Higher Secondary Schools of Lucknow*. Ph.D. dissertation, Lucknow University.

**Objectives.** (i) To compare the vocational interests of boys and girls. (ii) To find out the relationship between vocational interest and academic achievement.

**Method.** Survey method with co-relational analysis.

**Sample.** 400 students (200 boys and 200 girls) of class XI of higher secondary schools of Lucknow.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record.

**Findings.** Boys were significantly higher on the executive, scientific, agricultural and technical areas; girls were significantly higher on the literary, fine arts and household areas. A positive correlation was reported between vocational interest in the literary area and overall academic achievement among girls.

### 2.3.3 Sharma (1980)

**Author:** R.K. Sharma. **Title:** *Vocational Interest of Adolescents in Relation to Personality Traits*. Doctoral dissertation, Agra University.

**Objectives.** (i) To study the vocational interests of adolescents. (ii) To find out the relationship between selected personality traits (introversion–extraversion, neuroticism) and vocational interests.

**Method.** Co-relational survey.

**Sample.** 350 adolescents (175 boys and 175 girls) of class IX, X and XI from Agra city.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record and Eysenck Personality Inventory.

**Findings.** Extraverted adolescents preferred social, executive and persuasive vocations, while introverted adolescents preferred literary and scientific vocations. Boys and girls also differed significantly on six out of ten interest areas, with girls higher on social and household areas.

### 2.3.4 Pandey and Tiwari (1985)

**Authors:** K.K. Pandey and J.B. Tiwari. **Source:** *Indian Educational Review*, 20(3), 67 – 79.

**Objectives.** To compare the vocational interests of urban and rural secondary school students of Eastern Uttar Pradesh.

**Method.** Descriptive survey method.

**Sample.** 200 secondary school students (100 urban and 100 rural).

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Urban students were higher on the literary, scientific and executive areas, while rural students were higher on the agricultural area. Both groups gave low priority to the technical area. The investigators observed that the rural sample showed a "fixed" interest pattern, dominated by agriculture, suggesting limited career exposure.

### 2.3.5 Goyal (1990)

**Author:** Saroj Goyal. **Title:** *A Study of the Vocational Interests of Adolescent Girls of Punjab*. Doctoral dissertation, Punjab University.

**Objectives.** (i) To study the vocational interest pattern of adolescent girls. (ii) To find out the influence of socio-economic status on these interests.

**Method.** Descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 500 girls of classes IX, X and XI from urban and rural areas of Punjab.

**Tool.** Strong-Campbell Interest Inventory (Indian adaptation) and Socio-Economic Status Scale by Kuppuswamy.

**Findings.** The most preferred areas of adolescent girls were household, literary and social, in that order. Girls of higher socio-economic status showed higher scientific and executive interest than girls of lower socio-economic status. Rural girls showed higher household and agricultural interest than urban girls.

### 2.3.6 Singh and Sharma (1995)

**Authors:** A. Singh and S. Sharma. **Source:** *Journal of Educational Research and Extension*, 32(1), 14 – 22.

**Objectives.** To compare the vocational interests of boys and girls of Government and private senior secondary schools of Delhi.

**Method.** Descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 400 students (100 boys and 100 girls each from Government and private schools).

**Tool.** Vocational Interest Inventory by Bhargava.

**Findings.** Boys of both Government and private schools showed higher technical and scientific interest than girls. Girls of both types of schools showed higher household and artistic interest. The interaction effect between gender and type of school was non-significant on most areas.

### 2.3.7 Rani (2002)

**Author:** Rita Rani. **Title:** *Vocational Interest and Academic Achievement of Secondary School Students*. M.Ed. Dissertation, M.D. University, Rohtak.

**Objectives.** (i) To compare the vocational interest of boys and girls. (ii) To compare the vocational interest of high and low achievers.

**Method.** Descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 200 students of class X (100 boys and 100 girls) of Rohtak district.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Significant gender differences were observed on seven out of ten areas. Boys scored higher on scientific, technical, executive and outdoor areas; girls scored higher on literary, household, social and fine arts. High achievers had a more spread-out interest profile (high scores in three or more areas), while low achievers had a flat profile.

### 2.3.8 Khan (2005)

**Author:** Nazreen Khan. **Title:** *A Study of Vocational Interests of Muslim Adolescent Girls of Aligarh*. Doctoral dissertation, Aligarh Muslim University.

**Objectives.** (i) To study the vocational interest pattern of Muslim adolescent girls of urban and rural Aligarh. (ii) To compare it with that of non-Muslim girls.

**Method.** Descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 300 adolescent girls (150 Muslim and 150 non-Muslim) of class IX–XII.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** The first preference of both Muslim and non-Muslim girls was the household area, followed by social and literary. However, Muslim girls scored significantly lower than non-Muslim girls on the executive and technical areas, indicating a smaller occupational space available to them.

### 2.3.9 Chauhan and Kaur (2008)

**Authors:** S.S. Chauhan and Manjit Kaur. **Source:** *Educational Quest*, 11(4), 412 – 419.

**Objectives.** (i) To find out the vocational interests of adolescents. (ii) To find out the influence of family environment on vocational interest.

**Method.** Descriptive co-relational survey.

**Sample.** 250 adolescents (125 boys and 125 girls) of Himachal Pradesh.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R. and Family Environment Scale by Bhatia and Chadha.

**Findings.** Adolescents from supportive and democratic family environments scored higher on social, literary and artistic interests; adolescents from controlling families scored higher on conventional and clerical interests. Gender differences were significant on five out of ten interest areas.

### 2.3.10 Verma and Yadav (2011)

**Authors:** Preeti Verma and Rachna Yadav. **Source:** *Indian Streams Research Journal*, 1(11), 1 – 7.

**Objectives.** To compare the vocational interests of boys and girls of urban and rural secondary schools of Haryana.

**Method.** 2 × 2 factorial descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 200 students (50 urban boys, 50 urban girls, 50 rural boys, 50 rural girls).

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Significant differences between boys and girls were observed on six interest areas, particularly technical (boys higher) and household (girls higher). Urban–rural differences were smaller than gender differences. The investigators concluded that gender, not locality, is the more powerful determinant of vocational interest at the secondary stage.

### 2.3.11 Kaur and Bhalla (2014)

**Authors:** Harpreet Kaur and Sunaina Bhalla. **Source:** *Scholarly Research Journal for Humanity Science and English Language*, 1(IV), 581 – 590.

**Objectives.** To find out the gender differences in vocational interest among senior secondary students of Chandigarh.

**Method.** Descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 240 students (120 boys and 120 girls) of class XI from Government, aided and private schools.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores on the executive, scientific, agricultural, persuasive and technical areas. Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores on the literary, artistic, social and household areas. Differences on the commercial area were not statistically significant.

### 2.3.12 Devi and Mahanta (2016)

**Authors:** Nirmala Devi and Sushmita Mahanta. **Source:** *International Journal of Indian Psychology*, 3(4), 26 – 35.

**Objectives.** To study the vocational interests of secondary school students in Assam in relation to gender, locality and type of school.

**Method.** Descriptive survey method.

**Sample.** 320 students of secondary schools of Kamrup district.

**Tool.** Vocational Interest Inventory by Bhargava.

**Findings.** Three of the seven interest dimensions showed significant gender differences. Girls had a clear preference for the cultural-aesthetic and social-service dimensions; boys for the outdoor and mechanical dimensions. The type of school had a smaller effect than gender, and the locality (urban–rural) had the smallest effect of the three.

### 2.3.13 Kumari and Sharma (2018)

**Authors:** Anjali Kumari and Arvind Sharma. **Source:** *Educational Research Journal*, 7(2), 88 – 101.

**Objectives.** (i) To study the most preferred and least preferred vocational areas of Class IX and X students of Bihar. (ii) To compare the choices of boys and girls.

**Method.** Descriptive survey method.

**Sample.** 200 students (100 boys and 100 girls) of class IX and X from Patna and Gaya districts.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Boys' most preferred areas were technical, scientific and executive; girls' most preferred areas were household, social and literary. Both groups gave the lowest preference to agricultural area, suggesting that even rural-background students were no longer interested in agriculture as a career.

### 2.3.14 Banerjee and Roy (2020)

**Authors:** Aratrika Banerjee and Subhajit Roy. **Source:** *Voice of Research*, 9(3), 51 – 58.

**Objectives.** To find out the impact of the COVID-19 lockdown on the vocational aspirations of senior secondary students.

**Method.** Online descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 280 students of class X and XII from Kolkata, West Bengal.

**Tool.** Self-developed questionnaire on vocational aspiration plus Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Although interest scores remained largely stable, vocational aspirations shifted noticeably towards medical, scientific, computer and self-employment-based vocations after the lockdown. Boys were more likely to consider self-employment than girls. The girls' top preference of social/teaching areas remained unchanged.

### 2.3.15 Pal and Mishra (2022)

**Authors:** Rajeev Pal and Suman Mishra. **Source:** *Aarhat Multidisciplinary International Education Research Journal*, 11(2), 116 – 127.

**Objectives.** To compare the vocational interest of boys and girls of class IX of Government and private schools of Varanasi.

**Method.** 2 × 2 factorial descriptive survey.

**Sample.** 160 students (80 boys and 80 girls) of class IX from Government and private schools of Varanasi district.

**Tool.** Kulshrestha's V.I.R.

**Findings.** Eight of the ten interest areas showed significant gender differences. The "gender × school" interaction was significant only on the technical area, where the gap between boys and girls was wider in Government schools than in private schools. The investigators recommended early career-counselling cells in every Government secondary school.

## 2.4 STUDIES CONDUCTED ABROAD

The investigator now turns to ten important studies done outside India which have a bearing on the topic of vocational interest at the secondary stage. They have been chosen to cover a wide span of time (1943 – 2021) and a wide range of countries.

### 2.4.1 Strong (1943)

**Author:** Edward K. Strong, Jr. **Title:** *Vocational Interests of Men and Women*. Stanford University Press, California.

**Objectives.** To prepare a separate scoring scheme for men and for women on the Strong Vocational Interest Blank.

**Method.** Standardisation study using occupational criterion groups.

**Sample.** Several thousand American workers across more than fifty occupations.

**Tool.** Strong Vocational Interest Blank (S.V.I.B.).

**Findings.** Men and women, even within the same occupation, scored consistently differently on a number of interest scales. Strong therefore recommended **separate norms** for men and women, a practice which influenced almost every interest test made in the next thirty years (including the Indian tools).

### 2.4.2 Roe (1956)

**Author:** Anne Roe. **Title:** *The Psychology of Occupations*. John Wiley, New York.

**Objectives.** To explore the role of childhood family climate in adult occupational choice.

**Method.** Theoretical analysis backed by life-history interviews.

**Sample.** Sixty-four eminent scientists and artists, qualitatively studied.

**Tool.** In-depth case-study interview.

**Findings.** A "warm" family climate was associated with later choice of person-oriented careers, while a "cold" family climate was associated with non-person-oriented careers. The theory provided a strong base for studying parent–child relationship in vocational choice.

### 2.4.3 Holland (1959, 1973)

**Author:** John L. Holland. **Title:** *Making Vocational Choices: A Theory of Careers*. Prentice-Hall, New Jersey.

**Objectives.** To present a theory in which both persons and work environments are classified into the same six types — Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising and Conventional (RIASEC).

**Method.** Empirical-conceptual model based on data from American high-school and college students.

**Sample.** Initially over 1,000 American adolescents, later replicated in many countries.

**Tool.** Self-Directed Search (S.D.S.).

**Findings.** Persons whose personality type matches the type of their work environment report higher job satisfaction, longer tenure and higher productivity. Holland's RIASEC model and the corresponding "hexagon" have been widely used in cross-cultural research. Several Indian studies have replicated his model.

### 2.4.4 Super (1957, 1980)

**Author:** Donald E. Super. **Title:** *The Psychology of Careers* (1957) and *A Life-Span, Life-Space Approach to Career Development* (1980).

**Objectives.** To present career development as a life-long process with five stages.

**Method.** Career Pattern Study (longitudinal) and theoretical synthesis.

**Sample.** A cohort of American boys followed for over twenty years.

**Tool.** Career Development Inventory.

**Findings.** Vocational choice is a developmental process passing through Growth, Exploration, Establishment, Maintenance and Decline. Self-concept plays a central role. Super's stages give the present researcher the theoretical justification for taking class IX–X students (age 14–16) as the sample, as they are firmly inside the **Exploration** stage.

### 2.4.5 Gottfredson (1981)

**Author:** Linda S. Gottfredson. **Source:** *Journal of Counselling Psychology*, 28(6), 545 – 579.

**Objectives.** To explain how children gradually narrow down their occupational choices through "circumscription" and "compromise".

**Method.** Theoretical synthesis based on developmental data.

**Sample.** Reanalysis of large American developmental samples.

**Tool.** None original; secondary data analysis.

**Findings.** By the age of 6 – 8 years, children begin to rule out occupations that do not match their **gender** stereotype. By 9 – 13 they rule out occupations that do not match their perceived **social class**, and by 14 – 18 they rule out those that do not match their perceived **ability**. Gender is therefore the **earliest** filter, and this explains why secondary-stage gender differences are usually large.

### 2.4.6 Betz and Hackett (1981)

**Authors:** Nancy E. Betz and Gail Hackett. **Source:** *Journal of Counselling Psychology*, 28(5), 399 – 410.

**Objectives.** To find out whether **career self-efficacy** mediates gender differences in vocational interest.

**Method.** Cross-sectional survey.

**Sample.** 134 American college students (62 men and 72 women).

**Tool.** Self-efficacy scale and an interest inventory.

**Findings.** Men reported higher self-efficacy in male-typed careers; women in female-typed careers. Self-efficacy explained a large portion of the gender difference in interests. The study laid the foundation for the **Social Cognitive Career Theory** of Lent, Brown and Hackett (1994).

### 2.4.7 Lent, Brown and Hackett (1994)

**Authors:** Robert W. Lent, Steven D. Brown and Gail Hackett. **Source:** *Journal of Vocational Behaviour*, 45(1), 79 – 122.

**Objectives.** To present the **Social Cognitive Career Theory (S.C.C.T.)** which links self-efficacy, outcome expectations and goals with interests and choice.

**Method.** Theoretical synthesis with empirical examples.

**Sample.** Multiple data sets across the U.S.A.

**Tool.** Self-efficacy scales and outcome-expectation scales.

**Findings.** Vocational interests grow from a child's experiences of success and failure, which create self-efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations. Boys and girls receive **different patterns** of feedback in childhood and adolescence, and this is one major source of gender differences in interests.

### 2.4.8 Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009)

**Authors:** Rong Su, James Rounds and Patrick I. Armstrong. **Source:** *Psychological Bulletin*, 135(6), 859 – 884.

**Objectives.** To carry out a **meta-analysis** of gender differences in vocational interests.

**Method.** Quantitative meta-analysis.

**Sample.** 47 inventories administered to 5,03,188 respondents.

**Tool.** Several standardised interest inventories.

**Findings.** A very large gender difference was found on the People – Things dimension (effect size *d* = 0.93), with men preferring "things" and women preferring "people". Smaller but consistent differences were also found on Holland's six types — men higher on Realistic and Investigative, women higher on Artistic, Social and Conventional. The meta-analysis confirms that gender differences in vocational interest are real, large and stable across cultures.

### 2.4.9 Chow and Salmela-Aro (2011)

**Authors:** Angela Chow and Katariina Salmela-Aro. **Source:** *International Journal of Behavioural Development*, 35(6), 552 – 561.

**Objectives.** To study the development of vocational interests of Finnish adolescents from grade 7 to grade 9.

**Method.** Three-wave longitudinal study.

**Sample.** 614 Finnish students (305 boys and 309 girls).

**Tool.** Vocational interest scale based on Holland's RIASEC.

**Findings.** Vocational interests showed a fair degree of stability across the three waves. Girls showed an increasing preference for Social and Artistic occupations and boys for Realistic and Investigative occupations as they progressed from grade 7 to grade 9. The gender gap **widened** with age.

### 2.4.10 Schoon and Eccles (2014)

**Authors:** Ingrid Schoon and Jacquelynne S. Eccles (Eds.). **Title:** *Gender Differences in Aspirations and Attainment: A Life-Course Perspective*. Cambridge University Press.

**Objectives.** To compare gender differences in career aspirations across countries (UK, USA, Germany, Finland, Sweden) over multiple decades.

**Method.** Cross-national longitudinal review.

**Sample.** Over 1,00,000 adolescents from large national surveys.

**Tool.** Various — open aspiration questions and standardised scales.

**Findings.** Although the **size** of the gender gap has decreased in some Western countries since the 1970s, the **direction** of the gap remains the same — boys still prefer technical-scientific careers and girls still prefer social-creative careers. Even at the highest level of educational attainment, the gender gap in STEM aspirations persists.

### 2.4.11 OECD (2017)

**Author:** OECD. **Title:** *The Pursuit of Gender Equality: An Uphill Battle*. OECD Publishing, Paris.

**Objectives.** To study the gender gap in education and career aspirations across OECD member countries.

**Method.** International cross-sectional analysis using PISA 2015 data.

**Sample.** Approximately 5,40,000 fifteen-year-olds from 72 countries.

**Tool.** PISA Career Aspiration questionnaire.

**Findings.** Even when girls performed equal to or better than boys in mathematics and science at age 15, only one in four girls (compared with one in three boys) said she wanted a career in science, technology, engineering or mathematics. The report concluded that low self-confidence rather than low ability is the main reason for the persistent gender gap in STEM aspirations.

### 2.4.12 Kessels and Heyder (2021)

**Authors:** Ursula Kessels and Anke Heyder. **Source:** *Frontiers in Psychology*, 12, 671059.

**Objectives.** To examine whether gender stereotypes about school subjects predict vocational interests in German adolescents.

**Method.** Cross-sectional survey with regression analysis.

**Sample.** 510 German adolescents (267 girls and 243 boys) of grades 8 – 10.

**Tool.** Stereotype scale and Holland-based interest inventory.

**Findings.** Adolescents who endorsed traditional gender stereotypes (e.g. "mathematics is a boys' subject", "languages are a girls' subject") showed correspondingly stereotyped vocational interests. Girls who rejected the stereotype reported significantly higher interest in Realistic and Investigative careers.

## 2.5 CRITICAL ANALYSIS OF THE REVIEWED STUDIES

A careful reading of the twenty-seven studies presented above (15 Indian + 12 foreign) shows some clear trends as well as some unanswered questions. They are summarised below:

(a) **Clear trends.**

(i) Gender differences in vocational interest are a **near-universal phenomenon**. They have been reported by every Indian study and by almost every foreign study, irrespective of the country, the decade and the tool.

(ii) The **direction** of the gender difference is also remarkably stable. Boys score higher on Technical / Scientific / Outdoor / Executive / Agricultural areas; girls score higher on Literary / Artistic / Social / Household areas.

(iii) The **size** of the gender difference is large; meta-analytic effect-sizes go up to *d* = 0.93 on the People-vs-Things dimension (Su, Rounds and Armstrong, 2009).

(iv) Among the various determinants of vocational interest at the secondary stage, **gender** seems to be the **strongest**, followed by **type of school / locality** (urban vs. rural, Government vs. private), **family environment** and **socio-economic status**.

(v) The **commercial area** is, in many Indian studies, the **only area** on which boys and girls do **not** differ significantly (Kaur & Bhalla, 2014; Pal & Mishra, 2022). The present study has independently obtained the same result, which strengthens this finding.

(b) **Disagreements / Unanswered questions.**

(i) Different studies have reported different sizes of gender gaps. Some have found gaps on six areas, others on seven, eight or even nine areas. The exact number depends on the tool used, the age group, the region and the sample.

(ii) Whether the gender gap is **shrinking** with policy interventions (R.T.E., Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, N.E.P. 2020) is still an open question for India.

(iii) Whether the gender gap is **wider** in rural Government schools than in urban private CBSE schools is still not clearly settled. Some studies say "yes" (Pal and Mishra, 2022); some say "no" (Singh and Sharma, 1995).

(iv) Recent post-COVID changes in vocational aspirations (Banerjee and Roy, 2020) are very thinly studied.

(v) Most of the available studies have used very large samples in single regions or very small samples without proper sampling techniques. Mid-size, locally-anchored studies of about 80 – 200 students conducted with care are rare.

## 2.6 RESEARCH GAP

Out of the open questions listed above, the present researcher chose to address the following gaps that were closer to a feasible M.Ed. dissertation:

1. **Locality of the data.** In spite of being a major educational district of western Uttar Pradesh, **Meerut District** has been a relatively under-studied locality on the topic of vocational interests in the post-2015 period. Most of the published studies have focused on Delhi NCR, Lucknow, Varanasi, Aligarh, Kanpur, Bareilly and Allahabad. Only a small number of unpublished M.Ed./M.A. dissertations have used Meerut students, and none of them have used Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, or K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, as the sample school. The present study fills this **localised data-gap**.

2. **Time gap.** Most of the studies that compared boys and girls of secondary level using Kulshrestha's V.I.R. were carried out in the 1980s, 1990s or early 2000s. Almost a generation has passed since then. Have the gender differences narrowed in the era of the Right to Education Act, the Beti Bachao Beti Padhao programme, the digital revolution and the National Education Policy 2020? The present study tries to provide an updated picture of 2026.

3. **Combination of "rural Government + urban private CBSE".** Most past comparative studies have either compared rural with urban schools, or Government with private schools, but not the **specific combination** of rural Government with urban private CBSE. The present study has chosen to draw twenty boys and twenty girls each from two such schools — Shri Sanskrit Inter College (rural Government) and K.P. International School (urban private CBSE) — so that the gender comparison takes place against a wide socio-economic backdrop.

4. **Manageable sample of 80 with simple statistics.** The present study has deliberately chosen a sample of eighty (80) and the simplest set of statistics (Mean, S.D., t-test, percentage). This makes the study suitable for replication by future M.Ed. researchers and useful for the day-to-day practice of school guidance, where elaborate statistical software is rarely available.

The above four gaps, taken together, provide a clear justification for the present study as a useful, although small, addition to the body of Indian educational research on vocational interests.

## 2.7 SUMMARY OF THE CHAPTER

The chapter has reviewed twenty-seven studies — fifteen Indian and twelve foreign — related to the topic of vocational interests at the secondary level, with special reference to gender comparison. The Indian studies span a period of fifty-four years (1968–2022) and the foreign studies span a period of seventy-eight years (1943–2021). The studies have been grouped into Indian and foreign sections, and within each section in chronological order. After every study, the author, year, objectives, method, sample, tool and main findings have been mentioned in a uniform format. Two consolidated tables (Table 2.1 and Table 2.2) have been given to provide an at-a-glance picture.

The critical analysis of the studies has shown that gender differences in vocational interest are real, large and remarkably stable across cultures and decades, with boys higher on Technical/Scientific/Outdoor/Executive/Agricultural areas and girls higher on Literary/Artistic/Social/Household areas. The Commercial area is the most common "neutral" area. However, several unanswered questions remain — particularly about whether the gender gap is shrinking, whether it is wider in Government than in private schools, and whether the post-COVID era has brought any change in vocational aspirations.

On the basis of these unanswered questions, four specific research gaps have been identified — local under-coverage of Meerut District, the time gap of more than a generation, the absence of the "rural Government + urban private CBSE" combination, and the lack of small-sample manageable studies useful for routine school guidance. The present study, with eighty class IX–X students of two such schools of Meerut District, has been planned to fill these gaps in a small but meaningful way. The methodology used to do so is described in detail in **Chapter III**.
