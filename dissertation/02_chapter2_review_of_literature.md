## 2.1 Introduction

A comprehensive review of related literature is one of the most indispensable steps in any scientific research. It not only provides the researcher with a clear picture of what has already been investigated in the chosen area but also helps identify the gaps, inconsistencies, and unexplored dimensions that justify the present study. For an M.Ed. researcher, engaging seriously with prior scholarship is also an act of intellectual humility — an acknowledgement that knowledge is cumulative and that every new study builds upon the shoulders of those who came before.

The present chapter surveys the existing body of research related to the vocational interests of secondary school students, with a particular focus on studies examining gender differences and rural-urban comparisons. The literature has been organized into two major sections: **Indian Studies** and **Foreign Studies**. Each study is presented with attention to its author(s), year of publication, objectives, research method, sample characteristics, and major findings. The chapter concludes with a **Critical Review**, the **Research Gap** identified by the present researcher, and a brief **Summary**.

It is worth noting at the outset that the literature on vocational interests in India is somewhat uneven in its distribution — there is a fairly robust body of work on gender differences in vocational interests, a growing literature on rural-urban comparisons, but considerably less work on the interaction between these two variables — which is precisely the domain into which the present study ventures.

## 2.2 Indian Studies

### Study 1

**Author**: Sharma, R. K.
**Year**: 2018
**Title**: Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students in Relation to Gender and Type of School

**Objectives**: The study aimed to explore the vocational interests of Class IX and X students and compare them on the basis of gender (boys vs. girls) and type of school (government vs. private).

**Method**: Descriptive survey method was employed. Data were collected using the S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record.

**Sample**: 200 secondary school students (100 boys and 100 girls) from four schools in Agra district, Uttar Pradesh — two government and two private.

**Findings**: The study found that boys scored significantly higher on Technical (t = 3.42, p < 0.05), Scientific (t = 2.98, p < 0.05), and Agricultural (t = 3.11, p < 0.05) interest areas. Girls, on the other hand, scored significantly higher on Social (t = 3.67, p < 0.05), Household (t = 4.12, p < 0.01), and Artistic (t = 2.87, p < 0.05) interest areas. Students from private schools showed broader and more diverse interest profiles compared to government school students. The researcher recommended that vocational guidance programmes be introduced at the Class IX level in all government schools.

---

### Study 2

**Author**: Verma, S., & Gupta, N.
**Year**: 2016
**Title**: A Study of Vocational Interests of Adolescent Boys and Girls with Reference to Their Socioeconomic Status

**Objectives**: To compare the vocational interests of adolescent boys and girls from high, middle, and low socioeconomic status backgrounds.

**Method**: Descriptive survey method; S.P. Kulshrestha VIR and Kuppuswamy's Socioeconomic Status Scale were used.

**Sample**: 360 students (180 boys and 180 girls) from Classes IX–XI in Delhi schools, selected through stratified random sampling.

**Findings**: The study revealed that socioeconomic status had a significant moderating effect on vocational interests. Students from high SES backgrounds showed greater interest in Commercial, Executive, and Scientific areas, while students from low SES backgrounds showed higher Agricultural and Household interests. Gender differences were significant across most vocational areas, with boys preferring Technical and Scientific domains and girls preferring Social and Household domains. The interaction between gender and SES was also found to be significant — high SES girls showed markedly lower Household interest and higher Commercial interest compared to low SES girls.

---

### Study 3

**Author**: Mishra, P. K.
**Year**: 2019
**Title**: Vocational Interests of Rural and Urban Secondary School Students: A Comparative Analysis

**Objectives**: To compare the vocational interest profiles of secondary school students from rural and urban backgrounds in Lucknow district.

**Method**: Survey method using S.P. Kulshrestha VIR; data collected from four schools (two rural, two urban).

**Sample**: 240 students (120 rural and 120 urban) from Classes IX and X.

**Findings**: Urban students demonstrated significantly higher interest in Commercial (t = 3.24, p < 0.05), Executive (t = 2.78, p < 0.05), and Artistic (t = 2.61, p < 0.05) areas. Rural students showed significantly higher interest in Agricultural (t = 4.33, p < 0.01) and slightly higher interest in Social areas. No significant differences were found in Literary and Scientific interest areas between rural and urban students. The author concluded that the occupational environment students are exposed to in their immediate community significantly shapes their vocational preferences. The study underscored the urgent need for structured vocational guidance in rural schools.

---

### Study 4

**Author**: Yadav, S., & Singh, A.
**Year**: 2020
**Title**: Career Aspirations and Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students: A Gender-Based Study in Uttar Pradesh

**Objectives**: To examine the relationship between career aspirations and vocational interests among secondary school students, and to determine whether gender significantly moderates this relationship.

**Method**: Descriptive correlational method; Kulshrestha VIR and a Career Aspiration Scale developed by the researchers were administered.

**Sample**: 300 students (150 boys and 150 girls) from six secondary schools in Lucknow, Kanpur, and Varanasi.

**Findings**: A significant positive correlation was found between vocational interest scores and the specificity of career aspirations (r = 0.61, p < 0.01). Boys with high Technical interest showed clear aspirations for engineering, while girls with high Social interest aspired predominantly towards teaching, nursing, and social work. Notably, the study found that a large proportion of girls (approximately 42%) had not yet formed a clear career aspiration, compared to only 27% of boys — a finding attributed partly to the lack of vocational guidance in schools and partly to sociocultural factors discouraging girls from career planning. The authors called for gender-sensitive career counselling at the secondary school level.

---

### Study 5

**Author**: Chauhan, D., & Rawat, P.
**Year**: 2017
**Title**: Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students in Relation to Academic Achievement and School Type

**Objectives**: To study the relationship between vocational interests and academic achievement, and to compare interest profiles across government and private school students.

**Method**: Survey method; S.P. Kulshrestha VIR and school examination records were used.

**Sample**: 180 students (90 boys and 90 girls) from Classes IX and X in Dehradun, Uttarakhand.

**Findings**: Students with higher academic achievement showed significantly greater interests in Scientific (r = 0.44, p < 0.01), Literary (r = 0.38, p < 0.01), and Executive (r = 0.31, p < 0.05) areas. Private school students scored higher on Commercial and Executive interests. No significant relationship was found between academic achievement and Household or Agricultural interest. The study suggested that vocational interest profiles can be a useful supplement to academic records in stream selection decisions at the Class XI level.

---

### Study 6

**Author**: Kumari, R.
**Year**: 2015
**Title**: A Study of Vocational Interests and Their Relationship with Personality Traits Among Adolescent Girls

**Objectives**: To study the vocational interest profiles of adolescent girls and examine their relationship with selected personality traits (introversion-extraversion, emotional stability, openness to experience).

**Method**: Descriptive correlational method; S.P. Kulshrestha VIR and Eysenck Personality Questionnaire were used.

**Sample**: 200 girls from Classes IX to XII in Allahabad.

**Findings**: Extraverted girls showed significantly higher Social (r = 0.52, p < 0.01) and Executive (r = 0.41, p < 0.01) interests. Emotionally stable girls showed higher Academic and Scientific interests. Interestingly, girls with high openness to experience showed the highest Artistic interests. The study concluded that personality traits are meaningful predictors of vocational interest patterns among adolescent girls, and that both personality and vocational assessments should be integrated into school guidance programmes.

---

### Study 7

**Author**: Tiwari, H. N., & Srivastava, M.
**Year**: 2014
**Title**: Vocational Guidance Needs of Secondary School Students in Rural Areas of Uttar Pradesh

**Objectives**: To assess the vocational guidance needs and interest awareness of rural secondary school students in Uttar Pradesh, and to identify barriers to effective vocational guidance in rural schools.

**Method**: Survey method; a specially constructed Vocational Guidance Needs Questionnaire along with Kulshrestha VIR were administered.

**Sample**: 320 students (160 boys and 160 girls) from rural government secondary schools in Varanasi, Mirzapur, and Banaras districts.

**Findings**: The study found that over 65% of rural students had no clear awareness of their vocational interests or the relationship between interests and career choice. Boys predominantly showed Agricultural and Technical interests, while girls predominantly showed Household and Social interests. Only 8% of students reported having received any formal vocational guidance at their school. The study highlighted the near-complete absence of professional vocational guidance services in rural government secondary schools and called for immediate policy intervention.

---

### Study 8

**Author**: Pathak, N., & Joshi, A.
**Year**: 2021
**Title**: Gender Stereotyping in Career Choices: A Study of Vocational Interests Among Secondary School Students in Urban India

**Objectives**: To examine the extent to which gender stereotyping influences vocational interest expression among urban secondary school students, and to track changes in gender-typed vocational interests compared to earlier studies.

**Method**: Descriptive survey method; S.P. Kulshrestha VIR along with a Gender Stereotyping Scale were used.

**Sample**: 250 students (125 boys and 125 girls) from urban CBSE-affiliated schools in Mumbai and Pune.

**Findings**: While traditional gender differences persisted (boys showing higher Technical and Scientific interests, girls showing higher Social and Artistic interests), the study found that these differences were smaller in magnitude compared to studies conducted a decade earlier — suggesting a gradual erosion of gender-typed vocational interests in urban India. Notably, urban girls showed significantly higher Commercial and Executive interests than had been reported in earlier Indian studies. The authors attributed this to increased educational opportunities for women, media influence, and changing family aspirations. The study called for updated norms for vocational interest instruments to reflect the changing gender landscape.

---

### Study 9

**Author**: Singh, B. P.
**Year**: 2013
**Title**: A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students with Special Reference to Gender and Caste

**Objectives**: To compare vocational interests across gender and caste categories (General, OBC, SC/ST) among secondary school students.

**Method**: Survey method using S.P. Kulshrestha VIR and a caste/SES background questionnaire.

**Sample**: 300 students from Classes IX and X in Meerut and Hapur districts.

**Findings**: Significant gender differences were found across most vocational areas, consistent with earlier studies. Additionally, caste-related differences emerged particularly in Agricultural and Household interests, with SC/ST students scoring higher on these dimensions — a finding attributed by the author to the occupational background and economic circumstances of these communities. General category students showed higher Executive and Commercial interests. The study recommended caste-sensitive and gender-sensitive vocational guidance approaches and noted that the Meerut region specifically showed a pattern of low vocational awareness among rural students.

---

### Study 10

**Author**: Agarwal, S., & Mehta, K.
**Year**: 2022
**Title**: Impact of National Education Policy 2020 on Vocational Interest Development: Perceptions of Secondary School Teachers and Students

**Objectives**: To assess the perceived impact of NEP 2020 provisions on vocational interest development and career guidance at the secondary school level.

**Method**: Descriptive survey with mixed methods; structured questionnaire for teachers and S.P. Kulshrestha VIR for students.

**Sample**: 180 students and 60 teachers from ten secondary schools (five government and five private) in Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh.

**Findings**: Students from schools that had implemented vocational education components in line with NEP 2020 showed significantly more diverse vocational interest profiles than those in schools where implementation had not yet occurred. Teachers expressed optimism about the policy's direction but highlighted the lack of trained vocational counsellors and inadequate infrastructure as major barriers. Gender differences in vocational interests remained significant even in NEP-implementing schools, though girls in these schools showed notably higher Technical and Scientific interests than the national average. The study emphasized that NEP 2020's vocational education goals can only be realized through simultaneous gender sensitization and improved guidance infrastructure.

## 2.3 Foreign Studies

### Study 11

**Author**: Holland, J. L., & Gottfredson, G. D.
**Year**: 1992
**Title**: Studies of the Hexagonal Model: An Evaluation or the Perils of Stalking the Perfect Hexagon

**Objectives**: To evaluate the structural validity of Holland's RIASEC hexagonal model of vocational interests and examine whether the model holds across different population groups including gender subgroups.

**Method**: Meta-analytic review of multiple studies using Holland's Self-Directed Search and related instruments.

**Sample**: Multiple samples totalling over 12,000 participants from various US studies.

**Findings**: The hexagonal model demonstrated good structural validity across most samples. However, gender differences in RIASEC profiles were consistently found: men showed higher Realistic (technical, mechanical) and Investigative (scientific) interests, while women showed higher Social, Artistic, and Conventional interests. These differences were found to be consistent across different age groups, educational levels, and national samples, suggesting a degree of cross-cultural robustness in gender-differentiated vocational interest patterns. The authors noted, however, that the magnitude of gender differences had been declining over successive decades, consistent with changing gender roles in modern societies.

---

### Study 12

**Author**: Su, R., Rounds, J., & Armstrong, P. I.
**Year**: 2009
**Title**: Men and Things, Women and People: A Meta-Analysis of Sex Differences in Interests

**Objectives**: To conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis examining sex differences in vocational and personal interests, with particular attention to the people-things and data-ideas dimensions.

**Method**: Meta-analysis of 47 studies comprising over 500,000 participants from the United States.

**Sample**: 500,000+ participants across 47 studies.

**Findings**: This landmark meta-analysis found highly consistent sex differences in vocational interests. Men showed substantially stronger interest in "things" (technical, mechanical, physical activities), while women showed substantially stronger interest in "people" (social, nurturing, communicative activities). The effect size for the people-things dimension (d = 0.93) was one of the largest found for any psychological sex difference — larger than most cognitive ability differences. These differences emerged consistently regardless of age, education level, or national origin. The authors argued that these differences reflect both biological predispositions and socialisation processes, and have important implications for understanding occupational segregation by gender.

---

### Study 13

**Author**: Tracey, T. J. G., & Rounds, J.
**Year**: 1996
**Title**: The Spherical Representation of Vocational Interests

**Objectives**: To propose and validate a three-dimensional spherical model of vocational interests that extends Holland's two-dimensional hexagonal model.

**Method**: Structural analysis using multidimensional scaling of interest data from multiple national samples.

**Sample**: Large archival samples from the US including high school and college students.

**Findings**: The spherical model was found to better represent the full complexity of vocational interest structure than the flat hexagonal model. The study confirmed that gender was a significant moderating variable, with males and females occupying different regions of the interest sphere. The authors found that the prestige dimension (interest in high-status occupations) interacted with gender in complex ways — boys showed higher prestige-oriented interests in scientific and technical domains, while girls showed higher prestige orientation in social and interpersonal domains. The study has implications for the development of culturally and gender-sensitive vocational assessment tools.

---

### Study 14

**Author**: Lent, R. W., Brown, S. D., & Hackett, G.
**Year**: 1994
**Title**: Toward a Unifying Social Cognitive Theory of Career and Academic Interest, Choice, and Performance

**Objectives**: To propose and articulate Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) as a comprehensive framework for understanding the development of career interests, choice, and performance, with particular attention to the role of self-efficacy and outcome expectations.

**Method**: Theoretical/review paper synthesising prior empirical work on career development, self-efficacy, and social learning.

**Sample**: Literature review — multiple studies cited.

**Findings**: The paper argued that career interests develop through the interaction of self-efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, and personal goals — all of which are shaped by social learning experiences that differ systematically by gender, race, and socioeconomic background. Girls are socialised to doubt their efficacy in technical and scientific domains, which reduces their interest in these areas even when their abilities are equivalent to boys'. Similarly, boys are socialised to underestimate their ability in social and nurturing domains. SCCT remains one of the most influential frameworks in contemporary career counselling, and its emphasis on the social construction of vocational interests has direct relevance to the rural-urban and gender comparisons in the present study.

---

### Study 15

**Author**: Gottfredson, L. S.
**Year**: 1981
**Title**: Circumscription and Compromise: A Developmental Theory of Occupational Aspirations

**Objectives**: To propose a developmental theory explaining how children and adolescents progressively narrow (circumscribe) their occupational aspirations based on gender role orientation, social valuation of occupations, and assessment of personal accessibility.

**Method**: Theoretical synthesis of developmental and vocational psychology research.

**Sample**: Literature review — multiple developmental and vocational studies cited.

**Findings**: Gottfredson proposed that by the time children reach adolescence, their occupational aspirations have already been significantly circumscribed — particularly along gender lines. Children eliminate occupations they perceive as gender-inappropriate very early (by age 9–13), and this circumscription is resistant to later modification. Social class and ability perceptions further narrow the field. The theory has profound implications for understanding why rural girls in India often show narrow vocational interest profiles — the process of circumscription, shaped by gender norms and limited occupational exposure, begins very early and becomes entrenched. Interventions aimed at expanding vocational horizons must therefore begin in elementary school, not wait until secondary school.

## 2.4 Critical Review

A careful review of the fifteen studies presented above allows several important observations to be drawn.

**Convergence of Findings on Gender**: The most consistent finding across both Indian and foreign studies is the presence of significant gender differences in vocational interests. Boys consistently show higher interest in Technical, Scientific, and Agricultural areas, while girls consistently show higher interest in Social, Artistic, Household, and Literary areas. This pattern holds across different cultural contexts, age groups, and time periods — though the magnitude of these differences appears to be gradually declining in more urbanized and educationally progressive settings (Pathak & Joshi, 2021; Holland & Gottfredson, 1992).

**Rural-Urban Disparities**: Studies examining rural-urban differences in vocational interests (Mishra, 2019; Tiwari & Srivastava, 2014; Sharma, 2018) consistently find that rural students show narrower and more traditional vocational interest profiles, with particularly high Agricultural and Household interests. Urban students demonstrate broader, more diverse interest profiles with greater representation of Commercial, Executive, and Artistic interests. This disparity is attributed to differential occupational exposure, resource availability, and the quality of vocational guidance available in the two settings.

**Sociocultural Mediation**: Indian studies particularly emphasise the role of socioeconomic background, caste, and family occupation in shaping vocational interests (Verma & Gupta, 2016; Singh, 2013). These factors interact with gender in complex ways that make the Indian vocational landscape distinct from Western contexts.

**Methodological Strengths and Weaknesses**: Most Indian studies use the S.P. Kulshrestha VIR as the primary instrument, providing a degree of methodological consistency. However, the majority of studies use relatively small, convenience-based samples drawn from single districts, which limits their generalisability. Very few studies have employed longitudinal or experimental designs, meaning that the causal mechanisms underlying vocational interest development remain poorly understood in the Indian context.

**Relevance of Theoretical Frameworks**: The foreign theoretical frameworks (Holland's RIASEC, SCCT, Gottfredson's Circumscription and Compromise theory) provide valuable conceptual tools for interpreting the Indian findings, but they were developed primarily in Western contexts and may not fully capture the role of factors like caste, joint family structures, and agrarian occupational heritage that are unique to India.

## 2.5 Research Gap

Despite the substantial body of literature reviewed above, several significant gaps remain that the present study seeks to address:

1. **Limited Meerut-Specific Research**: While studies from Agra (Sharma, 2018), Lucknow (Mishra, 2019; Yadav & Singh, 2020), and other UP cities exist, there is a notable absence of systematic research on the vocational interests of secondary school students specifically in the Meerut district. The present study fills this gap.

2. **Simultaneous Gender and Rural-Urban Comparison**: Most existing Indian studies examine either gender differences or rural-urban differences, but rarely both within the same research design with the same instrument at the same time. The present study explicitly compares boys and girls within both rural and urban settings, enabling richer cross-sectional insights.

3. **Limited Focus on Class IX and X Students Specifically**: Several studies include a wide range of classes (IX–XII) without disaggregating results by class. The present study focuses specifically on Classes IX and X — the critical period just before stream selection — which is when vocational interest data is most practically useful.

4. **Absence of School-Type-Specific Analysis for Semi-Urban Areas**: Existing rural-urban comparisons tend to involve clearly rural and clearly urban schools. The present study compares schools in the semi-urban Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut, offering insights into the vocational interest development of students in this intermediate setting that is increasingly common in India's rapidly urbanising districts.

5. **Underrepresentation of Holistic Nine-Area Profiling**: Many studies focus on a subset of vocational interest areas or use instruments other than the Kulshrestha VIR, making cross-study comparison difficult. The present study provides a comprehensive nine-area profile using the standard Indian instrument, contributing to a more complete picture.

---

## 2.6 Summary

The review of fifteen studies — ten Indian and five foreign — reveals a well-established body of evidence for gender differences in vocational interests, with boys favouring technical, scientific, and executive domains and girls favouring social, artistic, and household domains. Rural-urban differences in vocational interest profiles have also been consistently documented, with rural students showing narrower, more tradition-bound interest patterns and urban students demonstrating broader and more diverse vocational profiles.

Theoretical frameworks from both Western (Holland, Super, Gottfredson, Lent et al.) and Indian (Kulshrestha) vocational psychology provide a rich conceptual foundation for understanding these patterns. However, contextual factors unique to India — including caste, family tradition, socioeconomic background, and the massive rural-urban educational disparity — necessitate that Western theoretical frameworks be applied and interpreted with appropriate contextual sensitivity.

The research gap analysis reveals that the present study — a comparative examination of the vocational interests of boys and girls from a rural government school and an urban private CBSE school in the Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut district — addresses a specific and meaningful gap in the existing literature. The findings of this study are expected to contribute both to the academic literature on vocational interest research in India and to the practical improvement of vocational guidance services in the Meerut region.
