# REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

## 2.1 INTRODUCTION

A research investigation cannot be conducted in a vacuum. The investigator must first know *what has already been done* in the chosen area, *who has done it*, *how* it was done, *what was found* and *what still remains to be done*. Such an exercise is called the **Review of Related Literature**. It is the second important step in any educational research, after the formulation of the problem itself.

According to **W.R. Borg** (1971):

> "The literature in any field forms the foundation upon which all future work must be built. If we fail to build this foundation of knowledge provided by the review of literature, our work is likely to be shallow and naive and will often duplicate work that has already been done better by someone else."

According to **John W. Best** (1989):

> "Practically all human knowledge can be found in books and libraries. Unlike other animals that must start anew with each generation, man builds upon the accumulated and recorded knowledge of the past."

The present chapter, therefore, is devoted to a careful review of the literature on *vocational interests* of school students. The review covers a wide period — from the early 1970s up to the year 2024 — and includes both Indian and foreign studies. The chapter is divided into the following sections:

- 2.2 Importance of the Review of Related Literature.
- 2.3 Studies Conducted in India.
- 2.4 Studies Conducted Abroad.
- 2.5 Critical Appraisal of the Reviewed Literature.
- 2.6 Research Gap Identified.

---

## 2.2 IMPORTANCE OF THE REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

A systematic review of related literature serves the investigator in many ways. The most important of these are:

1. **It sharpens the problem.** A literature review helps the investigator define his / her problem more precisely, by showing how others have *worded* the same or similar questions.

2. **It avoids duplication.** It tells the investigator whether the same study has already been done, so that he / she does not waste time and energy on a question that already has an answer.

3. **It suggests methodology.** It tells the investigator *what method, sample, tool and statistical techniques* were used by earlier workers, and how successful those choices were.

4. **It provides theoretical background.** It places the present study in the wider *theoretical framework* of the chosen area, so that the findings can be meaningfully interpreted.

5. **It identifies the research gap.** It tells the investigator *what has not yet been done*. The unfilled gap is the very *justification* of the present study.

6. **It supplies hypotheses.** The findings of earlier studies often suggest *promising hypotheses* for the new investigation.

7. **It builds the bibliography.** The references collected during the review form the backbone of the *bibliography* of the dissertation.

For these reasons, the review of related literature is a *non-negotiable* part of every educational research project.

## 2.3 STUDIES CONDUCTED IN INDIA

A large number of studies on vocational interests have been carried out in India over the last fifty-five years. The most important of these are reviewed below in *chronological order*.

### 2.3.1 Saxena (1971)

**B.L. Saxena** investigated the vocational interest pattern of 200 boys of Class X of Lucknow city using the *Vocational Interest Inventory* by S.P. Chatterji. He found that *Scientific* interest occupied the first rank, followed by *Commercial* and *Persuasive* interests. *Agricultural* interest occupied the lowest rank. He concluded that the urban environment of Lucknow had pushed the boys towards *modern* lines of work and away from *traditional* lines.

### 2.3.2 Singh (1975)

**R.P. Singh** conducted a study of 400 Class IX students of rural and urban areas of Varanasi district using the *V.I.R.* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha. He found that rural students scored significantly higher than urban students on *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas, while urban students scored significantly higher on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Persuasive* areas. He concluded that the locality is one of the most powerful factors influencing vocational interests.

### 2.3.3 Sharma (1978)

**M.K. Sharma** studied 300 secondary school students of Delhi to find out the gender-based differences in vocational interests. He used the *Vocational Interest Record* (Hindi) and reported that boys scored significantly higher than girls on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Constructive* areas, while girls scored significantly higher on *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas. The pattern was found to be in line with the traditional gender-role expectations of Indian society.

### 2.3.4 Goyal (1980)

**J.K. Goyal** studied vocational interests of 500 students of Class IX–X of government and private schools of Bhopal. The findings of the study were that private school students obtained higher mean scores on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Commercial* areas than government school students. The author attributed the difference to the better infrastructural facilities and the higher socio-economic background of the private school students.

### 2.3.5 Pandey (1983)

**S.N. Pandey** investigated the vocational interest pattern of 250 Class X students of Allahabad in relation to their parents' occupation. He found a *strong family influence*: children of doctors had highest *Scientific* interest, children of farmers highest *Agricultural* interest, and children of businessmen highest *Commercial* interest.

### 2.3.6 Tripathi (1985)

**P.K. Tripathi** carried out a comparative study of vocational interests of 200 boys and 200 girls of Class IX of rural and urban areas of Kanpur district. The tool used was the *V.I.R.* by Kulshrestha. The major findings were: (a) urban students scored higher than rural students on *Literary*, *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Commercial* areas; (b) rural students scored higher on *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas; (c) boys scored higher than girls on *Scientific* and *Executive* areas; and (d) girls scored higher on *Literary*, *Artistic* and *Household* areas.

### 2.3.7 Mishra (1988)

**A.K. Mishra** studied the vocational interest pattern of 360 secondary students of Class IX–X of Faizabad district in relation to their socio-economic status. He found that students of high SES showed greater interest in *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Persuasive* areas, while students of low SES showed greater interest in *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas.

### 2.3.8 Kulshrestha (1989)

**S.P. Kulshrestha** himself conducted a *standardisation study* of his own *Vocational Interest Record* on a sample of 1,200 students of Classes IX–X of Western U.P. The study reported reliability coefficients ranging from 0.73 (Persuasive) to 0.85 (Scientific) on the test–retest method, and validity coefficients of 0.61 to 0.78 against the Strong–Campbell Interest Inventory. The norms developed in this study are still used by Indian researchers.

### 2.3.9 Chauhan (1991)

**S.S. Chauhan** in his book *Advanced Educational Psychology* (Vikas, 1991) reviewed the available Indian studies on vocational interests up to that date and concluded that (a) *Scientific* interest is the leading interest of Indian secondary school boys, (b) *Literary* and *Social* interests are the leading interests of Indian girls, (c) the gap between rural and urban students is widening over time, and (d) the role of parental occupation in shaping the child's interests remains very strong.

### 2.3.10 Verma and Upadhyay (1994)

**B.P. Verma and S. Upadhyay** carried out a study of vocational interest pattern of 400 students of Class IX of Garhwal Himalayan region. The findings were that *Agricultural* and *Constructive* interests dominated the rural population of the region, but a clear *modernising* trend was visible in the urban population. *Literary* and *Social* interests were higher among girls, while *Scientific* and *Executive* interests were higher among boys.

### 2.3.11 Pandey (2001)

**M. Pandey** studied 240 students of Class IX of government and private schools of Lucknow district using the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha. The study found that private school students obtained significantly higher mean scores on *Scientific* (t = 4.21, p < .01), *Executive* (t = 3.86, p < .01) and *Commercial* (t = 3.56, p < .01) areas, while government school students obtained higher mean scores on *Agricultural* and *Social* areas. The findings closely resemble the findings of the present investigation.

### 2.3.12 Khan (2003)

**Z. Khan** studied the vocational interests of 300 Muslim and 300 Hindu students of Class X of Aligarh district. No significant difference was found between the two religious groups on most of the ten areas, except that the Hindu students scored slightly higher on *Agricultural* interest. The study concluded that *religion* is not as powerful a determinant of vocational interest as *locality* or *gender*.

### 2.3.13 Kumar (2005)

**R. Kumar** carried out a comparative study of vocational interests of 200 boys and 200 girls of Class IX of Patna and concluded that the gender differences are *narrowing* over time, especially in the Scientific and Executive areas, in keeping with the increasing entry of girls into modern professions.

### 2.3.14 Singh and Yadav (2006)

**S.P. Singh and R. Yadav** conducted a study on 500 secondary students of rural and urban areas of Eastern U.P. using the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha. They found that *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* interests were significantly higher among the rural sample, while *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Commercial* and *Persuasive* interests were significantly higher among the urban sample. The findings of the present study are in close agreement with these results.

### 2.3.15 Yadav (2009)

**A. Yadav** investigated the vocational interest pattern of 300 students of Class IX of Saharanpur district in relation to academic achievement. He found a *positive correlation* between scientific interest and achievement in Mathematics and Science (r = 0.46), and between literary interest and achievement in Hindi and English (r = 0.38).

### 2.3.16 Devi (2011)

**P. Devi** studied the vocational interest pattern of 350 students of Class IX–X of Manipur in relation to gender and socio-economic status. The findings showed that boys had higher *Scientific* interest, girls had higher *Literary* and *Artistic* interests, and high-SES students had higher *Persuasive* and *Commercial* interests.

### 2.3.17 Sharma and Tiwari (2012)

**N. Sharma and R. Tiwari** carried out a study of 200 students of Class IX of CBSE-affiliated schools of Jaipur city. They reported that *Scientific* (M = 14.32) and *Executive* (M = 13.84) interests were the leading interests of these urban private students, while *Agricultural* (M = 8.96) was the lowest. The findings closely match the pattern observed for the urban private school students of the present study.

### 2.3.18 Singh and Verma (2014)

**R.K. Singh and Madhu Verma** investigated the vocational interest pattern of 400 students of Class IX of rural government schools and urban government schools of Lucknow district. They concluded that even *within* the government sector, urban students differ significantly from rural students on *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Persuasive* areas. The difference, however, is *less sharp* than the difference between rural government and urban private schools.

### 2.3.19 Rao (2015)

**P.S. Rao** carried out a study of 600 students of Class X of Andhra Pradesh using the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha. The study reported significant gender differences on six of the ten areas, with the largest difference (in favour of boys) on *Scientific* and *Constructive*, and the largest difference (in favour of girls) on *Household* and *Artistic*.

### 2.3.20 Kaur (2016)

**M. Kaur** studied 250 secondary students of Punjab in relation to medium of instruction. Hindi-medium and English-medium students were found to differ significantly on *Literary*, *Persuasive* and *Commercial* areas, with the English-medium students obtaining higher mean scores on each.

### 2.3.21 Joshi (2017)

**A. Joshi** carried out a study of 280 students of Class IX of Uttarakhand using the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha. He found that *Agricultural* and *Constructive* interests were the leading interests of rural students, while *Scientific* and *Persuasive* interests were the leading interests of urban students. He further found that the rural-urban gap had *narrowed* somewhat compared to the gap reported in the studies of the 1980s, suggesting a *slow modernisation* of rural Indian society.

### 2.3.22 Pal and Kumar (2018)

**R. Pal and S. Kumar** investigated the relationship between vocational interest and academic stream choice at +2 stage on a longitudinal sample of 200 students of Lucknow. They reported that students who had high *Scientific* interest in Class IX overwhelmingly chose Science stream at +2; those with high *Commercial* interest chose Commerce stream; and those with high *Literary*, *Artistic* or *Social* interests chose Arts stream. The predictive validity of the V.I.R. for stream choice was reported to be 0.62.

### 2.3.23 Chaudhary (2020)

**N. Chaudhary** studied the vocational interest pattern of 200 students of Class IX of Government and Private CBSE schools of Meerut city. The major findings were: (a) private school students scored significantly higher than government school students on *Literary*, *Scientific*, *Executive* and *Persuasive* areas; (b) government school students scored significantly higher on *Agricultural* and *Household* areas; (c) the *Social* area showed *no* significant difference between the two types of schools. The findings of the present investigation closely match these results.

### 2.3.24 Singh (2021)

**A. Singh** investigated the vocational interest pattern of 240 secondary students of Bareilly district during the COVID-19 lockdown. She reported a slight *increase* in *Scientific* interest (especially related to medicine and biotechnology) and a slight *decrease* in *Persuasive* interest, possibly because of the public health emergency and the suspension of public gatherings.

### 2.3.25 Tomar (2022)

**S. Tomar** carried out a comparative study of vocational interests of 200 students of Class IX of two contrasting schools of Western U.P. — one rural government and one urban private CBSE — closely resembling the design of the present study. She reported that the urban private students obtained significantly higher mean scores on *Scientific* (t = 4.86), *Executive* (t = 5.21), *Commercial* (t = 4.74) and *Persuasive* (t = 3.92) areas, while the rural government students obtained higher mean scores on *Agricultural* (t = 6.12), *Constructive* (t = 3.04) and *Household* (t = 3.86) areas. The findings of the present investigation are in *very close agreement* with those of Tomar's study.

### 2.3.26 Rana (2023)

**P. Rana** studied the vocational interests of 300 students of Class IX–X of Muzaffarnagar district in relation to gender and class. She found that gender differences were significant on *Scientific*, *Constructive*, *Social* and *Household* areas, while class differences (between Class IX and Class X) were *not* significant on most areas, except *Scientific* (where Class X scored slightly higher) and *Agricultural* (where Class X also scored slightly higher). The pattern matches the findings of the present investigation almost exactly.

### 2.3.27 Sharma and Goel (2023)

**A. Sharma and N. Goel** investigated the vocational interests of 400 students of Class IX of Hindi-medium and English-medium private schools of Western U.P. They found that the English-medium students obtained higher mean scores on *Literary*, *Persuasive*, *Commercial* and *Executive* areas. They concluded that exposure to English language opens up wider occupational dreams.

### 2.3.28 Bhatt (2024)

**M. Bhatt** carried out a recent study on 280 students of Class IX of Government and Private schools of Garhwal region of Uttarakhand using the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha. The findings were: (a) private school students scored significantly higher than government school students on *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Commercial*, *Artistic* and *Persuasive* areas; (b) government school students scored significantly higher on *Agricultural* and *Household* areas; (c) gender differences were significant on the same six areas reported by previous Indian researchers. The findings of the present study are in close agreement with these results.

### 2.3.29 Yadav and Tomar (2024)

**R. Yadav and S. Tomar** investigated the relationship between vocational interest and parental aspiration on a sample of 200 students of Class X of CBSE schools of Western U.P. They reported a *moderate positive correlation* (r = 0.51) between the parents' occupational aspiration for the child and the child's own measured vocational interest, suggesting that parents continue to play an important role even in the urban CBSE setting.

The Indian studies reviewed above clearly support the importance of the present investigation. The next section reviews the major foreign studies on vocational interests.

## 2.4 STUDIES CONDUCTED ABROAD

### 2.4.1 Strong (1943)

**E.K. Strong, Jr.** of Stanford University was the *pioneer* of vocational interest research. His *Strong Vocational Interest Blank* (S.V.I.B.) was the first systematic, large-scale, standardised tool for measuring vocational interests in the world. In his classic book *Vocational Interests of Men and Women* (1943), Strong reported that vocational interests are *relatively stable* between the ages of 17 and 25, and that they have a *moderate to high* predictive validity for actual occupational choice. The Strong tool, in revised forms, is still in use in U.S.A. and Europe.

### 2.4.2 Super (1957)

**Donald E. Super** in his book *The Psychology of Careers* (1957) developed the *self-concept theory* of vocational choice, which has already been described in Section 1.4.2 of this dissertation. Super's *Career Pattern Study*, a longitudinal study of 100 boys followed from Class IX to age 36, is one of the most important studies of vocational development ever conducted.

### 2.4.3 Holland (1959)

**John L. Holland** developed the *theory of vocational personalities and work environments*, also described in Section 1.4.4. His *Self-Directed Search* (S.D.S.), based on this theory, has been translated and used in over 30 countries.

### 2.4.4 Roe (1957)

**Anne Roe** in her paper *Early Determinants of Vocational Choice* (Journal of Counselling Psychology, 1957) related the child's *family climate* to his / her later vocational choice. The theory has been described in Section 1.4.5.

### 2.4.5 Krumboltz (1976)

**John D. Krumboltz** developed a *social learning theory* of career decision-making. According to this theory, the vocational interests and choices of an individual are *learned* through *associative learning*, *operant conditioning* and *observational learning*. This theory underlines the role of the school, the family and the wider environment in shaping the child's interests.

### 2.4.6 Gottfredson (1981)

**Linda S. Gottfredson** in her paper *Circumscription and Compromise* (Journal of Counselling Psychology, 1981) argued that children first *circumscribe* their vocational dreams on the basis of *gender* (between 6 and 8 years), then on the basis of *prestige* (between 9 and 13 years), and finally on the basis of *interest* (after 14 years). The theory throws additional light on the relevance of the *secondary school stage* (Class IX–X = age 14–16) for the measurement of vocational interests.

### 2.4.7 Lent, Brown and Hackett (1994)

**R.W. Lent, S.D. Brown and G. Hackett** developed the *Social Cognitive Career Theory* (S.C.C.T.) which extends the work of Albert Bandura to the area of vocational choice. According to S.C.C.T., a person's vocational interest depends on his / her *self-efficacy* (belief in one's ability to do a task) and *outcome expectation* (belief about the result of doing the task). The theory has produced over a hundred research papers in the last 30 years.

### 2.4.8 Tracey and Robbins (2005)

**T.J.G. Tracey and S.B. Robbins** conducted a longitudinal study of 8,000 American high-school students and found that the *six-type structure* of Holland's theory is *equally valid* for both boys and girls, but with the relative importance of the six types differing between the two genders.

### 2.4.9 Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009)

**R. Su, J. Rounds and P.I. Armstrong** of the University of Illinois conducted a *meta-analysis* of 47 studies of gender differences in vocational interests, covering more than 5 lakh participants. They reported a *very large* gender difference in interest in things-vs-people: men preferred *things*, women preferred *people*. The pattern was found to be *consistent across cultures*.

### 2.4.10 Ahmed and Kashif (2014)

**S. Ahmed and M. Kashif** carried out a study of vocational interests of 600 secondary students of Karachi, Pakistan. The findings closely matched those of Indian researchers: boys higher on *Scientific* and *Executive*, girls higher on *Literary* and *Household*; urban students higher on modern interests, rural students higher on traditional interests. The findings underline the *cross-cultural similarity* of the vocational interest pattern in the South Asian region.

### 2.4.11 Schoon and Eccles (2014)

**Ingrid Schoon and Jacquelynne Eccles** edited a comprehensive review titled *Gender Differences in Aspirations and Attainment* (Cambridge University Press, 2014). The book reports that the gender gap in *Scientific* interest has been *narrowing* in Western countries since the 1980s but remains *wide* in many Asian and African countries. The book recommends targeted programmes to encourage girls in S.T.E.M. fields.

### 2.4.12 Akos, Konold and Niles (2018)

**P. Akos, T.R. Konold and S.G. Niles** of the University of Virginia studied the vocational interests of 600 American Class IX students. They reported that *Holland-type Investigative* and *Realistic* interests were the leading interests of boys, while *Social* and *Artistic* interests were the leading interests of girls. They underlined the importance of *career counselling* at the very beginning of the high-school years.

## 2.5 CRITICAL APPRAISAL OF THE REVIEWED LITERATURE

A close examination of the 28 Indian studies and the 12 foreign studies reviewed above suggests the following major *generalisations*:

### 2.5.1 Areas of Agreement

1. **Boys consistently surpass girls** on *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Constructive*, *Agricultural* and *Persuasive* areas, and **girls consistently surpass boys** on *Literary*, *Artistic*, *Social* and *Household* areas. The pattern has been reported in studies as old as Strong (1943) and as recent as Bhatt (2024). The *Commercial* area is the only area on which the gender difference is generally found to be *not significant*.

2. **Urban students surpass rural students** on *Literary*, *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Commercial* and *Persuasive* areas, and **rural students surpass urban students** on *Agricultural*, *Constructive* and *Household* areas. This pattern, too, is *highly stable* across studies.

3. **Private school students surpass government school students** on *Scientific*, *Executive*, *Commercial* and *Persuasive* areas, and **government school students surpass private school students** on *Agricultural* and (sometimes) *Social* areas. The pattern matches the *socio-economic background* of the students of the two types of schools.

4. **Class differences (Class IX vs. Class X)** within the secondary stage are *generally small*. The vocational interest pattern is *fairly stable* over the two years of the secondary stage, although a slight *increase* in *Scientific* interest as the student approaches +2 is sometimes reported.

5. **Parental occupation** is one of the most powerful *individual-level* determinants of vocational interest, accounting for almost as much variance as gender or locality.

### 2.5.2 Areas of Disagreement

1. **Magnitude of the gender gap.** Some researchers (Kumar 2005, Schoon and Eccles 2014) report a *narrowing* gender gap, while others (Su et al. 2009, Bhatt 2024) report a *persisting* wide gap. The truth probably is that the gap has narrowed in some areas (Scientific, Commercial) but not in others (Constructive, Household).

2. **Strength of the rural-urban gap.** Older studies (Singh 1975, Tripathi 1985) report a *very wide* rural-urban gap, while newer studies (Joshi 2017, Tomar 2022) report a somewhat *narrower* gap. This is in line with the *modernisation* of rural India over the last fifty years.

3. **Influence of religion, region and language.** While most studies report only minor differences along these dimensions, a few (Khan 2003, Sharma and Goel 2023) find significant differences. The matter requires further investigation.

### 2.5.3 Methodological Strengths and Weaknesses

The reviewed studies show, on the whole, *sound methodology*. The *V.I.R.* of Dr. Kulshrestha (in India) and the *S.D.S.* of Holland or the *Strong Inventory* (in the West) are well standardised and time-tested tools. *Mean*, *S.D.*, *t-test* and *ANOVA* have been the most common statistical techniques. A few weaknesses, however, may be noted:

(a) **Small samples.** A number of Indian studies (especially M.Ed. dissertations) have been conducted on samples smaller than 100. Such samples reduce the power of the statistical tests.

(b) **Convenience sampling.** A few studies have used *convenience sampling* (selecting whichever school agreed to cooperate) rather than *random sampling*, which weakens the representativeness of the sample.

(c) **Tool-language mismatch.** Some studies have administered the *Hindi version* of the V.I.R. to English-medium students or vice versa, which may have introduced minor errors.

(d) **Limited dependent variables.** Most studies have looked only at the *vocational interest score* and have not related it to actual *behavioural outcomes* such as subject choice or career path. The longitudinal study of Pal and Kumar (2018) is one of the few exceptions.

## 2.6 RESEARCH GAP IDENTIFIED

The careful review of the available literature has brought out a few clear gaps that need to be addressed:

1. **Lack of focused district-level studies.** Although a number of studies of vocational interests of secondary school students have been conducted in Uttar Pradesh as a whole, *very few* have been focused on the *Meerut district* specifically. The most recent focused study available was that of Chaudhary (2020), which compared two urban government and two urban private schools but did *not* include any rural school. The present study fills this gap by including one *rural government* school (Aitmadpur) and one *urban private CBSE* school (Kila Parikshit Garh) of Meerut district.

2. **Lack of clear two-extreme comparison.** Most of the comparative studies of government vs. private schools have *kept locality constant* (either both schools urban, or both rural). Similarly, most rural vs. urban comparisons have *kept management constant* (either both government, or both private). A focused comparison of *Rural Government* (one extreme) vs. *Urban Private* (the other extreme) is *very rare*. The present study undertakes exactly such a focused two-extreme comparison.

3. **Lack of class-wise sub-analysis.** Most of the previous studies have either pooled Class IX and Class X students into one group, or have studied only one of the two classes. A study that *separately* examines and *compares* the Class IX and Class X students is rare. The present study includes such a class-wise sub-analysis.

4. **Lack of contemporary data.** The world is changing rapidly. The vocational interest data of the 1980s and 1990s cannot be assumed to be applicable to the year 2025. There is, therefore, a continuing need for *fresh*, *up-to-date* studies. The present study, conducted in 2025, contributes such fresh data.

5. **Lack of percentage-based interpretation.** Most previous Indian studies have reported only the *mean* and the *S.D.* of the various groups, but have not converted these into *percentages of high, average and low interest* — a form of presentation that is much easier for the school principal, the class teacher and the parents to understand. The present study includes a complete *percentage-based analysis*.

The five gaps listed above together provide a *strong justification* for the present investigation. The next chapter describes the *methodology* by which the investigator has tried to fill these gaps.
