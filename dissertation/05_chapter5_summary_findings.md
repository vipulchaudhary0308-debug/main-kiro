## 5.1 Summary of the Study

The present dissertation is a systematic empirical investigation into the vocational interests of secondary school students, with a specific focus on gender-based and school-type-based comparisons. The study was undertaken in partial fulfilment of the requirements for the degree of Master of Education (M.Ed.) at Meerut College, Meerut, affiliated to Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut, under the supervision of Dr. Seema Sharma.

### 5.1.1 Background and Rationale

The period of secondary schooling — encompassing Classes IX and X — is a developmentally critical phase during which adolescents begin to form clearer vocational identities. In the Indian educational context, the choices students make at this stage often determine their stream selection at Class XI (Science, Commerce, or Arts), which in turn shapes their career trajectories for decades to come. Despite the enormous significance of these decisions, most Indian secondary schools — particularly in rural and semi-urban areas — offer little or no systematic vocational guidance. Students are left to navigate their career choices on the basis of family tradition, social pressure, peer influence, or simple lack of awareness about the full range of occupational possibilities available to them.

Against this backdrop, the present study set out to examine and compare the vocational interests of boys and girls studying at the secondary level in two schools in the Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut district — **Shri Sanskrit Inter College** (a rural government school affiliated to UPMSP) and **KP International School** (an urban private CBSE-affiliated school). The study also compared students from these two school types — rural government versus urban private — to understand how school environment shapes vocational interest profiles.

### 5.1.2 Objectives

The study was guided by the following objectives:
1. To assess the vocational interests of boys and girls at the secondary level.
2. To compare boys and girls on each of the nine vocational interest areas.
3. To compare rural government and urban private school students on each area.
4. To identify dominant and least preferred vocational areas for boys and girls separately.
5. To examine how school type influences vocational interest patterns.
6. To draw educational implications and offer practical suggestions.

### 5.1.3 Hypotheses

Eleven null hypotheses were tested — nine comparing boys and girls on each VIR subscale, and two comparing rural vs. urban students overall and by gender subgroup.

### 5.1.4 Method and Sample

The **Descriptive Survey Method** was employed. A sample of **60 students** — 30 boys (15 rural, 15 urban) and 30 girls (15 rural, 15 urban) — was selected through **Random Sampling** from the two participating schools. Data were collected in November 2024 through personally administered sessions conducted by the researcher at both schools.

### 5.1.5 Tool

The **S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record (VIR)** — a standardised, 90-item, three-point scale instrument measuring nine vocational interest areas (Literary, Scientific, Technical, Artistic, Commercial, Executive, Agricultural, Household, and Social) — was used as the data collection tool. Scores on each subscale ranged from 0 to 20.

### 5.1.6 Statistical Techniques

The data were analysed using:
- **Mean** and **Standard Deviation** for descriptive analysis
- **Independent Samples t-test** (df = 58) for inferential analysis
- **Percentage Analysis** for categorical distribution

## 5.2 Major Findings

The analysis of data yielded the following major findings, organized by the nature of the comparison:

### 5.2.1 Gender-wise Findings

**Finding 1**: Boys scored significantly higher than girls on **Technical Interest** (Boys mean = 16.47, Girls mean = 8.60; t = 24.59, p < 0.01). The null hypothesis H₀₃ was **rejected**. This was the largest gender difference in the entire study. 93.3% of boys achieved high interest scores in Technical areas, compared to 0% of girls.

**Finding 2**: Girls scored significantly higher than girls on **Household Interest** (Girls mean = 14.40, Boys mean = 5.57; t = 20.53, p < 0.01). The null hypothesis H₀₈ was **rejected**. This was the second largest gender difference. 86.7% of girls achieved high interest scores, compared to 0% of boys.

**Finding 3**: Girls scored significantly higher on **Social Interest** (Girls mean = 16.20, Boys mean = 12.87; t = 11.10, p < 0.01). H₀₉ was **rejected**. Social interest was the single highest-scoring area for girls overall, with 96.7% of girls achieving high interest scores.

**Finding 4**: Girls scored significantly higher on **Artistic Interest** (Girls mean = 15.80, Boys mean = 10.27; t = 13.17, p < 0.01). H₀₄ was **rejected**. Artistic interest was the second highest area for girls, with 90% achieving high scores.

**Finding 5**: Girls scored significantly higher on **Literary Interest** (Girls mean = 15.10, Boys mean = 12.17; t = 9.45, p < 0.01). H₀₁ was **rejected**.

**Finding 6**: Boys scored significantly higher on **Scientific Interest** (Boys mean = 15.13, Girls mean = 11.67; t = 9.13, p < 0.01). H₀₂ was **rejected**.

**Finding 7**: Boys scored significantly higher on **Agricultural Interest** (Boys mean = 13.67, Girls mean = 11.10; t = 3.52, p < 0.01). H₀₇ was **rejected**.

**Finding 8**: No significant gender difference was found in **Commercial Interest** (Boys mean = 12.20, Girls mean = 11.60; t = 1.62, p > 0.05). H₀₅ was **retained**.

**Finding 9**: No significant gender difference was found in **Executive Interest** (Boys mean = 12.87, Girls mean = 12.60; t = 0.61, p > 0.05). H₀₆ was **retained**.

### 5.2.2 Rural vs. Urban Findings

**Finding 10**: Rural students scored significantly higher than urban students on **Agricultural Interest** (Rural mean = 15.17, Urban mean = 9.60; t = 12.95, p < 0.01). This was the most dramatic rural-urban difference in the data.

**Finding 11**: Urban students scored significantly higher than rural students on **Executive Interest** (Urban mean = 14.60, Rural mean = 10.87; t = 13.32, p < 0.01) and **Commercial Interest** (Urban mean = 13.60, Rural mean = 10.20; t = 11.33, p < 0.01).

**Finding 12**: Urban students scored significantly higher on **Artistic Interest** (t = 3.33, p < 0.01), **Scientific Interest** (t = 2.92, p < 0.01), **Social Interest** (t = 3.56, p < 0.01), and **Literary Interest** (t = 2.02, p < 0.05).

**Finding 13**: No significant rural-urban difference was found in **Technical Interest** (t = 1.56, p > 0.05) or **Household Interest** (t = 1.39, p > 0.05).

**Finding 14**: H₀₁₀ was **rejected** — the overall vocational interest profiles of rural and urban students are significantly different across multiple areas.

### 5.2.3 Rural Boys vs. Urban Boys

**Finding 15**: Urban boys scored significantly higher than rural boys on **Commercial** (t = 5.10), **Executive** (t = 5.52), **Artistic** (t = 4.57), and **Social** (t = 3.40) interests. Rural boys scored dramatically higher on **Agricultural** interest (t = 5.89). H₀₁₁ was **rejected** for most areas.

### 5.2.4 Dominant Interest Areas

**For Boys (all)**: Technical (16.47) > Agricultural (13.67) > Scientific (15.13) [top three]
**For Girls (all)**: Social (16.20) > Artistic (15.80) > Literary (15.10) [top three]
**For Rural Students**: Agricultural (15.17) > Social (13.77) > Literary (13.17) [top three]
**For Urban Students**: Executive (14.60) > Social (15.30) > Artistic (14.30) [top three]

## 5.3 Educational Implications

The findings of this study carry significant implications for educational policy, school practice, and vocational guidance at the secondary level. These implications are discussed below under relevant headings.

### 5.3.1 Gender Stereotyping and Career Limitation

The dramatic gender differences observed in this study — particularly the complete absence of high Technical interest among girls and the complete absence of high Household interest among boys — are not merely statistical findings. They represent the tangible outcome of years of gender-differentiated socialization that begins long before secondary school. By the time children reach Classes IX and X, their vocational horizons have already been significantly narrowed along gender lines — a process precisely described by Gottfredson (1981) as "circumscription."

The educational implication is clear and urgent: schools must actively work to **expand students' awareness of non-traditional career options** through structured career exploration activities, career talks by professionals from diverse gender backgrounds, and conscious efforts by teachers to avoid reinforcing gender stereotypes in classroom discussions about careers and future plans.

### 5.3.2 Rural-Urban Educational Disparity

The finding that rural students score significantly lower on Commercial and Executive interests — and dramatically higher only on Agricultural interest — reflects a genuine educational disparity. Rural students are not less interested in diverse careers because they are less capable; they are less aware of diverse careers because they live and study in environments where the occupational landscape is far narrower. The implication is that **career information programmes must be specifically designed and targeted at rural government schools**, where students are most in need of exposure to the full range of modern occupational possibilities.

### 5.3.3 Vocational Guidance at Class IX Level

The timing of this study — at Class IX and X — is educationally significant. Students at this level are approximately one to two years away from the crucial stream selection decision (Science, Commerce, or Arts) that will shape their higher education and career options. The findings of this study argue strongly for **systematic vocational interest assessment at the Class IX level**, so that students can make stream selection decisions informed by their own interest profiles rather than by external pressure alone.

### 5.3.4 NEP 2020 Implementation

The National Education Policy 2020 envisions early and comprehensive exposure to vocational education for all students. The findings of this study demonstrate that, as of the present academic session, this vision remains far from realised in the schools studied — particularly the rural government school. The policy implication is that **NEP 2020 implementation must prioritise vocational guidance infrastructure in rural and semi-urban government schools**.

### 5.3.5 Gender-Sensitive Curriculum

The convergence of Artistic, Social, and Literary interests among girls, and Technical, Scientific, and Agricultural interests among boys, suggests that the current curriculum and extracurricular programme may be inadvertently reinforcing these patterns rather than broadening them. Schools should examine their implicit curriculum for gender-biased messages and **design activities that expose all students to a wider range of vocational domains** — for example, encouraging boys to engage in social service activities and encouraging girls to participate in science fairs and technology workshops.

## 5.4 Conclusions

On the basis of the data analysis and the major findings presented above, the following conclusions are drawn:

**Conclusion 1**: Gender is a highly significant determinant of vocational interest patterns among secondary school students in the Meerut district. Boys and girls show distinctly different — and in some areas dramatically different — vocational interest profiles.

**Conclusion 2**: The most pronounced gender differences are in Technical interest (overwhelmingly male-dominated) and Household interest (overwhelmingly female-dominated), reflecting deep-rooted cultural norms around gender and occupational activity.

**Conclusion 3**: Girls show the highest vocational interest in Social, Artistic, and Literary domains, while boys show the highest interest in Technical, Scientific, and Agricultural domains. These patterns are consistent with findings from across India and internationally.

**Conclusion 4**: Commercial and Executive interests are not significantly differentiated by gender at the secondary school level — a finding that suggests these emerging career domains may represent shared aspirational ground for both boys and girls in contemporary India.

**Conclusion 5**: School type (rural government vs. urban private) significantly influences vocational interest profiles. Urban private school students show broader, more diverse interest profiles, while rural government school students show a pronounced and distinctive orientation towards agricultural interests.

**Conclusion 6**: Rural students are significantly disadvantaged in terms of exposure to Commercial and Executive career domains — a disadvantage that can only be addressed through targeted vocational guidance and career information programmes in rural schools.

**Conclusion 7**: The vocational interest profiles observed in this study are consistent with the theoretical frameworks of Super (exploration stage), Holland (RIASEC types), Gottfredson (circumscription and compromise), and Lent et al. (SCCT) — all of which predict gender-differentiated and environment-shaped vocational interest development during adolescence.

**Conclusion 8**: The S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record continues to be a reliable and useful instrument for assessing vocational interests of secondary school students in the Indian context, yielding psychometrically meaningful and educationally interpretable results.

## 5.5 Suggestions

Based on the findings, conclusions, and educational implications of this study, the following suggestions are offered for various stakeholders in the educational system:

### 5.5.1 Suggestions for Teachers

1. **Integrate vocational awareness into regular teaching**: Teachers of all subjects should consciously draw connections between their subject and various career possibilities — not just the conventional ones. A Chemistry teacher, for example, should mention careers in pharmaceuticals, food technology, and environmental science, not only medicine and engineering.

2. **Avoid gender-stereotyped career messaging**: Teachers should be mindful of the messages they send — explicitly or implicitly — about which careers are "suitable" for boys and which for girls. Such stereotyped messages significantly constrain students' vocational imagination.

3. **Conduct classroom vocational discussions**: Regular structured discussions about careers, occupational roles, and workplace realities help students develop a more sophisticated and realistic understanding of the world of work.

4. **Use standardised vocational tools**: Teachers trained in guidance should administer the Kulshrestha VIR or similar instruments at the Class IX level and use the results to guide individual career counselling conversations.

### 5.5.2 Suggestions for Parents

1. **Expose children to diverse occupational models**: Parents should make conscious efforts to expose their children — particularly daughters — to successful role models from non-traditional career fields. This might involve family visits, conversations about various professions, or shared media content.

2. **Respect your child's vocational interests**: Parents should resist the temptation to impose career choices based on family tradition, social prestige, or financial considerations alone. A child whose vocational interests are respected is far more likely to find lasting career satisfaction.

3. **Support girls' interest in science and technology**: Parents of girls should actively encourage participation in science and mathematics, challenge limiting beliefs about girls' technical abilities, and provide resources (books, toys, activities) that support scientific and technical exploration.

4. **Discuss agriculture as a modern career**: For rural families, agriculture should be discussed not only as a traditional livelihood but as a modern, scientifically sophisticated, and potentially very rewarding career. Agricultural science, agribusiness, and food technology are growing fields with excellent prospects.

### 5.5.3 Suggestions for Schools

1. **Establish a Vocational Guidance Cell**: Every secondary school, regardless of its rural or urban location or government/private status, should establish a functioning Vocational Guidance Cell staffed by a trained guidance counsellor.

2. **Conduct Career Fairs and Vocational Exhibitions**: Annual events featuring professionals from diverse career fields — including non-traditional options — can significantly broaden students' occupational horizons.

3. **Provide career information resources**: Schools should maintain an updated career library with booklets, posters, and digital resources covering the full range of vocational areas, including those less familiar to students from rural backgrounds.

4. **Train teachers in basic guidance skills**: In the absence of professional counsellors (particularly in rural government schools), classroom teachers should receive basic training in vocational guidance so they can provide a minimum level of career support to their students.

5. **Administer vocational interest inventories systematically**: The VIR or similar instruments should be administered to all Class IX students as a routine part of the school's guidance programme, with results shared and discussed with each student individually.

### 5.5.4 Suggestions for Guidance Counsellors

1. **Use gender-sensitive counselling approaches**: When using VIR results in counselling, guidance counsellors should be careful not to confirm gender stereotypes. A girl who scores high on Technical interest should be actively encouraged and guided towards technical career options — not steered back towards more "conventional" female domains.

2. **Address rural students' limited occupational awareness**: Counsellors working in rural schools should begin career guidance conversations by expanding students' occupational awareness before administering vocational interest inventories — students cannot express interest in careers they have never heard of.

3. **Involve parents in guidance conversations**: Given the strong parental influence on career choices in Indian culture, counsellors should proactively engage parents in career guidance conversations, helping them understand the importance of their child's vocational interests.

4. **Follow up and track outcomes**: Guidance counsellors should track what happens to students who received career counselling — whether they chose streams and careers aligned with their interests, and whether they report satisfaction. Such follow-up data can improve the quality of future counselling practice.

### 5.5.5 Suggestions for Policymakers

1. **Mandatory vocational guidance in government secondary schools**: The government should mandate the appointment of trained guidance counsellors in all secondary schools, with particular priority for rural government schools where the need is greatest.

2. **Update NEP 2020 implementation timelines**: The NEP 2020 targets for vocational education should be supplemented with specific provisions for vocational guidance and interest assessment, not only vocational skills training.

3. **Invest in gender-sensitisation programmes**: Government-sponsored gender-sensitisation programmes in schools should explicitly address the narrowing of vocational horizons caused by gender stereotyping, particularly for girls in rural areas.

4. **Support research on vocational interests**: The government and NCERT should fund regular, nationally representative surveys of vocational interests among Indian secondary school students to track changes over time and inform educational policy.

### 5.5.6 Suggestions for Future Researchers

1. **Longitudinal studies**: Future researchers should conduct longitudinal studies tracking the vocational interests of the same students from Class VI through Class XII to understand how interests develop and change over the secondary school years.

2. **Larger and more diverse samples**: Future studies should use larger samples from multiple districts and states to improve the generalizability of findings.

3. **Mixed-methods approaches**: Qualitative methods (interviews, focus groups) should be combined with quantitative assessment to understand the experiential and sociocultural mechanisms through which gender and school-type differences in vocational interests are produced.

4. **Parent and teacher surveys**: Future research should investigate the attitudes and beliefs of parents and teachers regarding gender-appropriate careers, and examine how these attitudes influence students' vocational interest development.

5. **Effect of intervention programmes**: Experimental or quasi-experimental studies should evaluate the effectiveness of specific vocational guidance interventions — career fairs, counselling programmes, role model visits — in broadening and diversifying the vocational interest profiles of secondary school students.

6. **Caste and socioeconomic variables**: Future researchers in the Meerut district context should include caste and socioeconomic status as additional independent variables, as prior research (Singh, 2013; Verma & Gupta, 2016) suggests these factors interact significantly with gender and school type in shaping vocational interests.

## 5.6 Limitations of the Study

Every research study operates within certain practical constraints that limit the scope and generalizability of its findings. The following limitations of the present study are honestly acknowledged:

1. **Small sample size**: The sample of 60 students from two schools, while adequate for a Master's level dissertation, is too small to support broad generalization to the entire population of secondary school students in Meerut district or Uttar Pradesh.

2. **Only two schools**: The study covers only one rural government school and one urban private school. Different schools — even within the same category — may yield different results.

3. **Self-report data**: The VIR, like all self-report instruments, is subject to response biases such as social desirability, acquiescence, or careless responding. While the researcher took steps to minimize these biases (assurances of confidentiality, relaxed atmosphere), they cannot be entirely eliminated.

4. **Cross-sectional design**: The study provides a snapshot of vocational interests at a single point in time. It cannot tell us how interests have developed over time or how they may change in the future.

5. **Limited to nine interest areas**: The Kulshrestha VIR covers nine vocational areas. Newer and emerging career domains — such as information technology, digital media, environmental sciences, and healthcare management — are not fully captured by the nine-area framework.

6. **Influence of current events**: Data were collected during November 2024. Current events, recent media exposure, or school-specific activities at around that time may have temporarily influenced students' responses in ways that do not reflect their stable, long-term interests.

## 5.7 Final Conclusion

The present study set out to compare the vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls from a rural government school and an urban private CBSE school in the Meerut district. The findings are clear, consistent, and educationally significant.

Gender shapes vocational interests in profound and pervasive ways. Boys are drawn powerfully towards technical, scientific, and agricultural domains, while girls show strong orientations towards social, artistic, and literary domains. These patterns are not random — they are the product of years of socialization that assigns different occupational imaginaries to boys and girls from a very early age.

School type too leaves a clear imprint on vocational interest profiles. Urban private school students, exposed to a richer and more diverse educational and occupational environment, develop broader and more varied vocational interests. Rural government school students, whose occupational world is more narrowly defined by agriculture and traditional trades, show a corresponding narrowing of vocational aspirations — with the notable exception of their genuine and intense agricultural interest, which deserves to be valued and nurtured rather than dismissed.

The most urgent message of this study is that **both gender stereotyping and rural-urban educational disparity are acting as significant constraints on the vocational development of Indian adolescents**. These constraints are not inevitable — they can be challenged and reduced through informed educational policy, skilled vocational guidance, and the committed efforts of teachers, parents, and counsellors who take seriously their responsibility for the career development of young people.

The researcher hopes that the findings of this modest study will contribute, in however small a way, to the ongoing effort to build an Indian education system that is truly equitable, truly empowering, and truly responsive to the diverse dreams and potentials of every young person — boy or girl, rural or urban, rich or poor.
