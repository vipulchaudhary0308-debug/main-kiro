# CHAPTER – V

# SUMMARY, FINDINGS, CONCLUSIONS AND SUGGESTIONS

## 5.1 Summary of the Study

The present chapter brings the dissertation to a close by drawing together, in a compact form, the entire argument of the four preceding chapters and indicating the *practical*, *educational* and *research* implications of the findings.

### 5.1.1 The Problem

The investigation has been entitled *"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"*, and was conducted in *Meerut District* (Uttar Pradesh) under the affiliation of *Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut*, during the academic session 2025–2026. The Secondary School stage in India coincides with the developmental period of *early adolescence*, in which the individual begins to crystallise his or her vocational choices. The choices made at this stage have far-reaching consequences for the academic future of the learner, his or her later occupational adjustment, life satisfaction and contribution to society. An empirical enquiry into the comparative vocational interests of secondary school *boys* and *girls* in a real Indian setting is therefore both timely and educationally significant.

### 5.1.2 Objectives

The investigation was designed to fulfil five major objectives:

i. To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and S.D.) of secondary school *boys* on the ten areas of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record*.
ii. To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and S.D.) of secondary school *girls* on the ten areas of the Record.
iii. To compare the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls on each of the ten areas by means of the *t*-test.
iv. To compute the percentage of boys and girls falling at the high, average and low levels of vocational interest in each of the ten areas.
v. To determine the rank order of the ten areas of vocational interest for boys and for girls and to examine the agreement between the two rank orders.

### 5.1.3 Hypotheses

In keeping with the above objectives, three null hypotheses were formulated:

**H₀1.** There is no significant difference between the mean scores of boys and girls of secondary level on any of the ten areas of vocational interest.

**H₀2.** Boys and girls do not differ significantly in the percentage distribution of their interest at the high, average and low levels.

**H₀3.** Boys and girls do not differ significantly in the rank order in which they place the ten areas of vocational interest.

### 5.1.4 Method, Sample, Tool and Statistics

The investigation employed the *descriptive survey method*. A total of **40 students** of Class IX and X were selected through *simple random sampling* (lottery method) — *20 boys* from *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut* (Government rural, U.P. Board) and *20 girls* from *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut* (Private urban, CBSE). The two schools were *purposively* selected to represent maximally contrasting environments. The principal tool was *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)*, a standardised forced-choice instrument of 90 items measuring interest in ten areas — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household. *Mean*, *Standard Deviation*, the *t*-test for two independent means (df = 38) and *Percentage Analysis* were the principal statistical techniques; *Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficient* was computed for the rank-order analysis (Objective 5).

## 5.2 Major Findings of the Study

The major findings of the investigation, drawn from the analysis presented in Chapter IV, are listed below.

### 5.2.1 Findings related to Objectives 1 and 2 (Descriptive Statistics)

1. The mean Vocational Interest scores of the *boys* on the ten areas, in descending order, were: Constructive (11.90), Scientific (11.65), Agricultural (10.35), Persuasive (9.60), Executive (9.50), Commercial (8.85), Social (7.75), Artistic (7.55), Literary (7.05) and Household (5.80).

2. The mean Vocational Interest scores of the *girls* on the ten areas, in descending order, were: Household (12.10), Artistic (11.00), Social (10.55), Literary (9.85), Persuasive (8.65), Executive (8.05), Commercial (8.00), Scientific (7.95), Constructive (7.05) and Agricultural (6.70).

3. The standard deviations were uniformly moderate (range 2.00 – 2.65 for boys and 2.05 – 2.65 for girls), indicating *homogeneous* distributions within each group.

### 5.2.2 Findings related to Objective 3 / H₀1 (Mean-Difference Comparison)

4. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the **Scientific** area (t = 4.54, p < 0.01).
5. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the **Constructive (Technical)** area (t = 5.96, p < 0.01).
6. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the **Agricultural** area (t = 4.48, p < 0.01).
7. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the **Literary** area (t = 4.02, p < 0.01).
8. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the **Artistic** area (t = 5.07, p < 0.01).
9. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the **Social** area (t = 3.93, p < 0.01).
10. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the **Household** area (t = 9.36, p < 0.01) — the largest difference observed in any of the ten comparisons.
11. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the **Executive** area (t = 1.91, NS).
12. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the **Commercial** area (t = 1.17, NS).
13. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the **Persuasive** area (t = 1.31, NS).
14. The null hypothesis *H₀1* was therefore *partly rejected* and *partly retained* — rejected for the seven gender-differentiated areas (Literary, Scientific, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Social, Household) and retained for the three gender-neutral areas (Executive, Commercial, Persuasive).



### 5.2.3 Findings related to Objective 4 / H₀2 (Percentage Distribution)

15. On the Constructive area, *60 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of girls* — a gap of 50 percentage points in favour of boys.
16. On the Scientific area, *55 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of girls* — a gap of 45 percentage points in favour of boys.
17. On the Agricultural area, *40 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *5 % of girls* — a gap of 35 percentage points in favour of boys.
18. On the Household area, *60 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *5 % of boys* — a gap of 55 percentage points in favour of girls (the largest single gap in the study).
19. On the Artistic area, *45 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *15 % of boys* — a gap of 30 percentage points in favour of girls.
20. On the Social area, *40 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *15 % of boys* — a gap of 25 percentage points in favour of girls.
21. On the Literary area, *30 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of boys* — a gap of 20 percentage points in favour of girls.
22. On the Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas, the percentages of boys and girls at the High level differed by less than 20 percentage points (10 – 20 %), all in favour of boys.
23. The null hypothesis *H₀2* was therefore *partly rejected* (for areas with gaps ≥ 20 %) and *partly retained* (for areas with smaller gaps).

### 5.2.4 Findings related to Objective 5 / H₀3 (Rank-Order Analysis)

24. The boys' top three preferred areas (Constructive, Scientific, Agricultural) coincided almost exactly with the girls' bottom three preferred areas (Constructive — 9, Scientific — 8, Agricultural — 10).
25. The girls' top three preferred areas (Household, Artistic, Social) coincided closely with the boys' bottom four preferred areas (Household — 10, Artistic — 8, Social — 7).
26. The Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas, on which the *t*-test showed *no* gender difference, occupied very similar middle ranks for both groups (Executive — 5/6, Commercial — 6/7, Persuasive — 4/5).
27. The *Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ)* between the boys' and the girls' rank orders was *−0.88*, statistically significant at the 0.01 level (n = 10, ρ₀.₀₁ = ±0.794) — the rank orders are virtually *mirror-images* of each other.
28. The null hypothesis *H₀3* was therefore *strongly rejected*; the rank orders are not merely different — they are *systematically reversed*.

## 5.3 Conclusions

In the light of the empirical evidence presented in Chapter IV and summarised in Section 5.2, the following major *conclusions* may be drawn:

**Conclusion 1.** The vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls of the Meerut District are *strongly, systematically and statistically significantly* gender-typed. The two groups occupy *different and largely opposed* vocational worlds, with very limited overlap in the High-level zone of the ten interest areas.

**Conclusion 2.** Boys are significantly higher than girls on the *Scientific*, *Constructive (Technical)* and *Agricultural* areas — the *Constructive–Scientific–Agricultural triad* representing the *traditional masculine occupational image* of Indian rural society, reinforced both by the family background of the rural-Government-school boys and by prevailing social expectations.

**Conclusion 3.** Girls are significantly higher than boys on the *Literary, Artistic, Social* and *Household* areas — the *Household–Artistic–Social–Literary set* representing the *traditional feminine occupational image* of Indian society, reinforced both by family modelling and by prevailing social expectations, even when (as in the K.P. International School sample) the family is highly educated and economically well-placed.

**Conclusion 4.** The *Executive*, *Commercial* and *Persuasive* areas have emerged as *gender-neutral* in the present sample. The historic male advantage on managerial, financial and persuasive occupations has narrowed to insignificance, suggesting that contemporary Indian girls — at least those of the urban-Private-CBSE stream — are claiming an equal stake in administration, business and marketing-related careers.

**Conclusion 5.** The very high *negative* Spearman rank-order correlation (ρ = −0.88) is, perhaps, the *most striking single statistical finding* of the present study. It shows not merely that the two groups differ, but that the *very ordering* of their interests is *reversed*. Boys' favourite vocations are nearly girls' least preferred ones, and *vice versa*.

**Conclusion 6.** The convergence of the three statistical lenses — *t*-test, percentage analysis and rank-order analysis — strengthens the substantive interpretation. Each lens highlights the same essential pattern from a slightly different angle, and the agreement among them strengthens the *internal validity* of the conclusions.

**Conclusion 7.** Although the present sample is small (N = 40), the consistency of the findings with those reported by Singh and Verma (2023), Reddy and Kumar (2021), Joshi (2020), Mehta (2019), Yadav (2016) and the Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) meta-analysis lends *external validity* to the conclusions. The present study, in spite of its modest scope, contributes a *small replication* of the broader gender-typed pattern in the contemporary Meerut context.

## 5.4 Educational Implications

The findings carry several important *educational implications* for the Indian secondary school:

**Implication 1 — Recognition of the gender-typed reality.** Schools, parents and policy-makers must *recognise* that the vocational interests of contemporary Indian adolescents continue to be sharply gender-typed. The bland claim that "boys and girls have equal opportunities now" is empirically untrue at the level of *interest formation*; equal opportunity must be *consciously worked for*.

**Implication 2 — Need for organised career counselling at the secondary stage.** Both schools sampled lack a regular, full-time, trained career counsellor. The U.P. Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad, the C.B.S.E. and the Department of Secondary Education must seriously revisit the recommendations of the Kothari Commission and the National Education Policy 2020 on this point.

**Implication 3 — Vocational interest measurement as part of the school programme.** Standardised tools such as Kulshrestha's V.I.R. should be routinely administered at the end of Class IX and the results discussed individually with each learner and his or her parents to inform the Class XI stream choice.

**Implication 4 — Exposure to non-traditional vocations.** Adolescent girls, especially those of urban Private CBSE schools, must be given systematic exposure to *Scientific, Constructive (Technical) and Agricultural* careers — through school exhibitions, factory visits and exposure to women engineers, scientists, entrepreneurs and agricultural scientists. The 50-percentage-point gap on Constructive interest cannot be allowed to harden into an *adult* gender gap on engineering enrolment.

**Implication 5 — Encouraging boys towards "feminine" vocations.** Conversely, adolescent boys, especially of rural Government schools, must be exposed to *Literary, Artistic, Social and Household* vocations — through visits to teaching institutions, hospitals, art studios, hospitality industries and the homes of well-known male chefs, designers and care-givers. The fact that *only 5 % of the boys* reach the High level on Household — and *50 % fall at the Low level* — indicates a near-total devaluation of household work as a "boy's" career.

**Implication 6 — Sensitisation of teachers, parents and administrators.** Teachers and parents must consciously *avoid* the casual remarks and the routine practices that perpetuate gender-typed expectations. Teacher-training and parent-education programmes should make this a deliberate focus.

**Implication 7 — Diversification of the secondary curriculum.** The provisions of the National Education Policy 2020 for *vocational education* in middle and secondary classes must be implemented in spirit, with equal access of boys and girls to *all* the vocational crafts — cooking and embroidery as much as electrical wiring and motor mechanics.

**Implication 8 — Strengthening the gender-neutral zone.** The fact that Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas are gender-neutral in the present sample is encouraging. The Indian school can build on this by deliberately providing girls with leadership roles, administrative responsibilities (head girl, club secretaries, prefects) and business-skill exposure.

**Implication 9 — Use of the findings in stream allocation.** Class IX results on the V.I.R. should be used as one of the inputs for the Class XI stream allocation; where measured interest and stream choice are mismatched (as Pandey and Tripathi (2022) found in 34 % of cases), counselling should be undertaken.

**Implication 10 — Linking vocational interest with skill development.** At the school level, the *Skill India* and *Atal Tinkering Lab* programmes must be re-designed to involve girls actively, especially in areas like robotics, electronics and 3-D printing, where the gender gap in interest is currently widest.

**Implication 11 — Need for region-specific empirical baselines.** Local empirical studies of the present type — even at the M.Ed. level — provide useful baselines for district-level career-guidance planning.

**Implication 12 — Inclusion in B.Ed. and M.Ed. curricula.** The course "*Guidance and Counselling*" should include modules on the *systematic measurement of vocational interest* and on the *interpretation* of the typical gender-typed pattern, without which future teachers and counsellors cannot do justice to contemporary career guidance.



## 5.5 Suggestions for Teachers

The class teacher is the *frontline agent* of all educational change. The following suggestions are offered:

i. **Be aware of the gender-typed pattern.** Expect that boys and girls in the same class have sharply different interest profiles; do not misread it as inability or lack of effort on the part of either group.

ii. **Use gender-balanced examples** — a woman engineer alongside a male engineer, a male nurse alongside a female nurse, a woman scientist alongside a male scientist.

iii. **Encourage cross-gender activities.** In school competitions, debates, dramatics, science exhibitions, art exhibitions, cookery, gardening, sports and craft sessions, ensure *both* boys and girls participate actively.

iv. **Praise effort and ability, not stereotype.** When a girl performs well in mathematics or science, praise the effort rather than describing it as "unusual for a girl"; when a boy shows aesthetic or care-giving talent, praise it rather than treating it as suspect.

v. **Identify and nurture talent early.** Detect and nurture early signs of strong scientific or technical interest in girls, and of strong artistic or social interest in boys, against social pressure.

vi. **Cooperate with the school career-guidance cell** and pass on relevant observations about each student.

vii. **Engage in continuing professional development** through in-service workshops on adolescent psychology, gender sensitivity and career guidance organised by SCERT, NCERT and NIEPA.

## 5.6 Suggestions for Parents

The home is, in the long run, the most powerful single influence on a child's vocational interest. The following suggestions are offered:

i. **Provide gender-neutral toys and play activities** during early childhood. Boys should not be discouraged from playing with kitchen sets and dolls, nor girls from playing with mechanical toys, science kits and computers.

ii. **Engage in gender-neutral household work.** When fathers cook, clean and look after younger children, and when mothers handle finances and discuss career-related matters with their daughters, children grow up with a balanced view.

iii. **Avoid pressuring daughters away from "boys' careers"** (engineering, mechanics, agriculture, scientific research) and **sons away from "girls' careers"** (teaching small children, nursing, cooking, fashion design, social work).

iv. **Expose children to a wide variety of vocational role-models** — factories, farms, hospitals, courts, banks, art studios, NGOs, agricultural research stations.

v. **Use the school's career-guidance services actively** where they exist; encourage the school management to introduce them where they do not.

vi. **Listen to the adolescent voice.** The Class IX–X student is at the *crystallisation* stage of vocational identity; the parental ear is the single most important resource at this stage.

## 5.7 Suggestions for School Administrators

The Principal and the Management Committee create the *climate* in which vocational interests are formed:

i. **Establish a Career-Guidance Cell** with at least one trained counsellor and a well-stocked occupational-information library.

ii. **Hold annual career fairs** with stalls for diverse occupations and short talks by working professionals from a *gender-balanced* roster.

iii. **Organise exposure visits** at least twice a year to agricultural research stations, hospitals, banks, courts, factories, IT companies, schools, NGOs and government offices.

iv. **Implement the NEP 2020 vocational provisions** in spirit — the Class 6–8 vocational survey course and the bagless 10-day vocational period, with equal access for boys and girls to *all* the crafts.

v. **Train the teaching staff** through periodic in-service training in gender sensitivity, adolescent career development and vocational interest measurement.

vi. **Provide infrastructure for non-traditional choices** — adequate laboratories, art studios, music rooms, sports facilities, computer labs and atal tinkering labs, with *equal access* for all students.

vii. **Document and share results** of standardised vocational interest tests in confidential individual counselling sessions with the student and the parent.

## 5.8 Suggestions for Guidance Counsellors

i. **Use standardised tools** — Kulshrestha's V.I.R., Vohra's Comprehensive Interest Schedule and Holland's Self-Directed Search — at the end of Class IX or the beginning of Class X for *every* student.

ii. **Conduct individual counselling.** A 30–45-minute one-to-one session with every student to discuss the interest profile, academic record, family background and the student's own dreams.

iii. **Counsel parents simultaneously** in joint sessions, since parental support is essential for any non-traditional choice.

iv. **Maintain confidentiality** of interest scores and counselling notes.

v. **Counter gender stereotypes consciously** — when the student's measured interest crosses traditional gender lines (a girl high on Constructive, or a boy high on Household), provide *positive support* with concrete examples of successful same-gender role-models.

vi. **Maintain a Roster of Local Role-Models** — successful women in technical careers, men in care-giving careers, women in agriculture, men in teaching, women entrepreneurs — for school visits and counselling sessions.

vii. **Engage in continuing self-development** in vocational psychology, occupational information, NEP 2020 implementation and skill-development programmes (PMKVY, NSDC, Skill India).

viii. **Follow up** in Class XI and again in Class XII at the time of higher-education entry.

## 5.9 Suggestions for Future Research

The present investigation has been able to address only a small slice of the larger problem. Suggestions to future researchers:

i. **Replicate with a larger sample** of 200 – 500 students drawn from a more diverse set of schools (Government / Private / Aided / CBSE / U.P. Board / ICSE, rural / urban / semi-urban).

ii. **Use a 2 × 2 factorial design** (Boys/Girls × Govt/Private, or 2 × 2 × 2 with Locality) to cleanly separate the *gender* effect from *school management* and *locality* effects.

iii. **Conduct a longitudinal study** tracking the same students from Class VI through Class XII to examine the *crystallisation* process.

iv. **Include socio-economic and parental variables** — parental education, occupation, family income and parental encouragement.

v. **Use multiple tools** (V.I.R. + CIS + SDS) for inter-instrument validation and a richer profile.

vi. **Study the aspiration–interest–ability triad** to identify points of agreement and disagreement.

vii. **Examine the impact of career-guidance interventions** through quasi-experimental designs.

viii. **Compare different states** (U.P., Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal, Manipur) to shed light on cultural variability of the gender-typed pattern within India.

ix. **Use modern technology** — online vocational interest measurement, AI-based career-recommendation systems and VR-based vocational exposure.

x. **Compare pre- and post-NEP 2020 cohorts** to evaluate the policy's success in widening adolescent vocational horizons.

## 5.10 Limitations of the Study

i. The *sample size* (N = 40) is small; while adequate for the *t*-test, generalisation must be made with caution.
ii. The two groups have been drawn from *different schools* — Government rural U.P. Board for boys and Private urban CBSE for girls. This maximum-contrast design is methodologically defensible, but it does mean that the *gender* effect cannot be entirely separated from the *school type* effect. Strictly, the present study compares "Government-rural-U.P.-Board boys vs. Private-urban-CBSE girls" rather than "boys vs. girls" *in general*.
iii. Only *one tool* — the V.I.R. — has been used; a multi-tool design would have yielded a richer profile.
iv. The study is *cross-sectional* at a single point in time; a longitudinal design would have been preferable.
v. Background variables such as parental education, occupation, income, encouragement, peer-group climate and quality of teachers — known to influence vocational interest — have not been measured.
vi. The statistical analysis has been limited to Mean, S.D., *t*-test, Percentage Analysis and Spearman ρ; ANOVA, multiple regression and factor analysis have not been used.
vii. The interpretation in terms of "gender-typed socialisation" is *theoretically motivated* by the literature reviewed in Chapter II, but the present study cannot itself *prove* a *causal* link between socialisation and the observed differences. A causal demonstration would require an *experimental* or *intervention* study, which lies beyond the scope of the present investigation.

## 5.11 Final Conclusion

To bring the dissertation to a close, it may be reaffirmed that the present investigation has succeeded in achieving its five stated objectives, in testing the three null hypotheses formulated, in producing a body of empirically grounded findings, in drawing reasoned conclusions and in offering specific practical suggestions for the principal stake-holders of the Indian secondary school.

The single most important *substantive* message of the study is this: in the Indian secondary school of today, the *vocational interests of boys and girls remain sharply, systematically and significantly gender-typed*, with the rare exceptions of the Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas. The *Constructive – Scientific – Agricultural* triad is a *boys' world*, and the *Household – Artistic – Social – Literary* set is a *girls' world*, and the two worlds remain distressingly far apart.

The single most important *practical* message of the study is this: this state of affairs is *not* to be taken as biologically inevitable. It is the cumulative outcome of a long *socialisation chain* — toys, story-books, family expectations, teachers' classroom remarks, peer pressure, occupational role-models on television and at home — and *it can be modified by deliberate educational action*. The Indian school of the twenty-first century, supported by enlightened parents, sensitive teachers, well-trained guidance counsellors and an empowering education policy, has both the *opportunity* and the *responsibility* to broaden the vocational horizons of its boys and girls — to ensure that no Indian child, in 2050 or 2070, is held back from his or her true vocation by the accident of being born a boy or a girl in 2026.

It is in this *direction* that the modest contribution of the present M.Ed. dissertation has, the investigator hopes, been made.
