# CHAPTER – V

# SUMMARY, FINDINGS, CONCLUSIONS AND SUGGESTIONS

## 5.1 Summary of the Study

The present chapter brings the dissertation to a close by drawing together, in a compact form, the entire argument of the four preceding chapters and by indicating the *practical*, *educational* and *research* implications of the findings.

### 5.1.1 The Problem

The investigation has been entitled *"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level"*. It was conducted in the academic district of *Meerut* (Uttar Pradesh) under the affiliation of *Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut*, during the academic session 2025–2026. The Secondary School stage in India coincides with the developmental period of *early adolescence*, in which the individual begins to crystallise his or her vocational choices. The choices made at this stage have far-reaching consequences not only for the academic future of the learner but also for his or her later occupational adjustment, life satisfaction and contribution to society. In view of this, an empirical enquiry into the comparative vocational interests of secondary school *boys* and *girls* in a real Indian setting was considered timely and educationally significant.

### 5.1.2 Objectives

The investigation was designed to fulfil five major objectives:

i. To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and S.D.) of secondary school *boys* on the ten areas of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record*.

ii. To study the area-wise vocational interest scores (Mean and S.D.) of secondary school *girls* on the ten areas of the Record.

iii. To compare the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls on each of the ten areas by means of the *t*-test.

iv. To compute the percentage of boys and girls falling at the high, average and low levels of vocational interest in each of the ten areas.

v. To determine the rank order of the ten areas of vocational interest for boys and for girls and to examine the agreement between the two rank orders.

### 5.1.3 Hypotheses

In keeping with the above objectives, three null hypotheses were formulated:

**H₀1.** There is no significant difference between the mean scores of boys and girls of secondary level on any of the ten areas of vocational interest.

**H₀2.** Boys and girls do not differ significantly in the percentage distribution of their interest at the high, average and low levels.

**H₀3.** Boys and girls do not differ significantly in the rank order in which they place the ten areas of vocational interest.

### 5.1.4 Method, Sample and Tool

The investigation employed the *descriptive survey method*. A total of **40 students** of Class IX and X were selected through the *simple random sampling technique* (lottery method) — *20 boys* from *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut* (a Government rural U.P.-Board school) and *20 girls* from *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut* (a Private urban CBSE-affiliated school). The two schools were *purposively* selected to represent maximally contrasting school environments. The principal tool was *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)*, a standardised forced-choice instrument of 90 items measuring interest in ten areas — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

### 5.1.5 Statistical Techniques

*Mean*, *Standard Deviation*, the *t*-test for two independent means (df = 38) and *Percentage Analysis* were used. In addition, *Spearman's rank-order correlation coefficient* was computed for the comparative rank-order analysis (Objective 5).

## 5.2 Major Findings of the Study

The major findings of the present investigation, drawn from the analysis presented in Chapter IV, are listed below.

### 5.2.1 Findings related to Objectives 1 and 2 (Descriptive Statistics)

1. The mean Vocational Interest scores of the *boys* on the ten areas, in descending order, were: Constructive (11.90), Scientific (11.65), Agricultural (10.35), Persuasive (9.60), Executive (9.50), Commercial (8.85), Social (7.75), Artistic (7.55), Literary (7.05) and Household (5.80).

2. The mean Vocational Interest scores of the *girls* on the ten areas, in descending order, were: Household (12.10), Artistic (11.00), Social (10.55), Literary (9.85), Persuasive (8.65), Executive (8.05), Commercial (8.00), Scientific (7.95), Constructive (7.05) and Agricultural (6.70).

3. The standard deviations were uniformly moderate (range 2.00 – 2.65 for boys and 2.05 – 2.65 for girls), indicating *homogeneous* distributions within each group.

### 5.2.2 Findings related to Objective 3 / H₀1 (Mean-Difference Comparison)

4. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the Scientific area (t = 4.54, p < 0.01).

5. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the Constructive (Technical) area (t = 5.96, p < 0.01).

6. *Boys obtained significantly higher mean scores than girls* on the Agricultural area (t = 4.48, p < 0.01).

7. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the Literary area (t = 4.02, p < 0.01).

8. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the Artistic area (t = 5.07, p < 0.01).

9. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the Social area (t = 3.93, p < 0.01).

10. *Girls obtained significantly higher mean scores than boys* on the Household area (t = 9.36, p < 0.01) — the largest difference observed in any of the ten comparisons.

11. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the Executive area (t = 1.91, NS).

12. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the Commercial area (t = 1.17, NS).

13. *No statistically significant difference* between boys and girls was observed on the Persuasive area (t = 1.31, NS).

14. The null hypothesis *H₀1* was therefore *partly rejected* and *partly retained* — rejected for seven gender-differentiated areas (Literary, Scientific, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Social, Household) and retained for three gender-neutral areas (Executive, Commercial, Persuasive).

### 5.2.3 Findings related to Objective 4 / H₀2 (Percentage Distribution)

15. On the Constructive area, *60 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of girls* — a gap of 50 percentage points in favour of boys.

16. On the Scientific area, *55 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of girls* — a gap of 45 percentage points in favour of boys.

17. On the Agricultural area, *40 % of boys* fell at the High level as against only *5 % of girls* — a gap of 35 percentage points in favour of boys.

18. On the Household area, *60 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *5 % of boys* — a gap of 55 percentage points in favour of girls (the largest single gap in the study).

19. On the Artistic area, *45 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *15 % of boys* — a gap of 30 percentage points in favour of girls.

20. On the Social area, *40 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *15 % of boys* — a gap of 25 percentage points in favour of girls.

21. On the Literary area, *30 % of girls* fell at the High level as against only *10 % of boys* — a gap of 20 percentage points in favour of girls.

22. On the Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas, the percentages of boys and girls at the High level differed by less than 20 percentage points (10 – 20 %), all in favour of boys.

23. The null hypothesis *H₀2* was therefore *partly rejected* (for areas with gaps ≥ 20 %) and *partly retained* (for areas with smaller gaps).

### 5.2.4 Findings related to Objective 5 / H₀3 (Rank-Order Analysis)

24. The boys' top three preferred areas (Constructive, Scientific, Agricultural) coincided almost exactly with the girls' bottom three preferred areas (Constructive — 9, Scientific — 8, Agricultural — 10).

25. The girls' top three preferred areas (Household, Artistic, Social) coincided closely with the boys' bottom four preferred areas (Household — 10, Artistic — 8, Social — 7).

26. The Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas, on which the *t*-test showed *no* gender difference, occupied very similar middle ranks for both groups (Executive — 5/6, Commercial — 6/7, Persuasive — 4/5).

27. The *Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ)* between the boys' and the girls' rank orders was *−0.88*, statistically significant at the 0.01 level (n = 10, ρ₀.₀₁ = ±0.794). The negative correlation is so high that the two rank orders are virtually *mirror-images* of each other.

28. The null hypothesis *H₀3* was therefore *strongly rejected*; the rank orders are not merely different — they are *systematically reversed*.

## 5.3 Conclusions

In the light of the empirical evidence presented in Chapter IV and summarised in Section 5.2 of this chapter, the following major *conclusions* may be drawn from the present investigation:

**Conclusion 1.** The vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls of the Meerut District are *strongly, systematically and statistically significantly* gender-typed. The two groups occupy *different and largely opposed* vocational worlds, with very limited overlap in the High-level zone of the ten interest areas.

**Conclusion 2.** Boys are significantly higher than girls on the *Scientific*, *Constructive (Technical)* and *Agricultural* areas. The Constructive–Scientific–Agricultural triad represents the *traditional masculine occupational image* of Indian rural society, and is reinforced both by the family background of the rural-Government-school boys and by the prevailing social expectations.

**Conclusion 3.** Girls are significantly higher than boys on the *Literary, Artistic, Social* and *Household* areas. The Household–Artistic–Social–Literary set represents the *traditional feminine occupational image* of Indian society, and is reinforced both by family modelling and by the prevailing social expectations, even when (as in the K.P. International School sample) the family is highly educated and economically well-placed.

**Conclusion 4.** The *Executive*, *Commercial* and *Persuasive* areas have emerged as *gender-neutral* in the present sample. The historic male advantage on managerial, financial and persuasive occupations has narrowed to insignificance, suggesting that contemporary Indian girls — at least those of the urban-Private-CBSE stream — are claiming an equal stake in administration, business and marketing-related careers.

**Conclusion 5.** The very high *negative* Spearman rank-order correlation (ρ = −0.88) between the boys' and the girls' rank orders is, perhaps, the *most striking single statistical finding* of the present study. It shows not merely that the two groups differ, but that the *very ordering* of their interests is *reversed*. Boys' favourite vocations are nearly girls' least preferred ones, and *vice versa*.

**Conclusion 6.** The convergence of the three statistical lenses — the *t*-test, the percentage analysis and the rank-order analysis — strengthens the substantive interpretation. Each lens highlights the same essential pattern from a slightly different angle, and the agreement among them strengthens the *internal validity* of the conclusions.

**Conclusion 7.** Although the present sample is small (N = 40), the consistency of the findings with those reported by Singh and Verma (2023), Reddy and Kumar (2021), Joshi (2020), Mehta (2019), Yadav (2016) and the Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) meta-analysis lends *external validity* to the conclusions. The present study, in spite of its modest scope, contributes a *small replication* of the broader gender-typed pattern in the contemporary Meerut context.

## 5.4 Educational Implications

The findings carry several important *educational implications* for the Indian secondary school. The most important of these are spelt out below:

**Implication 1 — Recognition of the gender-typed reality.** Secondary schools, parents and policy-makers must, first of all, *recognise* that the vocational interests of contemporary Indian adolescents continue to be sharply gender-typed. The bland claim that "boys and girls have equal opportunities now" is empirically untrue at the level of *interest formation*; it cannot be assumed and must be *consciously worked for*.

**Implication 2 — Need for organised career counselling at the secondary stage.** Both schools sampled in the present study lack a regular, full-time, trained career counsellor. The U.P. Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad, the C.B.S.E. and the Department of Secondary Education must seriously revisit the recommendations of the Kothari Commission and the National Education Policy 2020 on this point.

**Implication 3 — Vocational interest measurement as a part of the school programme.** Standardised tools such as Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's *Vocational Interest Record* should be routinely administered at the end of Class IX. The results should be discussed individually with each learner and his or her parents to inform the Class XI stream choice.

**Implication 4 — Exposure to non-traditional vocations.** Adolescent girls, especially those of urban Private CBSE schools, must be given systematic exposure to *Scientific, Constructive (Technical) and Agricultural* careers — through school exhibitions, lab demonstrations, factory visits, exposure to women engineers, women scientists, women entrepreneurs and women agricultural scientists. The 50 percentage-point gap on Constructive interest cannot be allowed to harden into an *adult* gender gap on engineering enrolment.

**Implication 5 — Encouraging boys towards "feminine" vocations.** Conversely, adolescent boys, especially of rural Government schools, must be exposed to *Literary, Artistic, Social and Household* vocations — through visits to teaching institutions, hospitals, art studios, hospitality industries and the homes of well-known male chefs, designers and care-givers. The fact that *only 5 % of the boys* of the present sample reach the High level on Household — and *50 % fall at the Low level* — is indicative of a near-total devaluation of household work as a "boy's" career, which is socially and educationally undesirable.

**Implication 6 — Sensitisation of teachers, parents and administrators.** Teachers and parents must consciously *avoid* the casual remarks and the routine practices that perpetuate gender-typed expectations. Teacher-training and parent-education programmes should make this a deliberate focus.

**Implication 7 — Diversification of the secondary curriculum.** The provisions of the National Education Policy 2020 for *vocational education* in middle and secondary classes (a survey of important vocational crafts in Grades 6–8 and a 10-day "bagless period" of vocational exposure) must be implemented in spirit and not merely on paper. Equal access of boys and girls to *all* the vocational crafts — cooking and embroidery as much as electrical wiring and motor mechanics — is essential.

**Implication 8 — Strengthening the Persuasive, Executive and Commercial gender-neutral zone.** The fact that these three areas have emerged as gender-neutral in the present sample is encouraging. The Indian school can build on this gain by deliberately providing girls with leadership roles, administrative responsibilities (head girl, club secretaries, prefects) and business-skill exposure.

**Implication 9 — Use of the findings in stream allocation.** Class IX results on the Vocational Interest Record should be used as one of the inputs for the Class XI stream allocation. Where the *measured interest* and the *student's stream choice* are mismatched (as Pandey and Tripathi (2022) found in 34 per cent of cases), counselling should be undertaken to identify the source of the mismatch.

**Implication 10 — Linking vocational interest with skill development.** At the school level, the *Skill India* and *Atal Tinkering Lab* programmes must be re-designed to involve girls actively, especially in areas like robotics, electronics and 3-D printing, where the gender gap in interest is currently widest.

**Implication 11 — Need for region-specific empirical baselines.** Each district of the country has its own social, occupational and educational profile. Local empirical studies of the present type — even at the M.Ed. level — provide useful baselines for district-level career-guidance planning.

**Implication 12 — Inclusion in B.Ed. and M.Ed. curricula.** The B.Ed. and M.Ed. course "*Guidance and Counselling*" should include modules on the *systematic measurement of vocational interest* and on the *interpretation* of the typical gender-typed pattern. Without such training, future teachers and counsellors cannot do justice to the demands of contemporary career guidance.


## 5.5 Suggestions for Teachers

The class teacher is the *frontline agent* of all educational change. The following suggestions are offered to the secondary school teacher of Meerut District (and beyond):

i. **Be aware of the gender-typed pattern.** Every secondary school teacher should be aware that boys and girls in his or her class are likely to have sharply different vocational interest profiles. The teacher should *expect* this and should *not* misread it as inability or lack of effort on the part of either group.

ii. **Use gender-balanced examples.** When teaching mathematics, science, English, history, civics or any other subject, make a conscious effort to use *both* male and female occupational examples — a woman engineer alongside a male engineer, a male teacher alongside a female teacher, a male nurse alongside a female nurse, a woman scientist alongside a male scientist.

iii. **Encourage cross-gender activities.** In school competitions, debates, dramatics, science exhibitions, art exhibitions, cookery, gardening, sports and craft sessions, ensure that *both* boys and girls participate actively. Avoid the convention of routinely allotting cookery and embroidery to girls and woodwork or motor-repair to boys.

iv. **Praise effort and ability, not stereotype.** When a girl performs well in mathematics or science, praise the effort and ability rather than describing it as "unusual for a girl". When a boy shows aesthetic or care-giving talent, praise it rather than treating it as suspect.

v. **Identify and nurture talent early.** A class teacher who detects early signs of strong scientific or technical interest in a girl, or of strong artistic or social interest in a boy, should *nurture* it actively — by providing reading material, by speaking to the parents, by recommending exhibitions and competitions and by reinforcing the chosen path against social pressure.

vi. **Cooperate with the school career-guidance cell.** Teachers should cooperate fully with the school's career-guidance and counselling cell (where it exists) and should pass on relevant observations about each student.

vii. **Engage in continuing professional development.** Teachers should attend in-service workshops on adolescent psychology, gender sensitivity and career guidance, organised by SCERT, NCERT, NIEPA and the National Institute of Educational Planning.

## 5.6 Suggestions for Parents

The home is, in the long run, the most powerful single influence on a child's vocational interest. Parents in Meerut and elsewhere are therefore the most important stake-holders. The following suggestions are offered to them:

i. **Provide gender-neutral toys and play activities** during early childhood. Boys should not be discouraged from playing with kitchen sets and dolls, nor girls from playing with mechanical toys, science kits and computers.

ii. **Engage in gender-neutral household work.** When fathers cook, clean, look after younger children and involve their sons in these activities, the boys grow up with a balanced view of household work. When mothers handle finances, repair simple household appliances and discuss career-related matters with their daughters, girls grow up with a balanced view of practical and managerial work.

iii. **Avoid pressuring daughters away from "boys' careers".** When a daughter expresses interest in engineering, mechanics, agriculture or scientific research, parents should *support* her decision rather than steering her towards "safer" options.

iv. **Avoid pressuring sons away from "girls' careers".** When a son expresses interest in teaching small children, nursing, cooking, fashion designing, fine arts, hospitality or social work, parents should *support* him rather than ridiculing the choice.

v. **Expose children to a wide variety of vocational role-models.** Take adolescent children to factories, farms, hospitals, courts, banks, art studios, NGOs, film studios, agricultural research stations and any other places where varied vocational work is on display.

vi. **Use the school's career-guidance services actively.** Where a school provides career counselling, parents should use it. Where it does not, parents should encourage the school management to introduce it.

vii. **Allow the adolescent voice to be heard.** Above all, parents should *listen* to their adolescent children. The Class IX–X student is at the *crystallisation* stage of vocational identity, and the parental ear is the single most important resource at this stage.

## 5.7 Suggestions for School Administrators

The Principal and the Management Committee of the school create the *climate* in which vocational interests are formed. The following suggestions are offered to school administrators:

i. **Establish a Career-Guidance Cell.** Every secondary school, regardless of management or locality, should have a designated *Career-Guidance Cell*, with at least one trained counsellor and a well-stocked occupational-information library.

ii. **Hold annual career fairs.** A school-level *Career Fair* once a year, with stalls for diverse occupations and short talks by working professionals from a *gender-balanced* roster, can do much to broaden adolescent horizons.

iii. **Organise exposure visits.** Visits to agricultural research stations, hospitals, banks, courts, factories, IT companies, schools, NGOs and government offices should be organised at least twice a year for every Class IX – X student.

iv. **Implement the NEP 2020 vocational provisions.** The Class 6 – 8 vocational survey course and the bagless 10-day vocational period must be implemented in spirit, with equal access for boys and girls to *all* the vocational crafts.

v. **Train the teaching staff.** All teachers should receive periodic in-service training in gender sensitivity, adolescent career development and the basics of vocational interest measurement.

vi. **Provide infrastructure for non-traditional choices.** Schools should provide adequate laboratory facilities, art studios, music rooms, indoor and outdoor sports facilities, computer labs and atal tinkering labs, and ensure that *all* students have *equal access* to them.

vii. **Document and share results.** Results of standardised vocational interest tests should be properly documented, kept confidential and shared with the student and the parent in an individual counselling session.

## 5.8 Suggestions for Guidance Counsellors

The trained counsellor stands at the heart of the modern career-guidance enterprise. The following suggestions are offered:

i. **Use standardised tools.** Tools such as Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record, Sanjay Vohra's Comprehensive Interest Schedule and Holland's Self-Directed Search should be administered at the end of Class IX or the beginning of Class X to *every* student.

ii. **Conduct individual counselling.** A one-to-one counselling session, lasting 30 to 45 minutes, should be conducted with every student to discuss the interest profile, the academic record, the family background and the student's own dreams.

iii. **Counsel parents simultaneously.** Wherever possible, the parents should be invited to a *joint* counselling session, since their support is essential for any non-traditional choice.

iv. **Maintain confidentiality.** The student's interest scores and counselling notes should be kept strictly confidential.

v. **Counter gender stereotypes consciously.** When the student's measured interest crosses traditional gender lines (a girl high on Constructive, or a boy high on Household), the counsellor should provide *positive support* and accurate information about the chosen vocation and concrete examples of successful role-models of the same gender in that vocation.

vi. **Maintain a roster of role-models.** Every guidance cell should maintain a *Roster of Local Role-Models* — successful women in technical careers, successful men in care-giving careers, women in agriculture, men in teaching, women entrepreneurs, etc. — which can be drawn upon for school visits and counselling sessions.

vii. **Engage in continuing self-development.** The counsellor must keep up with the latest developments in vocational psychology, occupational information, NEP 2020 implementation and skill-development programmes such as PMKVY, NSDC and Skill India.

viii. **Follow up.** Counselling is not complete with the first session. Follow-up sessions in Class XI and again in Class XII (at the time of higher-education entry) should be built into the school's calendar.

## 5.9 Suggestions for Future Research

The present investigation, by virtue of its modest M.Ed. scope, has been able to address only a small slice of the larger problem. Many richer questions remain open. The following suggestions are offered to future researchers — M.Ed., M.Phil., Ph.D. and beyond — interested in this field:

i. **Replicate with a larger sample.** A study with 200 – 500 students drawn from a more diverse set of schools (Government / Private / Aided / CBSE / U.P. Board / ICSE / I.B., rural / urban / semi-urban) would provide much more generalisable results than the present 40-student sample.

ii. **Use a 2 × 2 factorial design.** A *2 (Boys/Girls) × 2 (Govt/Private)* or *2 × 2 × 2 (Boys/Girls × Govt/Private × Rural/Urban)* design would allow the *gender* effect to be cleanly separated from the *school management* and *locality* effects.

iii. **Conduct a longitudinal study.** A study tracking the same students from Class VI through Class XII would shed light on the *crystallisation* process — the precise stages at which the gender-typed pattern hardens.

iv. **Include socio-economic and parental variables.** Future research should measure parental education, occupation, family income and parental encouragement, and examine their relationship with the child's vocational interest.

v. **Use multiple tools.** A multi-tool design (Vocational Interest Record + Vohra's CIS + Holland's SDS) would allow inter-instrument validation and a richer profile.

vi. **Study the aspiration-interest-ability triad.** Future research should jointly measure the student's *aspiration* (what he or she wants to do), *interest* (what he or she likes) and *ability* (what he or she can do) and study the points of agreement and disagreement.

vii. **Examine the impact of career-guidance interventions.** A *quasi-experimental* study comparing the vocational interests of two matched groups, one of which has received a one-year career-guidance intervention and the other not, would provide direct evidence of the *modifiability* of vocational interest.

viii. **Compare different states.** A multi-state comparative study (e.g., U.P., Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, West Bengal, Manipur) would shed light on the cultural variability of the gender-typed pattern within India.

ix. **Use modern technology.** Future studies could also explore *online vocational interest measurement*, *AI-based career-recommendation systems* and *VR-based vocational exposure* in the secondary school setting.

x. **Compare pre- and post-NEP 2020 cohorts.** As the NEP 2020 reforms unroll, comparative studies of pre- and post-NEP cohorts will be valuable in evaluating the policy's success in widening adolescent vocational horizons.


## 5.10 Limitations of the Study

The investigator has, throughout the dissertation, been frank about the limits of the present study. The principal limitations may now be brought together in one place:

i. The *sample size* (N = 40) is small. While it is adequate for the *t*-test (df = 38), the generalisation of the findings to all secondary school students of Meerut District must be made with caution.

ii. The two groups (boys and girls) have been drawn from *different schools* — a Government rural U.P.-Board school (boys) and a Private urban CBSE school (girls). Although this maximum-contrast design is methodologically defensible, it does mean that the *gender* effect cannot be entirely separated from the *school type* effect. Strictly, the present study compares "*Government-rural-U.P.-Board boys vs. Private-urban-CBSE girls*" rather than "boys vs. girls" *in general*.

iii. Only *one tool* — the Vocational Interest Record — has been used. A multi-tool design would have yielded a richer profile.

iv. The study is a *cross-sectional* one, conducted at a single point in time. A longitudinal design would have been preferable.

v. Background variables such as parental education, parental occupation, family income, parental encouragement, peer-group climate and the quality of the teachers — all of which are known to influence vocational interest — have not been measured.

vi. The statistical analysis has been limited to Mean, S.D., *t*-test, Percentage Analysis and Spearman rank-order correlation. ANOVA, multiple regression and factor analysis have not been used.

vii. The interpretation in terms of "gender-typed socialisation" is *theoretically motivated* by the literature reviewed in Chapter II, but the present study cannot itself *prove* a *causal* link between socialisation and the observed differences. A causal demonstration would require an *experimental* or *intervention* study, which lies beyond the scope of the present investigation.

## 5.11 Final Conclusion

To bring the dissertation to a close, it may be reaffirmed that the present investigation has succeeded in achieving its five stated objectives, in testing the three null hypotheses formulated, in producing a body of empirically grounded findings, in drawing reasoned conclusions and in offering specific practical suggestions for the principal stake-holders of the Indian secondary school.

The single most important *substantive* message of the study is this: in the Indian secondary school of today, the *vocational interests of boys and girls remain sharply, systematically and significantly gender-typed*, with the rare exceptions of the Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas. The *Constructive – Scientific – Agricultural* triad is a *boys' world*, and the *Household – Artistic – Social – Literary* set is a *girls' world*, and the two worlds remain distressingly far apart.

The single most important *practical* message of the study is this: this state of affairs is *not* to be taken as biologically inevitable. It is the cumulative outcome of a long *socialisation chain* — toys, story-books, family expectations, teachers' classroom remarks, peer pressure, occupational role-models on television and at home — and *it can be modified by deliberate educational action*. The Indian school of the twenty-first century, supported by enlightened parents, sensitive teachers, well-trained guidance counsellors and an empowering education policy, has both the *opportunity* and the *responsibility* to broaden the vocational horizons of its boys and its girls — to ensure that no Indian child, in 2050 or 2070, is held back from his or her true vocation by the accident of being born a boy or a girl in 2026.

It is in this *direction* that the modest contribution of the present M.Ed. dissertation has, the investigator hopes, been made.
