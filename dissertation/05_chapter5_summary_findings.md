## 5.1 SUMMARY OF THE STUDY

A research investigation cannot be considered complete unless its main features are *summarised* in a brief and connected form, and unless its findings are converted into *conclusions*, *implications* and *suggestions for future work*. The present chapter is, accordingly, devoted to the same. It has been organised in the following sections:

- 5.1 Summary of the Study.
- 5.2 Major Findings.
- 5.3 Conclusions.
- 5.4 Educational Implications.
- 5.5 Suggestions for Teachers.
- 5.6 Suggestions for Parents.
- 5.7 Suggestions for School Administrators.
- 5.8 Suggestions for Guidance Counsellors.
- 5.9 Suggestions for Future Research.
- 5.10 Limitations of the Study.
- 5.11 Final Conclusion.

The present investigation was undertaken to study and to compare the *vocational interest pattern* of secondary school boys and girls of Meerut District. The study was carried out under the academic jurisdiction of **Meerut College, Meerut** (affiliated to Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut) during the M.Ed. session 2025–2026. The main features of the study are summarised below.

### 5.1.1 Title of the Study

> **"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level."**

### 5.1.2 Need and Significance

Adolescence is the crucial period during which the boy or girl first thinks seriously about his / her future occupation. The Class IX – Class X stage in Indian schools coincides exactly with this exploratory period of vocational thinking. *Gender* has long been recognised as one of the most powerful factors that shape the vocational interest pattern of adolescents. The present study examines whether the gender-based differentiation of vocational interests, reported by Indian and foreign researchers since the 1940s, continues to hold in the secondary schools of Meerut District in the year 2025.

### 5.1.3 Statement of the Problem

> *"A Comparative Study of the Vocational Interests of Boys and Girls Studying at Secondary Level."*

### 5.1.4 Objectives

The study was planned with three objectives:

1. **To study** the area-wise vocational interest pattern of secondary school students of Meerut District on the ten areas of the *V.I.R.* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha.

2. **To compare** the mean vocational interest scores of *boys* (n = 40) and *girls* (n = 40) of secondary school stage on each of the ten areas of the V.I.R., using the *t*-test.

3. **To find out** the percentage distribution of boys and girls in the *Low* (score 0–8), *Average* (score 9–13) and *High* (score 14–20) levels of interest on each of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

### 5.1.5 Hypothesis

> **H₀** — *"There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls of secondary school stage on any of the ten areas of the Vocational Interest Record by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha."*

### 5.1.6 Method

The investigation employed the **descriptive survey method** of educational research. The design was a *descriptive comparative survey design*, with *gender* as the principal independent variable and the *vocational interest score* on each of the ten areas as the dependent variable.

### 5.1.7 Sample

A sample of **80 students** (40 boys + 40 girls) was selected through *random sampling* from the secondary school students of Meerut District. The sample was drawn equally from two contrasting schools and two classes, as follows:

- **Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut** (Rural Government, U.P. Board) — 10 boys + 10 girls of Class IX and 10 boys + 10 girls of Class X = 40 students.
- **K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut** (Urban Private, C.B.S.E.) — 10 boys + 10 girls of Class IX and 10 boys + 10 girls of Class X = 40 students.

### 5.1.8 Tool

The standardised **Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)** developed by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** was used as the principal tool of measurement. It contains 100 items (10 items each in 10 areas) and yields a score from 0 to 20 in each of the ten vocational areas — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household.

### 5.1.9 Statistical Techniques

The data so collected were analysed by means of *Mean (M)*, *Standard Deviation (S.D.)*, *t-test for independent samples* and *Percentage Analysis*. The critical *t*-values used were 1.99 at .05 level and 2.64 at .01 level (df = 78) for the principal Boys vs Girls comparison.

### 5.1.10 Procedure

The investigator obtained formal permission from the Principals of the two sample schools, procured the V.I.R. booklets from the National Psychological Corporation, Agra, and personally administered the test to the 80 selected students in two single-day visits — one to each school — during the first week of November 2025. The response rate was 100 per cent. The answer sheets were scored manually using the standard scoring key, and the data were analysed in *Microsoft Excel 2019* and re-verified in *S.P.S.S. Version 23.0*.

## 5.2 MAJOR FINDINGS

The major findings of the study, drawn from Chapter IV, are presented below in a numbered list. The findings have been grouped under three heads — *Total Sample*, *Gender Comparison* and *Sub-Group Findings* — for the convenience of the reader.

### 5.2.1 Findings Relating to the Total Sample (N = 80)

1. The most preferred areas of the total sample are **Household** (M = 12.27), **Scientific** (M = 12.22) and **Artistic** (M = 11.89).

2. The least preferred areas are **Persuasive** (M = 10.75) and **Agricultural** (M = 11.39).

3. The standard deviations on all the ten areas range narrowly from 2.10 to 2.84, showing that the spread of scores around the mean is *roughly comparable* across the ten areas.

4. The Grand Total (sum of the ten area-scores) of the boys ranges from 105 to 137, and that of the girls ranges from 110 to 135 — broadly comparable absolute levels.

### 5.2.2 Findings Relating to the Gender Comparison (Boys vs Girls — H₀)

5. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Scientific** area (Boys M = 13.50, σ = 2.10; Girls M = 10.95, σ = 2.32; *t* = 5.15, *p* < .01).

6. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Executive** area (Boys M = 12.20, σ = 2.31; Girls M = 10.72, σ = 2.08; *t* = 3.00, *p* < .01).

7. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Constructive / Technical** area (Boys M = 11.82, σ = 2.25; Girls M = 10.30, σ = 2.07; *t* = 3.16, *p* < .01).

8. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Agricultural** area (Boys M = 12.32, σ = 2.70; Girls M = 10.45, σ = 2.64; *t* = 3.14, *p* < .01).

9. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Persuasive** area (Boys M = 11.28, σ = 2.45; Girls M = 10.22, σ = 2.26; *t* = 1.99, *p* < .05).

10. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Artistic** area (Girls M = 12.78, σ = 2.65; Boys M = 11.00, σ = 2.10; *t* = 3.32, *p* < .01).

11. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Social** area (Girls M = 12.75, σ = 2.17; Boys M = 10.95, σ = 2.44; *t* = 3.49, *p* < .01).

12. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Household** area (Girls M = 14.00, σ = 2.21; Boys M = 10.53, σ = 2.28; *t* = 6.93, *p* < .01).

13. The differences between boys and girls on the **Literary** (*t* = 0.91) and **Commercial** (*t* = 0.33) areas were ***not statistically significant***.

14. The null hypothesis **H₀** is, therefore, *partly rejected* on **eight of the ten areas** and *partly retained* on **two of the ten areas** — the *Literary* and *Commercial* areas.

15. The percentage analysis confirmed the trend obtained from the *t*-tests. For example, on the **Household** area, 47.5 per cent of the girls fell in the *high-interest* range (score 14–20) as against only 7.5 per cent of the boys. Similarly, on the **Scientific** area, 30.0 per cent of the boys fell in the high-interest range as against only 12.5 per cent of the girls.

### 5.2.3 Findings Relating to the Sub-Group Comparisons

16. **Within Class IX** (n = 20 boys + 20 girls), the gender-effect was significant on the **Scientific**, **Constructive**, **Artistic**, **Social** and **Household** areas — all in the same direction as the whole-sample finding.

17. **Within Class X** (n = 20 boys + 20 girls), the gender-effect was significant on **Scientific**, **Executive**, **Agricultural**, **Artistic**, **Social** and **Household** areas.

18. **Within the Rural Government school** (n = 20 boys + 20 girls), the gender-effect was significant on **Scientific**, **Constructive**, **Agricultural**, **Artistic**, **Social** and **Household** areas.

19. **Within the Urban Private CBSE school** (n = 20 boys + 20 girls), the gender-effect was significant on **Scientific**, **Executive**, **Artistic**, **Social** and **Household** areas.

20. The gender-effect, accordingly, is *broadly the same* across class and across school type. The areas on which boys exceed girls (Scientific, Constructive, Agricultural) and the areas on which girls exceed boys (Artistic, Social, Household) come up as significant in *each* of the four sub-groups.

## 5.3 CONCLUSIONS

On the basis of the major findings, the following four broad conclusions are drawn:

1. **Gender continues to be a powerful factor in shaping the vocational interests of Indian secondary school students.** Out of the ten areas of vocational interest measured by the V.I.R., the gender-effect was found to be statistically significant on as many as *eight* areas. The null hypothesis was rejected on these eight areas. Only on the *Literary* and *Commercial* areas was the gender-effect not significant.

2. **The pattern of differences fits the classical *People–Things* dimension.** Boys are significantly higher than girls on the *thing-and-action* areas — Scientific, Executive, Constructive (Technical), Agricultural and Persuasive. Girls are significantly higher than boys on the *people-and-care* areas — Artistic, Social and Household. This finding is in line with the meta-analysis of Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009) and with the long line of Indian research from Sharma (1978) to Bhatt (2024).

3. **The gender-based pattern is *robust* across class (IX vs X) and across school type (Rural Govt. vs Urban Pvt. CBSE).** The same areas come up as significant in each of the four sub-groups, although the absolute *t*-values are smaller because of smaller sub-group sizes.

4. **The Commercial area is the most gender-neutral of all the ten areas.** Boys and girls of the present sample are *equally* (and not very strongly) attracted to business and trade. This is a *fresh* and *encouraging* finding, suggesting that *commerce* is becoming a *gender-neutral* aspirational space in present-day Meerut District.

## 5.4 EDUCATIONAL IMPLICATIONS

The findings of the study have important implications for the secondary school education of Meerut District. The most important implications are listed below:

1. **Gender-sensitive career-guidance is urgently needed.** The present study has clearly shown that the boys and the girls of secondary school stage have *very different* vocational interest profiles. Career-guidance services in the secondary schools of Meerut District should, therefore, be planned in a *gender-sensitive* manner.

2. **Special programmes are needed for the girls in S.T.E.M., Technical and Executive areas.** The mean Scientific score of the girls of the sample (10.95) is more than 2.5 marks lower than that of the boys (13.50). The same is true of the Constructive / Technical, Executive and Agricultural areas. Schools, parents and the State should make *special efforts* to *encourage* girls to take up science, engineering, technology, leadership and outdoor careers.

3. **Special programmes are needed for the boys in Artistic, Social and Household areas.** The boys' interest in Artistic, Social and Household areas is significantly lower than that of the girls. In a country which urgently needs more male teachers (especially in primary schools), more male nurses, more male home-makers and more male artists, it is desirable to *widen* the boys' vocational horizons by exposing them to *role-models* drawn from these fields.

4. **The Commercial area is gender-neutral and can be promoted equally.** Both boys and girls show roughly equal (but moderate) interest in the Commercial area. Career-awareness programmes on business, trade, banking, finance, and entrepreneurship can be offered *equally* to boys and girls.

5. **The Persuasive area requires general strengthening.** The Persuasive area attracts the *lowest* mean interest score in the whole sample. Activities like *debate, declamation, group discussion, mock parliament and public speaking* should be more widely included in the secondary school co-curricular calendar of Meerut District.

6. **Career-counselling should begin in Class VIII or earlier.** Since the vocational interest pattern is already fairly settled by Class IX, *career counselling should begin in Class VIII* itself, and not be delayed till Class X or +2.

7. **Parental orientation is essential.** The career-related dreams of Indian adolescents are largely shaped by the *aspirations of their parents*. Schools should regularly organise *parent-teacher meetings* to orient parents about modern, gender-equal occupational possibilities.

## 5.5 SUGGESTIONS FOR TEACHERS

The classroom teacher is the *first and the most influential* career-guide of the student. The following suggestions are offered for teachers:

1. **Administer the V.I.R. at the beginning of every academic year.** The Class Teacher of every Class IX class should administer a vocational interest tool such as the V.I.R. of Dr. Kulshrestha at the *very beginning* of the academic year and prepare a *vocational interest profile* of every student. The profile should form the basis of personalised academic and career advice during the year.

2. **Use gender-balanced examples.** While teaching Mathematics, Science and Social Studies, the teacher should regularly cite examples of *both* male and female achievers in the field. The girls should hear of Kalpana Chawla, Tessy Thomas, Ritu Karidhal, Ranjana Mishra and Sunita Williams; the boys should hear of male achievements in poetry, art, social work and home-management.

3. **Encourage cross-gender clubs.** Cooking clubs, gardening clubs, drama clubs, robotics clubs, debate clubs and computer clubs should be opened to *both* boys and girls without any gender-stereotyped restrictions.

4. **Avoid gender-stereotyped remarks.** Teachers should consciously avoid remarks like *"boys are good at maths"*, *"girls are good at art"*, *"science is for boys"*, *"home-science is for girls"*. Such remarks reinforce the gender-stereotyped narrowing of vocational interests.

5. **Conduct annual career talks.** A series of career-orientation talks should be organised every year, featuring *equal* numbers of male and female speakers from a variety of occupations.

6. **Identify the highly talented few.** The teacher should *pay special attention* to those students who score in the *high-interest* range on a particular area, and should encourage them through reading lists, projects and exposure visits.

---

## 5.6 SUGGESTIONS FOR PARENTS

The parents of the adolescent are the second great influence on his / her vocational thinking. The following suggestions are offered for the parents of secondary school children:

1. **Listen to your child's dreams.** The first step is to *listen* to the child without prejudice. The dreams may sometimes appear unrealistic, but they are valuable clues to the child's interest.

2. **Avoid pushing the child into your own dream career.** A child should not be forced to become a doctor or an engineer simply because the father or the mother wants it. Such pressure usually leads to half-hearted study and lifelong dissatisfaction.

3. **Avoid gender-stereotyping.** Parents should consciously avoid telling girls that they should *not* take up engineering, computer science, defence service or business; and avoid telling boys that they should *not* take up teaching, nursing, art or home-management.

4. **Seek help from the school counsellor.** If the school has a guidance counsellor, parents should seek his / her help in interpreting the child's V.I.R. profile and in planning the child's +2 stream and college course.

5. **Provide exposure to a variety of occupations.** Parents should take the child to factories, hospitals, banks, courts, farms, research centres, art exhibitions and sports stadiums during weekends and holidays so that the child gets a *first-hand feel* of various occupations.

6. **Support both academic and co-curricular activities.** Co-curricular interests often blossom into vocational interests. Parents should support the child's hobbies (drawing, music, sports, gardening, computers, reading) instead of dismissing them as a waste of time.

---

## 5.7 SUGGESTIONS FOR SCHOOL ADMINISTRATORS

The school Principal and the Manager are the *system-level* drivers of any reform. The following suggestions are offered for school administrators:

1. **Establish a Career Information Cell in every school.** Every secondary school should have a *Career Information Cell*, equipped with career booklets, college prospectuses, internet-based career-search tools and a bulletin-board on which fresh career information is displayed every week.

2. **Appoint a trained guidance counsellor.** Every school should appoint at least *one trained guidance counsellor* (or share one with neighbouring schools on a part-time basis). This is one of the most direct, low-cost ways of strengthening career awareness.

3. **Organise an annual career-fair.** A *career-fair* should be held in the school every year, in which speakers from many occupations come and address the students. Special care should be taken to invite *equal* numbers of male and female speakers.

4. **Strengthen the laboratory and library facilities.** Adequate laboratory and library facilities are necessary if the *Scientific* and *Literary* interests of the students are to be developed.

5. **Provide art, music, drama and sports rooms.** *Artistic*, *Persuasive* and *Social* interests develop best when there are dedicated rooms and dedicated periods for the related activities.

6. **Co-ordinate with the Department of Education.** The Principal should co-ordinate with the District Education Officer and the *District Institute of Education and Training* (D.I.E.T.) for periodic guidance-related orientation programmes.

---

## 5.8 SUGGESTIONS FOR GUIDANCE COUNSELLORS

The role of the *guidance counsellor* in implementing the findings of the present study is of *crucial* importance. The following suggestions are offered for guidance counsellors:

1. **Use the V.I.R. as a starting point, not as a final answer.** The V.I.R. score of a student is a *starting point* for personal counselling. The counsellor should sit with the student, discuss the profile, and ask probing questions about *why* the student likes certain areas.

2. **Always interpret the profile with the student, never *for* him.** The counsellor should *never* tell the student "you are a Scientific type" or "you are a Social type". Instead, he should *help the student to discover* his own pattern through guided discussion.

3. **Pay special attention to the gender-non-conforming profiles.** Some boys will show high *Artistic*, *Social* or *Household* interest; some girls will show high *Scientific*, *Constructive* or *Agricultural* interest. These students need *special encouragement* — they will face social pressure from their families and peers to *change* their interests, and the counsellor must give them the strength to *follow their genuine interest*.

4. **Maintain a confidential record.** The V.I.R. profile and the counselling notes of every student should be kept *strictly confidential*.

5. **Conduct group-counselling sessions.** Apart from individual counselling, the counsellor should organise *group-counselling* sessions during which the entire class discusses careers, watches career-related videos and meets professionals.

6. **Build a referral network.** The counsellor should build a referral network of *local professionals* (doctors, engineers, lawyers, teachers, artists, social workers, businessmen, farmers, computer experts) who are willing to talk to the school students.

## 5.9 SUGGESTIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

The present study has been delimited in several respects. Many *related* questions have remained unanswered. The following suggestions are offered for future researchers:

1. **A larger sample.** The study may be replicated on a much larger sample (say, 600 to 1,000 students) drawn from a number of schools spread over different blocks of Meerut District.

2. **Wider geographical coverage.** A similar study may be conducted simultaneously in several districts of Western U.P. (Meerut, Muzaffarnagar, Saharanpur, Ghaziabad and Bulandshahr) to compare the vocational interest pattern of the secondary students of the entire region.

3. **Inclusion of additional independent variables.** *Birth order*, *number of siblings*, *family income*, *parental occupation*, *parental education*, *intelligence quotient*, *personality type* and *academic achievement* are some of the additional variables that may be related to vocational interests. A future study may include these.

4. **A longitudinal follow-up.** It will be highly instructive to *follow-up* the present 80 students for two years and see whether their *actual stream choice* at +2 stage matches their *measured vocational interests* at the secondary stage.

5. **Comparison with other tools.** A future study may simultaneously administer the *V.I.R.* of Kulshrestha, *Holland's Self-Directed Search* and the *Strong Vocational Interest Blank*, and compare the findings of all the three tools on the same sample.

6. **Construction of an updated tool.** The present V.I.R. is an excellent tool but the world of work has *changed* substantially since its standardisation. New occupations such as *software engineering, data science, digital marketing, YouTube content creation, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, food technology and event management* should be incorporated into the tool. A future research project may attempt the *standardisation of an updated tool* covering these emerging fields.

7. **Effect of career-guidance interventions.** An *experimental* study may be conducted to find out the *effect of a year-long career-guidance programme* on the vocational interest pattern of secondary school students.

8. **Religion-wise comparison.** A future study may compare students belonging to different religious / community groups to find out whether their vocational interests differ.

9. **Co-educational vs single-sex schools.** A future study may compare the vocational interests of students of *co-educational* schools with those of *single-sex* schools to find out whether the school environment moderates the gender-effect.

10. **Vocational maturity studies.** Apart from vocational *interest*, the study of *vocational maturity* (Crites' concept) is also important. Future research may include both these concepts in a single study.

## 5.10 LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

In all honesty, the investigator records the following *limitations* of the present study, which the reader is requested to keep in mind while interpreting the findings:

1. **Small sample.** The sample of 80 students, although carefully drawn through random sampling, is *small* in relation to the population of about 1,55,000 secondary school students of Meerut District. Generalisations should, therefore, be made cautiously.

2. **Only two schools.** The two schools studied — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, and K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh — are taken to be *representative* of the secondary schools of Meerut District. The investigator has *not been able* to administer the test in additional schools to cross-validate the findings.

3. **Self-report tool.** The V.I.R. is a *self-report* tool. A student who, for one reason or the other, gives *socially desirable* responses rather than truthful responses may distort the data. Although the investigator made strong efforts to assure the students that there are no right or wrong answers, the possibility of *self-report bias* cannot be entirely ruled out.

4. **Limited independent variables.** The study has examined the effect of only one principal independent variable — *gender* — and two secondary variables — *class* and *school*. Many other potentially important variables — birth order, family income, parental occupation, parental education, intelligence and academic achievement — have not been examined.

5. **Cross-sectional design.** The data have been collected at *one point of time* (November 2025). No *longitudinal* follow-up has been done. The effect of *time* on the vocational interest pattern of the same students has, therefore, remained unstudied.

6. **No control variables.** The present design does not statistically control for *socio-economic status*, *intelligence* or *family type*. Although the random sampling has spread these variables equally across boys and girls, their *individual* effects have not been studied.

7. **Hindi-English language difference.** The V.I.R. is a Hindi tool. The K.P. International School students are in English medium. A small number of K.P. students may have found the Hindi vocabulary of one or two items unfamiliar, although the investigator made every effort to clarify the meanings of any difficult words.

8. **One district only.** The study is delimited to *Meerut District*. Findings may, in some respects, be specific to Meerut and may not directly generalise to other districts of U.P. or to other states.

These limitations notwithstanding, the present study is a *modest but useful* contribution to the slowly growing body of Indian research on the vocational interests of secondary school adolescents.

---

## 5.11 FINAL CONCLUSION

The present investigation, conducted on a balanced sample of 80 secondary school students (40 boys + 40 girls) of Meerut District, has clearly shown that *gender remains a powerful and statistically significant factor* in the shaping of vocational interests at the secondary stage. Out of the ten areas of vocational interest measured by the *V.I.R.* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha, the difference between boys and girls was found to be significant on *eight* areas. Boys were higher on the *Scientific, Executive, Constructive (Technical), Agricultural* and *Persuasive* areas; girls were higher on the *Artistic, Social* and *Household* areas. The *Literary* and the *Commercial* areas showed no significant gender differences.

The study, accordingly, supports the long-standing finding of Indian and foreign research that *boys prefer working with things and girls prefer working with people* — the *People–Things* dimension of Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009).

The findings of the study underline the *urgent need* for *gender-sensitive* career-guidance services in the secondary schools of Meerut District. Both girls (in S.T.E.M., Technical and Leadership areas) and boys (in Artistic, Social and Household areas) need to be *gently encouraged* to broaden their vocational horizons beyond the traditional gender-stereotyped pattern. With the careful, sustained efforts of *teachers*, *parents*, *school administrators* and *guidance counsellors*, the gender-based occupational segregation of the Indian work-force can be gradually reduced, leading ultimately to a more *equal*, *just* and *productive* society.
