## 5.1 SUMMARY OF THE STUDY

A research investigation cannot be considered complete unless its main features are *summarised* in a brief and connected form, and unless its findings are converted into *conclusions*, *implications* and *suggestions for further work*. The present chapter is, therefore, devoted to the same. It is divided into the following six sections:

- 5.1 Summary of the Study.
- 5.2 Major Findings.
- 5.3 Conclusions.
- 5.4 Educational Implications.
- 5.5 Suggestions for Further Research.
- 5.6 Limitations of the Study.

The present investigation was undertaken to study and to compare the *vocational interest pattern* of the secondary school students of two contrasting types of schools of Meerut district. The study was carried out under the academic jurisdiction of the **Department of Education, Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut**, during the M.Ed. session 2025–2026. The main features of the study are summarised below.

### 5.1.1 Title of the Study

> **"A Comparative Study of Vocational Interests of Secondary School Students of Rural Government and Urban Private Schools of Meerut District."**

### 5.1.2 Need and Significance

Adolescence is the crucial period during which the boy or girl first thinks seriously about his / her future occupation. The Class IX – Class X stage in Indian schools coincides with this exploratory period of vocational thinking. The choices made at this stage have lifelong effects. Hence, the *systematic measurement* of vocational interests of secondary school students is both an educational and a social necessity. Two of the most important factors that influence vocational interests are (a) *type of locality* — rural or urban — and (b) *type of management* — government or private. The present study has selected two schools that represent the two extremes of these two factors put together — one *Rural Government* and one *Urban Private CBSE* — and has compared their students.

### 5.1.3 Objectives

The study was planned with the following five major objectives:

1. To study the area-wise vocational interest pattern of the total sample (N = 60) on the ten areas of the V.I.R. by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha.
2. To find out the percentage distribution of high, average and low levels of vocational interest of the total sample, and of the various sub-groups, on each of the ten areas.
3. To compare the vocational interests of the boys (n = 30) and the girls (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.
4. To compare the vocational interests of the Rural Government school students (n = 30) and the Urban Private school students (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.
5. To compare the vocational interests of the Class IX students (n = 30) and the Class X students (n = 30) on each of the ten areas.

### 5.1.4 Hypotheses

Three null hypotheses were formulated:

> H₀1 — There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

> H₀2 — There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of Rural Government and Urban Private school students on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

> H₀3 — There is no significant difference between the mean vocational interest scores of Class IX and Class X students on any of the ten areas of the V.I.R.

### 5.1.5 Method, Sample and Tool

The study employed the *survey method* of educational research within a *descriptive comparative design*. The sample of **60 students** (30 boys + 30 girls) was selected through the *stratified random sampling* technique from two schools of Meerut district — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur (Rural Government) and K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh (Urban Private CBSE) — with 7 boys + 8 girls of Class IX and 8 boys + 7 girls of Class X drawn from each school. The *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* developed and standardised by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** was used as the principal tool. It measures interest in ten well-defined areas — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household — on a 0 – 20 scale.

### 5.1.6 Statistical Techniques

The data collected were analysed by means of the following four statistical techniques:

- *Mean (M)* — to find the average level of interest of each group on each area.
- *Standard Deviation (S.D.)* — to find the variability of the scores around the mean.
- *t-test for two independent samples* — to test the three null hypotheses.
- *Percentages* — to show the distribution of the students into Low, Average and High levels of interest in each area.

### 5.1.7 Procedure

The investigator obtained formal permission from the Principals of the two schools, procured the V.I.R. booklets from the National Psychological Corporation, Agra, and personally administered the test to the selected students in two single-day visits — one to each school — during the first week of November 2025. The response rate was 100 per cent. The answer sheets were scored manually using the scoring key, and the data were analysed in *Microsoft Excel 2019* and re-verified in *S.P.S.S. Version 23.0*.

## 5.2 MAJOR FINDINGS

The major findings of the study, as drawn from Chapter IV, are presented below in a numbered list. The findings have been grouped by *area of interest* for ready reference.

### 5.2.1 Findings Relating to the Total Sample

1. The *most preferred* areas of the total sample are **Social** (M = 12.78), **Scientific** (M = 12.50) and **Artistic** (M = 12.22).

2. The *least preferred* areas are **Persuasive** (M = 11.17) and **Commercial** (M = 11.43).

3. The *largest high-interest* group is found in the **Social** area — 45.0 per cent of the students score in the *high* range (score 14 – 20) on this area.

4. The other areas in which more than 30 per cent of the students fall in the *high* range are **Scientific** (35.0 %), **Executive** (31.7 %) and **Household** (31.7 %).

5. The *largest low-interest* group is found in the **Commercial** area — 15.0 per cent of the students score in the *low* range.

6. The standard deviations on the ten areas range narrowly from 2.49 (Agricultural) to 3.24 (Scientific), showing that the *spread* of scores around the mean is *roughly comparable* across the ten areas.

### 5.2.2 Findings Relating to Boys and Girls (H₀1)

7. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Scientific** area (Boys M = 13.60 vs. Girls M = 11.40; *t* = 2.77, p < .01).

8. **Boys** scored *significantly higher* than girls on the **Constructive** area (Boys M = 13.10 vs. Girls M = 10.90; *t* = 3.78, p < .01).

9. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Literary** area (Girls M = 12.57 vs. Boys M = 11.00; *t* = 2.31, p < .05).

10. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Social** area (Girls M = 13.90 vs. Boys M = 11.67; *t* = 3.70, p < .01).

11. **Girls** scored *significantly higher* than boys on the **Household** area (Girls M = 13.17 vs. Boys M = 10.83; *t* = 3.69, p < .01).

12. The differences between boys and girls on the **Executive**, **Commercial**, **Artistic**, **Agricultural** and **Persuasive** areas were *not statistically significant*.

13. The null hypothesis **H₀1** is, therefore, *rejected* on five of the ten areas and *retained* on the remaining five.

14. The percentage analysis reveals that **boys** have substantially more *high-interest* representation than girls on **Constructive** (40.0 % vs. 6.7 %), **Scientific** (46.7 % vs. 23.3 %), **Executive** (40.0 % vs. 23.3 %) and **Commercial** (30.0 % vs. 10.0 %) areas; **girls** have substantially more *high-interest* representation than boys on **Social** (66.7 % vs. 23.3 %), **Household** (46.7 % vs. 16.7 %) and **Literary** (36.7 % vs. 20.0 %) areas.

### 5.2.3 Findings Relating to Rural Government vs Urban Private Students (H₀2)

15. **Urban Private** school students scored *significantly higher* than Rural Government school students on the **Scientific** area (UP M = 13.53 vs. RG M = 11.47; *t* = 2.58, p < .05).

16. **Urban Private** school students scored *significantly higher* than Rural Government students on the **Executive** area (UP M = 13.57 vs. RG M = 10.80; *t* = 4.55, p < .01).

17. **Urban Private** school students scored *significantly higher* than Rural Government students on the **Commercial** area (UP M = 12.80 vs. RG M = 10.07; *t* = 4.96, p < .01).

18. **Urban Private** school students scored *significantly higher* than Rural Government students on the **Artistic** area (UP M = 13.67 vs. RG M = 10.77; *t* = 5.04, p < .01).

19. **Urban Private** school students scored *significantly higher* than Rural Government students on the **Persuasive** area (UP M = 12.20 vs. RG M = 10.13; *t* = 3.09, p < .01).

20. **Rural Government** school students scored *significantly higher* than Urban Private students on the **Constructive** area (RG M = 12.73 vs. UP M = 11.27; *t* = 2.36, p < .05).

21. **Rural Government** school students scored *significantly higher* than Urban Private students on the **Agricultural** area (RG M = 12.77 vs. UP M = 10.63; *t* = 3.66, p < .01).

22. **Rural Government** school students scored *significantly higher* than Urban Private students on the **Household** area (RG M = 13.10 vs. UP M = 10.90; *t* = 3.43, p < .01).

23. The differences between the two schools on the **Literary** and **Social** areas were *not statistically significant*.

24. The null hypothesis **H₀2** is, therefore, *rejected* on **eight of the ten areas** and *retained* only on two — *Literary* and *Social*.

25. The school effect, accordingly, is **the most powerful** of the three independent variables studied.

### 5.2.4 Findings Relating to Class IX vs Class X (H₀3)

26. **Class X** students scored *significantly higher* than Class IX students on the **Scientific** area (Class X M = 13.50 vs. Class IX M = 11.50; *t* = 2.49, p < .05).

27. **Class X** students scored *significantly higher* than Class IX students on the **Agricultural** area (Class X M = 12.47 vs. Class IX M = 10.93; *t* = 2.49, p < .05).

28. The differences between Class IX and Class X on the remaining eight areas — *Literary, Executive, Commercial, Constructive, Artistic, Persuasive, Social* and *Household* — were *not statistically significant*.

29. The null hypothesis **H₀3** is, therefore, *retained* on eight of the ten areas and *rejected* only on two.

30. The class effect, accordingly, is the *weakest* of the three independent variables studied. The vocational interest pattern is *fairly stable* over the two years of the secondary stage.

### 5.2.5 Findings Relating to Sub-group Comparisons

31. The school-effect among **boys** (RG-Boys vs. UP-Boys, n = 15 each) is significant on six areas — *Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive* and *Household*.

32. The school-effect among **girls** (RG-Girls vs. UP-Girls, n = 15 each) is significant on four areas — *Executive, Commercial, Artistic* and *Household*.

33. The school-effect, accordingly, operates on *both* genders, although it is somewhat more *broad-based* among boys.

## 5.3 CONCLUSIONS

On the basis of the major findings, the following four general conclusions are drawn:

1. **The type of school (Rural Government vs. Urban Private) is by far the most powerful factor influencing the vocational interests of secondary school students of Meerut district.** It produces significant differences on as many as eight of the ten areas of the V.I.R. The Urban Private school is consistently higher on the *modern* areas — Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Artistic and Persuasive — while the Rural Government school is consistently higher on the *traditional* areas — Constructive, Agricultural and Household. Only on the *Literary* and *Social* areas are the two schools roughly equal.

2. **Gender continues to be a significant factor in shaping vocational interests.** Even in the year 2025, traditional gender stereotypes persist on the most basic occupational dimensions — boys remain significantly higher on Scientific and Constructive areas, while girls remain significantly higher on Literary, Social and Household areas. However, the gender difference has *disappeared* on five other areas — Executive, Commercial, Artistic, Agricultural and Persuasive — pointing to a gradual *narrowing* of the gender gap in modern occupational attractions.

3. **Vocational interests are fairly stable across Class IX and Class X.** The two classes differ significantly only on Scientific and Agricultural areas. This shows that the basic vocational interest pattern is *already in place* by the time the student enters Class IX, and only a slight *deepening* of certain practical interests takes place during the next year.

4. **The most attractive single occupational area for the present sample, taken as a whole, is Social.** Forty-five per cent of the entire sample is found to be highly interested in this area. This is encouraging news for the *teaching*, *nursing*, *counselling* and *social-work* professions in Meerut district.

## 5.4 EDUCATIONAL IMPLICATIONS

The findings of the present study have important implications for educational policy and practice. The most important of these implications are listed below:

1. **Strengthening of Career Guidance Services in Rural Government Schools.** The Rural Government school is significantly *lower* than the Urban Private school on as many as five modern occupational areas. This reflects not the *absence of ability*, but the *absence of exposure*. The State Government should immediately strengthen the *career-guidance machinery* in rural government schools by appointing trained guidance teachers, distributing career-information booklets, and organising regular *career awareness assemblies*.

2. **Industry, Hospital and University Visits.** The rural students should be regularly taken to *factories*, *hospitals*, *banks*, *Information Technology parks*, *agricultural research stations* and *universities*, so that the world of modern occupations becomes *visible and tangible* to them.

3. **Equipping the Rural Government School with Modern Laboratories and Computer Rooms.** A great part of the difference in *Scientific* interest between the two schools is traceable to the *better laboratory facilities* of the urban private school. Adequate funds should be earmarked for science laboratories, computer labs, smart classrooms and a well-stocked library in every rural government school.

4. **Enrichment Programmes in the Urban Private School in Areas of Weakness.** The urban private school is significantly *lower* than the rural government school on the *Constructive*, *Agricultural* and *Household* areas. These areas are connected with *self-reliance*, *food security* and *home-making* — life skills that every Indian citizen needs. The urban private school should organise *kitchen gardening*, *carpentry workshops*, *cooking classes* and *farm visits* as part of its co-curricular programme.

5. **Bridging the Gender Gap on Scientific and Constructive Areas.** Girls of both schools are significantly lower than boys on the *Scientific* and *Constructive* areas. This should not be allowed to continue. Schools should organise *Women in S.T.E.M.* talks, invite women scientists, engineers and architects to address the girls, and encourage girls to take up *Mathematics-Physics-Biology-Computer-Science* courses at +2 stage. The example of women like Kalpana Chawla, Tessy Thomas, Ritu Karidhal and Sunita Williams should be regularly placed before the girls.

6. **Bridging the Gender Gap on Social and Household Areas (in the case of Boys).** Boys are significantly lower than girls on the *Social* and *Household* areas. This is equally undesirable. Boys should be encouraged to take up *social-service projects*, *household-management classes* and *cooking classes* in the school co-curricular programme. The image of *Bharat Ratna Mother Teresa* in social work, and the rising number of male chefs and male nurses, should be highlighted.

7. **Class-Wise Career Counselling.** Since the vocational interest pattern is already fairly settled by Class IX, *career counselling should begin in Class VIII* itself, and not be delayed till Class X or +2. Career awareness should be a regular component of the school's pastoral-care programme right from upper primary stage.

8. **Career Counsellor in Every Government School.** The state government should appoint a *full-time career counsellor* in every government secondary school, on the lines of the urban private schools. This is one of the most direct, low-cost ways of narrowing the rural-urban gap in vocational awareness.

9. **Parental Orientation.** Parents of rural-government-school students should be regularly oriented through *parent-teacher meetings* about the *new* occupational opportunities outside agriculture and trade. Without parental encouragement, the rural student remains tied to the traditional occupational mould.

10. **Career Information Cell.** Every secondary school should establish a *Career Information Cell*, equipped with *career booklets*, *prospectuses*, *internet-based career-search tools* and a *bulletin board* on which fresh career information is displayed every week.

11. **Identifying the Highly-Talented Few.** The present study has shown that 45 per cent of the sample is highly interested in *Social* occupations and 35 per cent in *Scientific* occupations. These large groups are the *human capital* of tomorrow's India and need *targeted enrichment*.

12. **Curricular Reforms.** The *Vocational Education* component of the secondary curriculum should be strengthened so that it offers each student at least *three different exposure courses* during Classes IX – X — one in a Scientific area, one in a Commercial area, and one in a Social-or-Artistic area. This will give every student a *taste* of the major occupational fields before he or she has to make the +2 stream choice.

13. **Periodic Re-administration of the V.I.R.** The *V.I.R.* should be administered to every Class IX student at the beginning of the academic session and the results discussed individually with the student and his / her parents. This single, low-cost step can dramatically improve the *quality of stream-choice* at the +2 stage.

## 5.5 SUGGESTIONS FOR FURTHER RESEARCH

The present study has been delimited in several respects. Many *related* questions have remained unanswered. The following ten suggestions are offered for further research:

1. **A larger sample.** The present study has been conducted on a sample of only 60 students drawn from two schools. A similar study may be repeated on a much larger sample (say, 600 to 1,000 students) drawn from a number of schools spread over different blocks of Meerut district. The findings of such a study will be more *generalisable*.

2. **Inclusion of Rural Private and Urban Government Schools.** The present study has looked only at the *two extreme* cells of the 2 × 2 management × locality design. A future study may include all the *four cells* — Rural Government, Rural Private, Urban Government and Urban Private — to draw a *complete* picture.

3. **A longitudinal follow-up study.** It will be highly instructive to *follow up* the present 60 students for two years and to see whether their *actual stream choice* at +2 stage matches their *measured vocational interests* at the secondary stage. This kind of *predictive validity* study is rare in India.

4. **Comparison across districts.** A similar study may be carried out simultaneously in several districts of Western U.P. (Meerut, Muzaffarnagar, Saharanpur, Ghaziabad and Bulandshahr) to find out whether the vocational interest pattern of the secondary student of *Western U.P.* is *uniform* or shows *district-wise variations*.

5. **Inclusion of additional independent variables.** *Birth order*, *number of siblings*, *family income*, *parental occupation*, *parental education*, *intelligence quotient* and *academic achievement* are some of the additional variables that may be related to vocational interests. A future study may include these.

6. **Boys' schools vs. Girls' schools vs. Co-educational schools.** The present study has been conducted on co-educational institutions. A future study may compare the vocational interest pattern of students of *separate-gender* schools with that of *co-educational* schools.

7. **Religion-wise comparison.** A future study may compare students belonging to different religious / community groups to find out whether their vocational interests differ.

8. **CBSE vs. ICSE vs. State Board comparison.** A future study may compare the vocational interest pattern of students of CBSE, ICSE and U.P. Board schools — all of whom are accessible in Meerut district — to find out whether the *board of affiliation* makes a significant difference.

9. **Construction of an updated Vocational Interest Tool.** The present *V.I.R.* of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha is an excellent tool, but the world of work has *changed* substantially since its standardisation. New occupations such as *software engineering*, *data science*, *digital marketing*, *YouTube content creation*, *artificial intelligence*, *biotechnology*, *food technology*, *event management*, etc., need to be incorporated into the tool. A future research project may attempt the *standardisation of an updated tool* covering these emerging fields.

10. **Effect of Career-Guidance Intervention.** An *experimental* study may be conducted to find out the *effect of a year-long career-guidance programme* on the vocational interest pattern of secondary school students. Such a study can directly test whether the rural-urban gap can be *narrowed* by intervention.

## 5.6 LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

In all honesty, the investigator records the following *limitations* of the present study, which the reader is requested to keep in mind while interpreting the findings:

1. **Small Sample.** The sample of 60 students, although carefully drawn through stratified random sampling, is *small* in relation to the population of 1,55,000 secondary school students of Meerut district. Generalisations should, therefore, be made cautiously.

2. **Only Two Schools.** The two schools studied — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, and K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh — are taken to be *representative* of the Rural Government and Urban Private CBSE categories of Meerut district. The investigator has *not been able* to administer the test in additional schools of the same categories to cross-validate the findings.

3. **Self-report Tool.** The V.I.R. is a *self-report* tool. A pupil who, for one reason or the other, gives *socially desirable* responses rather than truthful responses may distort the data. Although the investigator made strong efforts to assure the students that there are no *right* or *wrong* answers, the possibility of *self-report bias* cannot be entirely ruled out.

4. **Limited Independent Variables.** The study has examined the effects of only three independent variables — type of school, gender and class. Many other potentially important variables — birth order, family income, parental occupation, parental education, intelligence and academic achievement — have not been examined, although they may also influence vocational interests.

5. **Cross-sectional Design.** The data have been collected at *one point of time* (November 2025). No *longitudinal* follow-up has been done. The effect of *time* on the vocational interest pattern of these specific students has, therefore, remained unstudied.

6. **No Control Variables.** The present design does not control for some of the *background variables* — for example, the *socio-economic status* of the families of the two schools is *known* to be different, but this difference has not been *statistically controlled*.

7. **Hindi-English Linguistic Difference.** Although the V.I.R. is a Hindi tool that was made available in Hindi to all the students, the K.P. International School students study in *English medium*. A small number of K.P. students may have found the Hindi vocabulary slightly unfamiliar — although the investigator made every effort to clarify the meanings of any difficult words.

These limitations notwithstanding, the present study makes a *modest but useful contribution* to the slowly growing body of Indian research on vocational interests of secondary school students. It is hoped that the findings will be *useful* to the *Principals*, *Teachers*, *Counsellors* and *Parents* of Meerut district, and that they will encourage some readers to take up further investigations along the lines suggested in Section 5.5.
