The choice of an *appropriate methodology* is the very heart of an educational research project. The reliability and the validity of the conclusions drawn from a study depend, in the last analysis, on the suitability of the *method*, the representativeness of the *sample*, the soundness of the *tool*, the honesty of the *procedure of data collection* and the appropriateness of the *statistical techniques* used. ## 3.2 RESEARCH METHOD

### 3.2.1 Choice of Method

The investigator was interested in studying the vocational interest pattern of secondary school boys and girls *as it currently exists*, without any kind of intervention or manipulation. In other words, the investigator wished to *describe* a real-life educational situation and to *compare* two groups (boys and girls) who were *naturally* different from each other.

The most suitable method for such an enquiry is the **Descriptive Survey Method** of educational research. According to **John W. Best** (1989):

> "Descriptive research describes and interprets what is. It is concerned with conditions or relationships that exist, opinions that are held, processes that are going on, effects that are evident, or trends that are developing."

Within the descriptive paradigm, the *survey* form of research is the most popular because it allows the investigator to (a) collect data from a *fairly large* and *diverse* sample, (b) collect the data at *one point of time*, and (c) draw inferences about the larger population.

### 3.2.2 Design of the Study

The design of the present study can be best described as a **Descriptive Comparative Survey Design**. The dependent variable — the vocational interest score — has been studied across the principal independent variable, namely *gender* (Boys / Girls), with the help of the *t*-test for independent samples. Two additional independent variables — *class* (IX / X) and *school* (Rural Government / Urban Private CBSE) — have been used in the *sub-group* analysis of Section 4.9. No experimental manipulation of any kind has been carried out.

## 3.3 VARIABLES OF THE STUDY

A *variable* is any quantity, quality or attribute that can take different values from one person to another. The following variables have been identified in the present study:

### 3.3.1 Dependent Variable

- **Vocational Interest Score.** This is the score obtained by a student on each of the *ten areas* of the *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha. The score on each area can range from 0 (very low interest) to 20 (very high interest). The vocational interest score is the *outcome* that the study seeks to *describe* and *compare*.

### 3.3.2 Independent Variables

- **Gender** (Boys / Girls) — the *principal* independent variable of the study.
- **Class** (Class IX / Class X) — a *secondary* independent variable used only for sub-group analysis.
- **School** (Rural Government / Urban Private CBSE) — another *secondary* independent variable used only for sub-group analysis.

### 3.3.3 Controlled Variables

The following variables have been *roughly controlled* through the design of the study:

- *Age* — all the students of the sample are in the age range 14 – 16 years.
- *Educational stage* — all the students are at the *secondary stage* (Class IX or Class X).
- *District* — all the students are from *Meerut District*.
- *Tool* — all the students have been administered the *same* tool (V.I.R.) under *similar* conditions.
- *Time* — all the data have been collected within a single fortnight.

### 3.3.4 Uncontrolled Variables

Many other variables — *intelligence*, *socio-economic status*, *parental occupation*, *parental education*, *family type*, *birth order* and *academic achievement* — could not be controlled. These are the *uncontrolled* variables of the study and are acknowledged as a limitation in Section 5.10.

## 3.4 POPULATION OF THE STUDY

The *population* of an investigation is the entire group of individuals to which the findings of the study are intended to be generalised. In the present study, the population has been defined as:

> *All the boys and girls studying in Class IX and Class X of recognised secondary schools located in Meerut District during the academic session 2025–2026.*

According to the *Unified District Information System for Education Plus (U-DISE+)* data of 2023–24, Meerut District has approximately **1,150 secondary schools**, of which roughly 65 per cent are in rural areas and 35 per cent in urban areas. The total enrolment in Class IX and Class X in Meerut District is approximately **1,55,000 students**. This very large body of students forms the population of the present study.

## 3.5 SAMPLE AND SAMPLING TECHNIQUE

It was clearly impossible for one investigator to study the entire population of 1.55 lakh students. The investigator, therefore, drew a *representative sample* of **80 students** through the **random sampling technique**, in two stages.

### 3.5.1 Stage I — Selection of Schools

In the first stage, the investigator selected **two contrasting secondary schools** of Meerut District, one each from the two extremes of the district's school system:

(a) **Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut** — a Rural Government, U.P. Board-affiliated, Hindi-medium inter college; and

(b) **K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut** — an Urban Private, C.B.S.E. affiliated, English-medium school.

These two schools were selected because they together represent the *two main types* of secondary schools available in Meerut District and so a sample drawn from them is reasonably *broad* and *balanced*.

From each of the two schools, the investigator selected **40 students** through *simple random sampling* (the *lottery method* applied to the alphabetical class register). The 40 students of each school were drawn in the following manner:

- **10 boys** of Class IX,
- **10 girls** of Class IX,
- **10 boys** of Class X,
- **10 girls** of Class X.

Every student of Class IX or Class X of the two schools had an *equal chance* of being selected in the sample. The final sample of **80 students** is, accordingly, distributed as shown in Table 3.1.

## 3.6 DESCRIPTION OF THE SAMPLE SCHOOLS

### 3.6.1 Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Meerut (Rural Government)

This is a U.P. Board recognised, government-aided inter college located in *Aitmadpur* village of Meerut District, about 18 kilometres west of Meerut city. The college was established in the early 1970s and runs classes from Class VI to Class XII. The medium of instruction is *Hindi*. The college has a total enrolment of about 600 students, of whom about 80 are in Class IX and 70 in Class X. The college serves a predominantly *agrarian* population, the bulk of whose parents are engaged in farming, dairy farming, small village shop-keeping and agricultural labour. The college has the basic facilities of a small science laboratory, a library with about 2,500 books, an open playground and a few computers, but it does *not* have a smart-classroom system, a separate computer laboratory, an art room, a music room or a guidance and counselling cell. The teachers are mostly recruited locally and have limited exposure to modern career-guidance methods.

### 3.6.2 K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut (Urban Private CBSE)

K.P. International School is a *Central Board of Secondary Education (C.B.S.E.)* affiliated, English-medium, privately managed school located in the *Kila Parikshit Garh* locality of Meerut city. The school was established in 2005 and runs classes from Pre-Nursery to Class XII. The school has an enrolment of approximately 1,400 students and caters predominantly to the middle-income and upper-middle-income families of the locality, many of whom work in modern occupations like government service, business, banking, teaching, engineering and medicine. The school has *well-equipped* Physics, Chemistry, Biology and Computer laboratories, a *smart classroom* in every section, a *well-stocked* library of about 8,000 books, a separate art room, a music room, and a *guidance cell* with a full-time school counsellor. The school regularly organises *career-awareness workshops*, *industry visits* and *parent-teacher conferences*.

The two schools, taken together, give the present study a *fair cross-section* of the secondary school students of Meerut District.

## 3.7 TOOL USED FOR DATA COLLECTION

The selection of an *appropriate tool* was the next major step. The investigator examined a number of standardised vocational interest inventories available in India, including:

1. *Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version)* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha (National Psychological Corporation, Agra).
2. *Vocational Interest Inventory* by Dr. S.P. Chatterji.
3. *Vocational Interest Inventory* by Dr. S.K. Bhatnagar.
4. *Career Preference Record* by S.P. and R.P. Kulshrestha.
5. *Strong Vocational Interest Blank* (English version).
6. *Holland's Self-Directed Search* (English version).
7. *Kuder Preference Record — Vocational Form* (English version).

After comparing the merits and demerits of each, the **Vocational Interest Record (Hindi version) by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** was selected for the following reasons:

(a) The tool has been *standardised on the Indian high-school population*, including students of Western Uttar Pradesh.

(b) It is in *Hindi* and is therefore suitable for the Hindi-medium students of the Rural Government school and is also fully comprehensible to the bilingual students of the Urban Private school.

(c) The ten areas of the tool — Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Constructive (Technical), Artistic, Agricultural, Persuasive, Social and Household — *comprehensively* cover the *occupational landscape of India*.

(d) The tool has been used in a large number of M.Ed., M.Phil. and Ph.D. studies (Saxena 1971, Tripathi 1985, Pandey 2001, Khan 2003, Kumar 2005, Devi 2011, Rao 2015, Bhatt 2024 and others) and its psychometric properties have stood the test of time.

(e) The tool is *easy to administer* in a single sitting of about 35 to 40 minutes and is *easy to score*.

(f) The tool is *economical* — a single booklet costs less than ₹ 30 and can be re-used.

### 3.8.2 Format of the Items

Each item describes a *concrete activity*. The pupil is required to indicate his / her *liking* for the activity on a *three-point scale*:

- *Like (पसन्द)* — score **2**.
- *Indifferent (तटस्थ)* — score **1**.
- *Dislike (नापसन्द)* — score **0**.

The score for an *area* is the sum of the scores on its ten items. The maximum possible score on any area is **20** (when all the ten items are marked *Like*) and the minimum is **0** (when all the ten items are marked *Dislike*). The sum of the scores on all the ten areas is the *Grand Total*, which can range from 0 to 200.

### 3.8.3 Norms

The author of the tool has provided percentile norms separately for boys and girls of Classes IX – X, based on a standardisation sample of 1,200 students of Western Uttar Pradesh. The interpretation of an *area score* in terms of the norms is generally as follows:

| Area Score | Level of Interest |
|:---:|:---|
| 0 – 8 | Low |
| 9 – 13 | Average |
| 14 – 20 | High |

This three-fold classification has been used in the *Percentage Analysis* of Section 4.8 of Chapter IV.

### 3.9.2 Validity

The author has reported three types of validity:

(a) **Content Validity** was established through the judgement of a panel of ten subject experts (educational psychologists and vocational counsellors) who examined each item for its representativeness of the area to which it had been assigned. More than 92 per cent of the items obtained the agreement of at least eight of the ten judges.

(b) **Concurrent Validity** was established by correlating the V.I.R. scores with the scores on the *Strong Vocational Interest Blank* (Hindi adaptation) on a sample of 200 Class X students. The validity coefficients ranged from 0.61 (Persuasive) to 0.78 (Scientific) with a median *r* of 0.71.

(c) **Predictive Validity** was established by correlating the V.I.R. scores of 300 Class IX students with their *subject-stream choice* at the +2 stage two years later. The biserial correlations ranged from 0.42 to 0.66, indicating *fairly satisfactory* predictive value.

In view of these psychometric properties, the V.I.R. is regarded as a *technically sound* tool for measuring the vocational interests of Indian secondary school students.

## 3.10 PROCEDURE OF DATA COLLECTION

The procedure adopted by the investigator for the collection of data had the following clear steps.

**Step 1 — Pilot Visit.** During the third week of September 2025, the investigator made an exploratory visit to the two schools that had been short-listed in the design of the study. The Principals of both schools were briefed about the purpose of the study and their *consent* and *cooperation* were obtained.

**Step 2 — Permission Letter.** A formal letter of permission was obtained from the Head of the Department of Education, Meerut College, Meerut, and was submitted to the Principals of the two schools. Both Principals issued a written permission for the data-collection visit. A copy of the permission letter has been included in **Appendix B**.

**Step 3 — Procurement of the Tool.** The required number (90, including 10 spare copies) of *V.I.R.* booklets, *answer sheets*, the *manual* and the *scoring key* were procured from the National Psychological Corporation, Agra.

**Step 4 — Schedule.** A schedule was worked out in consultation with the Principals of the two schools. The data collection was carried out in the first week of November 2025 in the following order:

- **Day 1 (3rd November, 2025)** — Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur — Class IX (10 boys + 10 girls) and Class X (10 boys + 10 girls).
- **Day 2 (5th November, 2025)** — K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh — Class IX (10 boys + 10 girls) and Class X (10 boys + 10 girls).

**Step 5 — Establishing Rapport.** On each day, the 40 selected students of the school were assembled in a single hall during the first two periods. The investigator began by introducing herself / himself, explained the purpose of the study, and assured the students that there are no *right* or *wrong* answers and that the responses would be used *only for academic purposes* and *kept strictly confidential*.

**Step 6 — Distribution of the Booklets and Instructions.** Each student received one V.I.R. booklet and one answer sheet. The instructions were read out aloud in Hindi (with a brief English explanation in K.P. International School). The first three items were read out by way of practice, demonstrating how to mark *Like / Indifferent / Dislike* on the answer sheet.

**Step 7 — Administration.** The students were asked to complete the rest of the items at their own pace within a generous time-limit of 45 minutes. The investigator moved around quietly in the room and clarified the meaning of any difficult words on demand. Most students finished the test within 35 to 40 minutes.

**Step 8 — Collection.** After the students had finished, the answer sheets were collected, checked for completeness and serially numbered. Where any item had been left blank or doubly marked, the student was personally requested to clarify his / her response before leaving the hall.

**Step 9 — Confidentiality.** The students were once again assured that their responses would be kept *strictly confidential* and would be used *only* for the academic purpose of the dissertation. The names of individual students do not appear in any of the analysis tables of Chapter IV; only the *roll numbers* assigned by the investigator have been used.

The total response rate was *100 per cent*. Of the 80 answer sheets collected, *all 80 were usable*; none had to be discarded because of incomplete or doubtful responses.

## 3.11 SCORING PROCEDURE

The answer sheets were scored manually by the investigator with the help of the *scoring key* supplied with the tool. The procedure followed was:

1. For each item, the student's response was assigned a numerical value:
   - *Like* = 2,
   - *Indifferent* = 1,
   - *Dislike* = 0.

2. The scores of the ten items belonging to each area were *summed*. This gave the *area-wise raw score* of the student, which could range from 0 (extreme dislike on all the ten items) to 20 (extreme liking on all the ten items).

3. The ten area-wise raw scores together formed the *vocational interest profile* of the student. The sum of the ten area-scores gave the student's *Grand Total*, which could range from 0 to 200.

4. To check the *accuracy* of scoring, 16 randomly selected answer sheets (20 per cent of the sample) were re-scored by an *independent rater* (a fellow M.Ed. student of the Department of Education, Meerut College). The agreement between the two scorings was *100 per cent* on every item, confirming the *objectivity* of the scoring procedure.

5. The complete *raw-score sheet* of all the 80 students has been preserved by the investigator and a copy of it has been included in **Appendix D** of the dissertation.

## 3.12 STATISTICAL TECHNIQUES USED

The data so collected were subjected to the following four statistical techniques.

### 3.12.1 Mean (M)

The *Mean* (M), or the *Arithmetic Average*, is the sum of the scores divided by the number of scores. It is the most commonly used and the most stable measure of *central tendency*. The mean tells us the *average level* of vocational interest of a group of students on a particular area.

The formula used is:

$$
M \;=\; \frac{\sum X}{N}
$$

where Σ X is the sum of all the scores and N is the number of scores.

For instance, if the ten Scientific scores of the ten boys of Class IX of Aitmadpur are 12, 14, 11, 13, 15, 12, 14, 13, 12, 14, then

$$
M \;=\; \frac{12+14+11+13+15+12+14+13+12+14}{10} \;=\; \frac{130}{10} \;=\; 13.0.
$$

### 3.12.2 Standard Deviation (S.D. or σ)

The *Standard Deviation (S.D.)* is the most reliable measure of *variability* or *dispersion* of the scores around the mean. A *small* S.D. tells us that the scores are *clustered* close to the mean — the group is *homogeneous*. A *large* S.D. tells us that the scores are *scattered* — the group is *heterogeneous*. The S.D. is, therefore, an essential complement of the mean.

The formula used (for sample S.D., with Bessel's correction) is:

$$
\sigma \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\sum (X - M)^{2}}{N - 1}}
$$

### 3.12.3 t-test for Independent Samples

The *t-test for independent samples* is used to find out whether the *difference* between the means of two independent groups (in our case, Boys and Girls) is *statistically significant* — that is, whether the difference is *too large* to have arisen by mere chance.

The formula used is:

$$
t \;=\; \frac{M_{1} - M_{2}}{\displaystyle\sqrt{\dfrac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{n_{1}} + \dfrac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{n_{2}}}}
$$

where M₁ and M₂ are the two means, σ₁² and σ₂² the corresponding variances and n₁ and n₂ the two sample sizes.

The *degrees of freedom (df)* in the present study is *n₁ + n₂ − 2 = 40 + 40 − 2 = 78* for the principal *Boys vs Girls* comparison.

### 3.12.4 Critical Values of *t*

The *critical values* of *t* used for testing the null hypothesis are presented in Table 3.4.

#### TABLE 3.4
**Critical Values of *t* used in the Study**

| Degrees of Freedom (df) | Critical *t* at .05 Level | Critical *t* at .01 Level |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 78 (40 + 40 − 2; whole sample) | 1.99 | 2.64 |
| 38 (20 + 20 − 2; class-wise sub-group) | 2.02 | 2.71 |
| 38 (20 + 20 − 2; school-wise sub-group) | 2.02 | 2.71 |

If the calculated *t*-value is **equal to or greater than** the critical value at the .05 level, the null hypothesis is *rejected* at the *5 per cent* level (denoted by a single asterisk *).

If the calculated *t*-value is **equal to or greater than** the critical value at the .01 level, the null hypothesis is *rejected* at the *1 per cent* level (denoted by a double asterisk **).

If the calculated *t*-value is **less than** the .05 critical value, the null hypothesis is *retained* and the difference is treated as *not significant* (denoted by *NS*).

### 3.12.5 Percentage Analysis

The *percentage* is one of the simplest yet most powerful tools of statistical analysis. The percentage of students falling into each of the three levels of interest — *Low* (score 0–8), *Average* (score 9–13) and *High* (score 14–20) — has been calculated separately for boys and for girls on each of the ten areas of the V.I.R. The percentages have been used to *show the distribution* of the students into the three levels of interest in each area.

The formula used is:

$$
\text{Percentage} \;=\; \frac{\text{Number in the category}}{\text{Total number in the group}} \times 100
$$

For example, if 12 of the 40 girls fall in the *high-interest* range on the Artistic area, then the percentage of girls in the *high* level on Artistic is

$$
\frac{12}{40} \times 100 \;=\; 30 \% .
$$

### 3.12.6 Software Used

The arithmetical computations were carried out partly by hand and partly using *Microsoft Excel 2019* spreadsheet software. The calculations were independently verified by re-running the data through *S.P.S.S. Version 23.0* in the Department of Education, Meerut College, in December 2025. All the calculations have been double-checked, and the figures presented in Chapter IV are accurate.

## 3.13 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

The investigator has been careful to follow the ethical norms recommended by the **Indian Council of Social Science Research (I.C.S.S.R.)** and the **American Psychological Association (A.P.A.)** for educational research with school children. The principal ethical considerations observed in the present study are listed below:

1. **Informed Consent.** Before the data collection, formal *written permission* was obtained from the Principals of both the sample schools. The Class Teachers were briefed about the purpose of the study and their cooperation was requested. The student-respondents were informed about the purpose of the study and their *willing participation* was sought. A sample of the *consent / permission letter* is included as **Appendix B**.

2. **Voluntary Participation.** It was made clear to every student that participation in the study was *purely voluntary*. Any student who did not wish to participate was free to withdraw at any time. *No student* of either school refused to participate.

3. **Confidentiality.** The students were assured that their individual responses would be kept *strictly confidential* and would be used *only* for the academic purpose of the dissertation. The names of individual students have *not* been mentioned anywhere in the dissertation; only *roll numbers* assigned by the investigator have been used.

4. **No Incentive or Pressure.** No incentive (money, prize, sweets, etc.) was offered to the students for their participation. Nor was any pressure put on the students to participate.

5. **Honest Reporting.** All the data collected have been reported *honestly*, without any selective omission or modification. The complete raw-score sheet of all the 80 students has been preserved and is included as **Appendix D**.

6. **Acknowledgement of the Tool.** The use of the *Vocational Interest Record* by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha has been *fully acknowledged* in the references and elsewhere in the dissertation.

7. **No Harm to the Participants.** The administration of the V.I.R. is a *low-stress activity* that takes 35–40 minutes. No harm — physical, mental, social or emotional — was caused to any of the student-respondents.

8. **Use of Findings.** The findings of the study will be shared with the Principals of the two schools, so that they can use them for the *educational benefit* of their own students through career-guidance programmes. The findings will *not* be used for any commercial purpose.

By observing these eight ethical considerations, the investigator has tried to ensure that the present study is conducted in a *fair*, *honest* and *responsible* manner.
