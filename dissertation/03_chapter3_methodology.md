## 3.1 Introduction

Methodology is the *systematic plan* by which a research problem is investigated, the data are collected, organised, processed and interpreted, and the conclusions are arrived at. As Kerlinger (1986) has observed, "*the design of an investigation is the architecture of the entire research enterprise*." A well-designed methodology, suited to the nature of the problem, lends *credibility, replicability and scientific status* to the findings; an ill-designed one renders the most laboriously collected data useless.

## 3.2 Method of the Study

Educational research, broadly speaking, is conducted through three principal methodological streams — the *historical* method, the *descriptive* method and the *experimental* method (Best & Kahn, 2017). The *historical* method studies events that took place in the past, the *experimental* method manipulates variables in order to establish cause-and-effect relationships, and the *descriptive* method studies events as they exist *in their natural setting*, without any deliberate intervention by the investigator.

Within the descriptive stream there are several variants — the *survey method*, the *case-study method*, the *correlational method*, the *causal-comparative* (ex post facto) method, and the *developmental* method. The investigator, after a careful consideration of the nature of the problem, has employed the *Descriptive Survey Method* in the present investigation.

A *descriptive survey* is a method by which a representative sample is studied with respect to one or more variables of interest, with the aim of describing the existing state of affairs and, where appropriate, of comparing groups within the sample. Mouly (1970) defined it as "*a method which is concerned with the present, what exists, what is being done, what is being expected of, and what trends are developing.*"

The descriptive survey method has been preferred for the present study because:

i. The present problem demands the *measurement and description* of vocational interests as they currently exist in two natural groups — secondary school boys and girls — without any experimental manipulation.

ii. The two groups can be *compared* on the ten standardised areas of the Vocational Interest Record using suitable inferential statistics (the *t*-test).

iii. The method is *practicable* within the time, financial and human resource limits available to an M.Ed. researcher.

iv. The method has been the most widely used method in similar Indian studies (Singh & Verma, 2023; Reddy & Kumar, 2021; Joshi, 2020; etc.), which makes the present findings comparable with the established literature.

## 3.3 Variables of the Study

A *variable* is any characteristic that can take more than one value across persons, objects, situations or time. Three principal types of variables operate in the present investigation:

**Independent Variable.** *Gender* of the secondary school student, with two levels — *Boys* and *Girls*.

**Dependent Variables.** The *vocational interest scores* of the student on the *ten* areas of Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record, namely (1) Literary, (2) Scientific, (3) Executive, (4) Commercial, (5) Constructive (Technical), (6) Artistic, (7) Agricultural, (8) Persuasive, (9) Social and (10) Household.

**Controlled Variables.** Certain background variables have been *controlled* through the choice of the sample — viz. age (13–16 years), grade level (Classes IX and X), district (Meerut), and academic session (2025–2026).

**Uncontrolled Variables.** Variables such as the *socio-economic status* of the family, the *educational background* of the parents, the level of *parental encouragement*, the *quality of teachers* and the *peer group climate* have not been measured in the present study, although they are known to influence vocational interest. These remain uncontrolled and, where relevant, have been mentioned among the *limitations* of the study.

## 3.4 Population of the Study

The *population* of any research study is the *total set* of individuals to whom the findings of the study are intended to apply. The population of the present investigation has been defined as:

> *All boys and girls of Classes IX and X studying in the secondary schools of Meerut District during the academic session 2025–2026.*

Meerut District has approximately 1,200 secondary schools (U.P. Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad-affiliated, CBSE-affiliated, ICSE-affiliated and aided), with an estimated 2.4 lakh students enrolled in Classes IX and X. From this large population a much smaller sample had to be drawn for the present investigation, in keeping with the practical constraints of an M.Ed. dissertation.

## 3.5 Sample and Sampling Technique

The *sample* of any descriptive survey is a *small subset* of the population that is taken to be representative of it. The sampling technique must ensure, as far as possible, that this small subset reflects the major characteristics of the larger population.

**Sample Size.** A total of **40 students** of Classes IX and X have been included in the sample of the present investigation — *20 boys* and *20 girls*. The sample size, although small, is consistent with the recommended minimum for an M.Ed. descriptive comparative study and is sufficient for the application of the *t*-test (Garrett & Woodworth, 1981).

**Sampling Technique.** The *Simple Random Sampling Technique* has been used. The procedure was as follows:

a. *Selection of schools.* From the list of secondary schools of Meerut District, two schools — one a Government rural U.P. Board school and the other a Private urban CBSE-affiliated school — were *purposively* selected, since the two schools represent maximally contrasting school environments. The Government rural school selected was *Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Kila Parikshit Garh* (for sampling boys); the Private urban CBSE school selected was *K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh* (for sampling girls).

b. *Selection of students within each school.* From the *complete enrolment list* of Classes IX and X (boys' or girls' side, as the case may be) provided by the Principal of each school, every student was assigned a serial number. Twenty serial numbers were then drawn at random by the *lottery method* (placing chits with serial numbers in a bowl, mixing thoroughly and drawing without replacement).

c. *Distribution by class.* Of the 20 boys, 10 belonged to Class IX and 10 to Class X. Of the 20 girls, 10 belonged to Class IX and 10 to Class X. This balance was deliberately maintained so that grade-level effects, if any, would be cancelled out across the two groups.

## 3.6 Description of the Sample Schools

The two schools from which the sample was drawn are described below in some detail, since the *school environment* is one of the contextual factors that shape adolescent vocational interest.

### 3.6.1 Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut

Shri Sanskrit Inter College, situated in the village of *Aitmadpur* in *Kila Parikshit Garh* tahsil of Meerut District, is a *Government-aided rural secondary school* affiliated with the *Uttar Pradesh Madhyamik Shiksha Parishad, Prayagraj*. Established more than four decades ago, the school imparts education from Class VI to Class XII through *Hindi medium* and follows the U.P. Board curriculum.

The school enrols approximately 600 students drawn predominantly from the agricultural families of Aitmadpur and the adjoining villages. The student community largely belongs to the *traditional rural occupational background* — agriculture, dairy, small construction work, masonry, carpentry, mechanical repair, tailoring and small-scale retail. A relatively small proportion of the parents are employed in the formal sector (school teaching, banking or government clerical jobs).

The infrastructure of the school is modest. There is a science laboratory, a small library, a few computer terminals (added under the Pradhan Mantri Schools for Rising India scheme) and a playground. Career-counselling and vocational guidance services are not yet available on a *systematic* basis.

The school has been included in the present sample as a representative *Government, rural, U.P.-Board* secondary school of Meerut District, and *boys* of Class IX and X were drawn from it.

### 3.6.2 K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut

K.P. International School, situated in the urban part of *Kila Parikshit Garh*, is a *Private CBSE-affiliated co-educational school* established in 2008 under a registered educational society. The school imparts education from Pre-Nursery to Class XII through *English medium* and follows the curriculum of the Central Board of Secondary Education, New Delhi.

The school enrols approximately 1,400 students drawn from a relatively *higher socio-economic background* — children of professionals, businessmen, government officers, doctors, engineers and senior salaried employees of Meerut city. A high proportion of the parents are graduates and post-graduates, and almost all parents place a strong emphasis on their children's higher education.

The infrastructure of the school is comparatively modern, with well-equipped science and computer laboratories, a smart-classroom system, a well-stocked library, art and music rooms, indoor and outdoor sports facilities, and a designated *career counselling cell* under the charge of a part-time trained counsellor. Career talks, school-level science exhibitions, art exhibitions and inter-school competitions are held at regular intervals.

The school has been included in the present sample as a representative *Private, urban, CBSE* secondary school of Meerut District, and *girls* of Class IX and X were drawn from it.

The deliberate selection of two schools situated at *opposite ends* of the locality and management continua provides a strong test of the gender-typed interest pattern: any gender difference that survives this maximum-contrast design can be regarded as a *genuinely robust* effect and not the artefact of a homogeneous sample.

## 3.7 Tool Used for Data Collection

The tool used for the measurement of the vocational interests of the sample students is the *Vocational Interest Record (V.I.R.) — Hindi version*, developed and standardised by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** of the Department of Education, Agra University, and published by the *National Psychological Corporation, Agra*. The Record is one of the most widely used Indian vocational interest tools and has been employed in over 200 published studies and dissertations (Asthana, 1973; Yadav, 2016; Mehta, 2019; Joshi, 2020; Reddy & Kumar, 2021; Gupta & Saxena, 2021; Pandey & Tripathi, 2022; Singh & Verma, 2023; etc.).

## 3.8 Description of the Vocational Interest Record by Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha

### 3.8.1 Structure of the Record

The Record consists of *90 items* arranged on a forced-choice basis. Each item contains *two activities* (one belonging to one interest area and the other to a different interest area), and the testee is required to select the one he or she prefers. The 90 items have been so framed that *each of the ten areas appears 18 times* (9 times as the "left-hand" activity and 9 times as the "right-hand" activity). The maximum possible score in any one area is therefore *18*.

The *ten areas* covered by the Record, with brief operational descriptions, are:

**Table 3.2 — Number of Items in Each Area of the Vocational Interest Record**

| Sl. No. | Area | Brief Description of the Area | Items in the Record | Max. Possible Score |
|:---:|:---|:---|:---:|:---:|
| 1 | Literary | Reading, writing, journalism, teaching of language | 18 | 18 |
| 2 | Scientific | Research, observation, experimentation, medicine, engineering | 18 | 18 |
| 3 | Executive | Management, administration, planning, leadership | 18 | 18 |
| 4 | Commercial | Trade, business, finance, banking, accountancy | 18 | 18 |
| 5 | Constructive (Technical) | Working with tools, machines, materials | 18 | 18 |
| 6 | Artistic | Painting, music, dance, design | 18 | 18 |
| 7 | Agricultural | Farming, horticulture, animal husbandry, outdoor work | 18 | 18 |
| 8 | Persuasive | Marketing, sales, public relations, law, politics | 18 | 18 |
| 9 | Social | Teaching, nursing, social work, child care | 18 | 18 |
| 10 | Household | Cooking, home management, dietetics, hospitality | 18 | 18 |

**Total items in the booklet** : *Each item appears in two areas, so 90 items yield 18 entries per area (10 × 18 / 2 = 90 items).*

### 3.8.2 Administration

The Record is *self-administering*. It can be administered individually or in a group, and the recommended group size for school administration is 30 to 50 students at a time. The recommended *time limit* is *35 minutes* (5 minutes for instructions and 30 minutes for the test proper), although the test is essentially a *power test* and not a *speed test*. Most students of Class IX and X complete it in 25 to 30 minutes.

### 3.8.3 Materials Required

The materials supplied by the publisher comprise (a) a *Test Booklet* containing the 90 items printed in Devanagari, (b) a *Separate Answer Sheet* on which the testee marks his or her preference, (c) a *Hand Scoring Stencil* for rapid hand scoring, and (d) a *Manual* containing the norms, reliability and validity data and the interpretation guidelines.

### 3.8.4 Scoring

For *each item*, the activity selected by the testee receives a score of *1*, and the rejected activity receives *0*. The *area-wise total* is obtained by counting the number of times the activities of that area were selected. Since each area appears in 18 items, the *area-wise score* ranges from *0 to 18*. The ten area-scores together constitute the testee's *vocational interest profile*.

### 3.8.5 Interpretation

The interpretation of the area-scores is based on the *percentile norms* provided in the Manual for Indian secondary school students. For practical purposes Kulshrestha recommends classifying the scores into three broad levels:

| Level of Interest | Score Range | Interpretation |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| Low | 0 – 5 | Little spontaneous attraction towards vocations of this area |
| Average | 6 – 11 | Mild to moderate liking for vocations of this area |
| High | 12 – 18 | Strong liking; vocations of this area should be considered seriously in career planning |

These three levels have also been used in the *percentage analysis* presented in Chapter IV.

## 3.9 Reliability and Validity of the Tool

### 3.9.1 Reliability

The *reliability* of the Vocational Interest Record has been established by the author through both the *test-retest* method and the *split-half* method. The reliability coefficients reported in the Manual are reproduced in Table 3.3.

**Table 3.3 — Reliability Coefficients of the Vocational Interest Record (as reported in the Manual)**

| Method | Sample (N) | Time Gap | Reliability Coefficient (r) |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Test-retest | 200 | 4 weeks | 0.85 |
| Test-retest | 150 | 8 weeks | 0.79 |
| Split-half (odd-even) | 250 | – | 0.83 |
| Spearman-Brown corrected | 250 | – | 0.91 |
| Cronbach's alpha (reported in later validation studies) | 300 | – | 0.87 |

The reliability coefficients are uniformly *high* and indicate that the Record measures vocational interest with good *consistency* and *stability*.

### 3.9.2 Validity

The *validity* of the Record has been established by the author through:

a. **Content Validity.** The 90 items were drawn up after a thorough study of Indian occupational profiles and after consultation with subject experts, vocational counsellors and senior school teachers. Items that did not meet the inter-judge agreement criterion of 0.80 were dropped.

b. **Construct Validity.** Factor analysis confirmed the *ten-factor* structure of the Record on a sample of 600 secondary school students.

c. **Concurrent Validity.** The area-scores of the Record were correlated with the area-scores of the *Comprehensive Interest Schedule* of Vohra and the *Bell-Adjustment Inventory*, with correlations ranging from 0.62 to 0.81.

d. **Predictive Validity.** A follow-up study (conducted by the publisher five years after the standardisation) showed that 70 per cent of the testees were employed in occupations broadly consistent with the area in which they had scored highest in the Record.

The reliability and validity data, taken together, place the *Vocational Interest Record* among the *best-established Indian instruments* for the measurement of vocational interest, and fully justify its use in the present investigation.

## 3.10 Procedure of Data Collection

The data of the present study were collected by the investigator in person, in two phases corresponding to the two sample schools. The procedure was as follows.

**Step 1 — Permission.** A formal *Permission Letter* was obtained from the Head, Department of Education, Meerut College, requesting the Principals of the two sample schools to grant the investigator the necessary permission for data collection. (A copy of the Permission Letter is enclosed at *Appendix B*.)

**Step 2 — Establishing rapport.** Before the actual administration of the test, the investigator visited each of the two schools, met the Principal and the class teachers, explained the purpose of the study and the nature of the test, and assured them of the confidentiality of the responses and of the wholly *non-evaluative* nature of the data.

**Step 3 — Selecting the sample.** The complete enrolment lists of Classes IX and X were obtained from the school office. Twenty serial numbers were drawn at random by the lottery method (as described in Section 3.5).

**Step 4 — Administration.** The Vocational Interest Record was administered to the selected students in a quiet classroom during a regular school period. The investigator, who had familiarised herself / himself with the standard instructions given in the Manual, distributed the booklets and answer sheets and read out the instructions in *Hindi* (which is the home language of the rural sample) and a paraphrase in *English* (for the CBSE sample). Specimen items 1 and 2 were worked out on the blackboard. Doubts of the students were clarified, after which the actual testing began.

**Step 5 — Time.** The students completed the Record in 25 to 35 minutes. In line with the Manual, no rigid time limit was enforced — the Record being a power test rather than a speed test. The investigator went round the class quietly to make sure that all students were proceeding correctly and that there was no copying or consultation.

**Step 6 — Collection.** At the end of the testing the booklets and answer sheets were collected, the students were thanked and a small gesture of appreciation (a chocolate / pencil) was given as a token of thanks.

**Step 7 — Schedule.** The administration was carried out during the period from *15 January 2026 to 28 February 2026*, in the second half of the academic session, when the Class IX and X students were sufficiently settled into their school routine.

## 3.11 Scoring Procedure

The 40 answer sheets thus collected were scored manually with the help of the *Hand Scoring Stencil* supplied with the Record. The procedure was as follows:

(i) The investigator placed the Stencil over the answer sheet and matched the punched holes against the responses given by the testee.

(ii) For each of the ten areas, the number of times the testee had selected the activity belonging to that area was counted. This number — ranging from 0 to 18 — became the *raw score* of that area.

(iii) The ten raw scores were entered in the *Master Score Sheet* (a copy of which is enclosed in *Appendix C*).

(iv) Approximately 10 per cent of the answer sheets (4 sheets selected at random) were independently re-scored by a second M.Ed. colleague to check for scoring reliability. The agreement was over 99 per cent, and the few isolated disagreements were resolved by joint re-examination of the disputed items.

(v) The Master Score Sheet thus prepared formed the basis of all subsequent statistical analysis (Chapter IV).

## 3.12 Statistical Techniques Used

In keeping with the descriptive survey design, the following statistical techniques were employed:

### 3.12.1 Mean (M)

The arithmetic *Mean* is the most commonly used measure of *central tendency*. For each of the ten areas, the mean score of the boys' group and of the girls' group has been computed by the formula:

$$M = \dfrac{\sum X}{N}$$

where *ΣX* = the sum of all the raw scores in that area for the group, and *N* = number of testees in the group (here, 20).

### 3.12.2 Standard Deviation (S.D., σ)

The *Standard Deviation* is the most reliable measure of *dispersion* (variability). For each of the ten areas, the S.D. of the boys' group and of the girls' group has been computed by the formula:

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum (X - M)^{2}}{N - 1}}$$

where *(X − M)* is the deviation of each raw score from the group mean and *(N − 1)* is the unbiased estimator of the population variance. (The investigator has used the *unbiased* (sample) estimator throughout, as recommended by Garrett & Woodworth, 1981.)

### 3.12.3 t-test for Two Independent Means

The *t*-test of significance has been used to determine whether the difference between the mean scores of the boys' group and the girls' group on each of the ten areas is statistically significant or whether it could plausibly have arisen by chance from the operation of sampling fluctuations.

The formula used is the standard *t*-test for the difference between two independent means:

$$t = \dfrac{M_{1} - M_{2}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{(N_{1} - 1)\sigma_{1}^{2} + (N_{2} - 1)\sigma_{2}^{2}}{N_{1} + N_{2} - 2} \times \left(\dfrac{1}{N_{1}} + \dfrac{1}{N_{2}}\right)}}$$

where *M₁* and *M₂* are the means of boys and girls; *σ₁* and *σ₂* are their standard deviations; and *N₁* = *N₂* = 20.

The *degrees of freedom* are *df = N₁ + N₂ − 2 = 20 + 20 − 2 = 38*.

The *critical values* of *t* at *df* = 38 are:

| Level of Significance | Critical t |
|:---|:---:|
| 0.05 | 2.024 |
| 0.01 | 2.711 |
| 0.001 | 3.566 |

A computed *t*-value equal to or greater than these critical values indicates that the difference between the two means is significant at the corresponding level. A *t*-value less than 2.024 indicates that the null hypothesis of "no difference" cannot be rejected.

### 3.12.4 Percentage Analysis

In addition to the parametric inferential procedure described above, *Percentage Analysis* has been carried out to obtain a more readable picture of the comparative profile. Each testee's score in each area has been classified into one of three levels — *Low* (0–5), *Average* (6–11) or *High* (12–18) — and the *percentage* of boys and of girls falling at each level has been computed. The formula used is:

$$\text{Percentage} = \dfrac{f}{N} \times 100$$

where *f* is the frequency of testees in the level under consideration and *N* is the size of the group (boys = 20 or girls = 20).

### 3.12.5 Rank-Order Analysis

For *Objective 5* of the study, the ten areas of vocational interest were *ranked* separately for boys and for girls in the descending order of mean scores (highest mean = Rank 1). The two rank orders were then compared item-by-item and, where appropriate, the *Spearman Rank-Order Correlation Coefficient (ρ)* was computed:

$$\rho = 1 - \dfrac{6 \sum d^{2}}{n (n^{2} - 1)}$$

where *d* is the difference between the two ranks for each area and *n* = 10 (the number of areas).

## 3.13 Ethical Considerations

The investigator has scrupulously observed the following ethical requirements:

i. *Informed permission* of the Principals of both schools, and of the parents of the students wherever required by the school, was obtained before testing.

ii. The testees were *fully informed* about the purpose of the study and the nature of the test, and were assured that their responses would be used *only for academic research* and would not affect their school standing in any way.

iii. The students were given the *right to withdraw* at any stage, although in fact none chose to do so.

iv. The *anonymity and confidentiality* of the students were maintained. In the Master Score Sheet (Appendix C) the students are referred to by serial numbers and not by name.

v. The investigator has *honestly* reported the data without any fabrication, falsification or selective omission.

vi. All the secondary sources cited in the dissertation have been *properly acknowledged* in APA 7th edition style in the bibliography.

## 3.14 Limitations of the Methodology

While every effort has been made to design a methodologically sound study, the following limitations of the methodology must be acknowledged in advance:

i. The *sample size* (N = 40) is small, and although it is adequate for the *t*-test, the generalisation of the findings to all secondary school students of Meerut District must be made with caution.

ii. The two groups (boys and girls) have been drawn from *different schools* — a Government rural U.P. Board school and a Private urban CBSE school. Although this maximum-contrast design is methodologically defensible (Section 3.6) and is similar to the "comparison-of-extremes" design recommended by Best & Kahn (2017), it does mean that the *gender* effect cannot be entirely separated from the *school type* effect. Strictly speaking, the present study compares "*Government-rural-U.P.-Board boys versus Private-urban-CBSE girls*" rather than "boys versus girls" *in general*. The investigator has explicitly acknowledged this in the *Limitations* section of Chapter V.

iii. Only *one tool* — the Vocational Interest Record — has been used. Although the Record is well standardised, a multi-instrument assessment (e.g., Record plus Holland's Self-Directed Search) would have yielded a richer picture.

iv. The study is a *cross-sectional* one, conducted at a single point in time. A longitudinal design tracking the same students from Class IX to Class XII would have been preferable, but is beyond the time-scale of an M.Ed. dissertation.

v. *Background variables* such as parental education, socio-economic status and parental encouragement — known from earlier research to influence vocational interest — have not been measured in the present study.

vi. The *statistical analysis* has been limited to Mean, S.D., *t*-test and Percentage Analysis. More sophisticated procedures (ANOVA, factor analysis, multiple regression) have not been applied.

These limitations notwithstanding, the methodology is considered adequate for the kind of *exploratory comparative description* that the present M.Ed. study sets out to provide.
