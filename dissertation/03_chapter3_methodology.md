## 3.1 Introduction

Research methodology is the backbone of any scientific investigation. It is the systematic framework that guides the researcher in collecting, organising, and analysing data in a manner that is logical, replicable, and academically rigorous. A well-designed methodology ensures that the findings of a study are trustworthy, valid, and practically meaningful. In the words of **John W. Best (1977)**, "Research methodology is the systematic and objective analysis and recording of controlled observations that may lead to the development of generalizations, principles, or theories resulting in prediction and possibly ultimate control of events."

The present chapter provides a detailed and transparent description of the entire methodological framework adopted in this study. It covers the research method, variables, population, sample, sampling technique, the tool used (including its construction, dimensions, reliability, validity, and scoring), the procedure of data collection — including a candid account of the fieldwork experience — the statistical techniques employed, and the ethical considerations observed throughout the research process.

A transparent and thorough methodology section is especially important in educational research, where the quality of human interaction during data collection can significantly affect the reliability and authenticity of the data. Every procedural decision described in this chapter was made deliberately, with the twin objectives of maximizing scientific rigour and minimizing disruption to the participating schools and students.

---

## 3.2 Research Method

The present study employs the **Descriptive Survey Method** of educational research.

The descriptive survey method is one of the most widely used approaches in educational research. As the name suggests, it aims to describe the characteristics of a population or phenomenon as they exist at a given point in time, without any manipulation of variables by the researcher. It involves the systematic collection of information from a defined sample using standardised instruments, followed by statistical analysis and interpretation of the data.

According to **Good and Hatt (1952)**, "The descriptive method is concerned with the conditions or relationships that exist, practices that prevail, beliefs and processes that are going on, effects that are being felt, or trends that are developing."

The descriptive survey method is particularly appropriate for the present study because:

1. The study aims to **describe** the existing pattern of vocational interests among secondary school students — not to manipulate any variable or test a cause-effect relationship.
2. A **standardised tool** (S.P. Kulshrestha VIR) is available for measuring the variable of interest reliably and validly.
3. The study involves **comparing** two groups (boys vs. girls; rural vs. urban) on a measured variable — which is a classic descriptive-comparative research design.
4. The method allows for the **generalisation** of findings to a larger population within the defined geographic and demographic boundaries of the study.

It is important to note that while this is a descriptive study, it does employ inferential statistics (t-test) to test whether the observed group differences are statistically significant — thus going beyond mere description to limited inference about the population.

## 3.3 Variables of the Study

### 3.3.1 Independent Variables

The independent variables in this study are:

1. **Gender**: Male (Boys) and Female (Girls)
2. **Type of School**: Rural Government School (Shri Sanskrit Inter College) and Urban Private CBSE School (KP International School)

### 3.3.2 Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is:

- **Vocational Interest** — as measured by the scores obtained on the nine subscales of the S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record (VIR): Literary, Scientific, Technical, Artistic, Commercial, Executive, Agricultural, Household, and Social.

### 3.3.3 Control Variables

The following variables were held constant (controlled) to the extent possible:

- **Level of Schooling**: All participants were from Classes IX or X (secondary level only).
- **Age Group**: The participants were broadly in the 14–16 age range.
- **Language of Administration**: The VIR was administered in Hindi to ensure uniform comprehension across both schools.
- **Time of Administration**: The tool was administered during regular school hours in a structured group setting.

## 3.4 Population of the Study

The **population** of the present study consists of all students enrolled in Classes IX and X in secondary schools in the **Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut district, Uttar Pradesh**, during the academic session 2024–2025.

Meerut district has a large and educationally diverse student population, with both government (UPMSP-affiliated) schools and private (CBSE-affiliated) schools serving students from different socioeconomic and geographical backgrounds. The Kila Parikshit Garh area represents a semi-urban to rural transitional zone — making it an ideal location for studying the vocational interest profiles of students from both government and private school backgrounds.

For practical purposes, the accessible population is limited to students from the two selected schools — **Shri Sanskrit Inter College** (a government secondary school) and **KP International School** (a private CBSE-affiliated school) — both located in or near the Kila Parikshit Garh area of Meerut district.

## 3.6 Sampling Technique

The **Random Sampling Method** was used to select participants from each school.

Random sampling is a probability-based sampling technique in which every member of the accessible population has an equal and independent chance of being selected for inclusion in the sample. This is considered the gold standard of sampling in quantitative research because it minimises selection bias and maximises the representativeness of the sample.

### Procedure of Random Sampling

**At Shri Sanskrit Inter College**: The Class IX register was obtained from the school office with the principal's permission. The names of all Class IX students were listed separately by gender. From the list of boys, 15 names were selected by the lottery method (chit system) — each name was written on a separate slip of paper, all slips were mixed in a box, and 15 were drawn at random. The same procedure was followed for selecting 15 girls from the Class IX girls' list.

**At KP International School**: The same procedure was applied to the Class X student register. Fifteen boys and fifteen girls were randomly selected from the respective gender-segregated class lists using the lottery method.

Students who were absent on the day of data collection were replaced by the next randomly drawn name from the reserve list to maintain the sample size.

### Justification of Sample Size

A sample size of 60 (30 per school; 15 boys and 15 girls per school) is considered adequate for a descriptive comparative study at the M.Ed. level, particularly given the practical constraints of time and resource availability. The sample size meets the minimum requirement for applying the t-test statistic meaningfully, and the equal-group design maximizes statistical power for the planned comparisons.

## 3.7 Tool Used: S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record (VIR)

### 3.7.1 Background and Introduction

The **S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record (VIR)** is a standardised psychological instrument designed specifically for measuring the vocational interests of Indian secondary and higher secondary school students. It was developed by **Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha** and published by the **National Psychological Corporation (NPC), Agra**. The VIR has been widely used in Indian educational research for over four decades and is considered one of the most reliable and culturally appropriate vocational interest instruments available for Indian students.

The VIR was selected for the present study because:
1. It is specifically normed for Indian secondary school students, making it culturally and contextually appropriate.
2. It assesses nine occupational interest areas that comprehensively cover the major vocational domains relevant to the Indian occupational landscape.
3. It has been used in numerous published Indian studies, facilitating comparison of the present findings with prior research.
4. It is available in both Hindi and English, making it accessible to students from diverse educational backgrounds.
5. It has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity in multiple independent studies.

### 3.7.2 Construction of the Tool

The VIR was constructed through a systematic process of item generation, expert review, pilot testing, and item analysis. The initial item pool was generated by consulting the occupational descriptions and task analyses of major Indian vocational categories. Items were written to reflect concrete, everyday activities associated with each vocational area — avoiding overly technical or abstract language so that secondary school students could respond meaningfully.

The items were subjected to review by a panel of vocational psychologists and educational experts to assess content validity. Following expert review, the instrument was pilot tested on a sample of secondary school students, and item-level statistics (item-total correlations, discrimination indices) were computed to retain only those items that contributed meaningfully to their respective subscales.

The final version of the VIR consists of **90 items** — **10 items per vocational interest area** across the following nine areas:

**Table 3.2: Vocational Interest Areas in S.P. Kulshrestha VIR**

| **Sr. No.** | **Vocational Interest Area** | **No. of Items** | **Representative Sample Activity** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Literary | 10 | Reading books, writing essays, studying languages |
| 2 | Scientific | 10 | Conducting experiments, studying science subjects |
| 3 | Technical | 10 | Repairing machines, working with tools |
| 4 | Artistic | 10 | Drawing, painting, music, dance, crafts |
| 5 | Commercial | 10 | Trading, accounting, business activities |
| 6 | Executive | 10 | Organising events, leading groups, decision-making |
| 7 | Agricultural | 10 | Farming, gardening, animal care |
| 8 | Household | 10 | Cooking, sewing, home management |
| 9 | Social | 10 | Helping people, teaching, social service |
| | **Total** | **90** | |

### 3.7.3 Format and Response Mode

Each item in the VIR presents a specific activity or task. The student is required to indicate their response to that activity on a **three-point scale**:

- **Like (L)** — The student enjoys or is attracted to this activity
- **Uncertain (U)** — The student is unsure about their preference for this activity
- **Dislike (D)** — The student does not enjoy or is not attracted to this activity

The three-point format was chosen over a dichotomous (Yes/No) format to capture the gradation of interest intensity and to allow for the realistic expression of uncertainty — a common psychological state among adolescents who are still exploring their interests.

### 3.7.4 Scoring Method

The VIR is scored as follows:

- **Like (L)** = **2 marks**
- **Uncertain (U)** = **1 mark**
- **Dislike (D)** = **0 marks**

The score for each vocational interest area is computed by **summing the weighted scores for all 10 items** in that subscale:

**Subscale Score = Σ (Item scores for that area)**

- **Minimum possible score per area** = 0 (if all items marked "Dislike")
- **Maximum possible score per area** = 20 (if all items marked "Like")

The area with the **highest score** represents the student's **dominant vocational interest**, and the area with the **lowest score** represents the **least preferred vocational domain**.

There is no total score across areas; rather, the VIR yields a **profile** of nine subscale scores for each student, which can be visually represented as a vocational interest profile.

### 3.7.5 Administration Procedure

The VIR is a **group-administered, paper-and-pencil instrument**. It can be administered to an entire class simultaneously. The standard administration procedure is as follows:

1. **Distribution**: Answer booklets (or response sheets) are distributed to all students.
2. **Instructions**: The examiner reads out the standardised instructions from the manual, ensuring that all students understand the format and response procedure. Instructions are given in Hindi for this study.
3. **Practice Items**: Two practice items are worked through collectively before the formal administration begins, to ensure comprehension.
4. **Administration**: Students complete the 90-item inventory at their own pace. The typical completion time is **25–35 minutes**.
5. **Collection**: Completed response sheets are collected and checked for completeness before formal scoring.

### 3.7.6 Reliability of the Tool

**Reliability** refers to the consistency of a measurement instrument — the degree to which it produces stable, reproducible results under similar conditions.

The reliability of the S.P. Kulshrestha VIR has been established through multiple methods:

**Split-Half Reliability**: Kulshrestha (1975) reported split-half reliability coefficients ranging from **0.82 to 0.91** across the nine subscales, after applying the Spearman-Brown correction formula. These values indicate high internal consistency.

**Test-Retest Reliability**: A test-retest study with a four-week interval yielded reliability coefficients ranging from **0.78 to 0.88** across subscales, indicating good temporal stability of the measures.

**Inter-rater Reliability**: As the VIR is an objectively scored instrument (no judgment required in scoring), inter-rater reliability is not applicable in the traditional sense. However, independent scoring of the same response sheets by two scorers has yielded perfect agreement, confirming that scoring is entirely objective.

These reliability values are well within the acceptable range for psychological instruments used in educational research (generally, coefficients above 0.70 are considered acceptable; above 0.80 is considered good).

### 3.7.7 Validity of the Tool

**Validity** refers to the degree to which an instrument actually measures what it claims to measure.

The validity of the Kulshrestha VIR has been established on multiple grounds:

**Content Validity**: The items were generated from a systematic analysis of activities associated with nine occupational domains, and reviewed by a panel of vocational psychologists. The content coverage of each subscale is comprehensive and representative of the corresponding vocational area.

**Construct Validity**: Factor analytic studies have confirmed that the VIR items load onto their intended subscales with satisfactory factor loadings, and the nine subscales are meaningfully differentiated from each other — indicating good construct validity.

**Criterion-Related Validity (Concurrent)**: Students' VIR scores have been found to correlate significantly with their independently stated occupational preferences and their teachers' ratings of their vocational orientations, providing evidence of concurrent validity.

**Predictive Validity**: Follow-up studies have found that students who scored highest on particular VIR subscales were more likely to choose corresponding educational streams and career pathways in subsequent years, providing evidence of predictive validity.

## 3.8 Procedure of Data Collection

### 3.8.1 Preparatory Steps

Before the formal data collection could begin, several preparatory steps were undertaken. The researcher spent considerable time during September–October 2024 reviewing the literature, finalising the research design, and preparing the necessary documentation.

The **S.P. Kulshrestha Vocational Interest Record** was procured from the **National Psychological Corporation (NPC), Agra**, along with the test manual, sufficient copies of the response sheets, and the scoring keys. The researcher familiarised himself thoroughly with the administration and scoring procedures by carefully studying the test manual and conducting a trial administration with a few students at his own college.

A **formal application for permission** was prepared, addressed to the principals of both selected schools. The application clearly described the nature of the study, its academic purpose, the expected time commitment from students, and the assurance of confidentiality. The application was countersigned by the researcher's supervisor, **Dr. Seema Sharma**, to lend institutional authority to the request.

### 3.8.2 Visit to Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Meerut

The researcher visited **Shri Sanskrit Inter College** in the second week of November 2024. The school is situated in a predominantly agricultural area in the Kila Parikshit Garh tehsil of Meerut district. It is a government-aided secondary school with a student population of approximately 400, mostly from farming and labour-class families of the surrounding villages.

The researcher first met with the **Principal, Shri Sanskrit Inter College**, presented his formal application, explained the objectives of the study, and requested permission to administer the VIR to Class IX students. The principal received the researcher warmly and, after reviewing the documentation, gave his verbal and written permission. He also introduced the researcher to the **Class IX class teacher**, who facilitated all subsequent interactions.

On the day of administration, the researcher arrived at the school at 9:00 AM and was assigned the Class IX classroom after the first period. The classroom had approximately 45 students enrolled, from whom 15 boys and 15 girls had been pre-selected through the lottery method. The non-selected students attended a different session.

Before distributing the response sheets, the researcher introduced himself, explained the purpose of the study in simple Hindi, and assured the students that their responses would be kept completely confidential — that the data would be used only for research purposes and would not affect their school grades or be shared with their teachers or parents. This assurance was important in creating a psychologically safe environment for honest responses.

The VIR was then administered following the standard procedure. The researcher read out the standardised instructions from the manual in Hindi, worked through the two practice items with the students, and then allowed them to proceed at their own pace. The atmosphere was relaxed and cooperative. Most students completed the inventory within 30–35 minutes.

The researcher noticed with interest that several students — particularly the girls — paused and thought carefully before responding to items in the Technical and Scientific areas, while the boys showed similar hesitation over Household items. These informal observations, while not part of the formal data, provided valuable qualitative context for interpreting the quantitative findings.

After collecting the completed response sheets, the researcher checked each one for completeness. Two response sheets (one boy, one girl) were found to have missed a few items and were gently encouraged to complete their responses before submission. All 30 response sheets were finally collected in good condition.

### 3.8.3 Visit to KP International School, Kila Parikshit Garh, Meerut

The researcher visited **KP International School** in the third week of November 2024. This school is a well-established private CBSE-affiliated institution with modern infrastructure — computer labs, science labs, a library, and a dedicated sports ground. The student population is largely from middle-class and lower-middle-class urban families, with parents employed in diverse occupations including small business, government service, teaching, and skilled trades.

The permission procedure was similar to that at the government school. The researcher met the **Principal of KP International School**, presented his application (countersigned by Dr. Seema Sharma), explained the research objectives, and received ready permission. The principal was familiar with the concept of educational research and expressed genuine interest in the findings, asking that a summary report of the results be shared with the school after the dissertation was submitted.

The Class X class teacher was extremely supportive and arranged for a free period for the data collection session. The pre-selected 15 boys and 15 girls assembled in the school's computer laboratory, which provided adequate seating with individual desks for each student.

The administration procedure was identical to that at Shri Sanskrit Inter College. The researcher was struck by the notably different atmosphere in this school compared to the government school — students here were more verbally expressive, asked a few clarifying questions during instructions, and showed greater familiarity with the concept of psychological tests (several mentioned having taken aptitude tests before). The administration was completed smoothly within approximately 30 minutes. All 30 response sheets were collected, complete and properly filled.

### 3.8.4 Scoring

After data collection, all 60 response sheets were scored by the researcher following the standardised scoring procedure described in the Kulshrestha VIR manual. Each response sheet yielded nine subscale scores (one for each vocational interest area). The scores were entered into a master data sheet organized by school, gender, and student code number. A sample of 10 randomly selected response sheets was re-scored by a fellow M.Ed. student (serving as an independent scorer) to check for scoring accuracy. Perfect agreement was obtained on all re-scored sheets, confirming the objectivity of the scoring process.

## 3.9 Statistical Techniques

The data collected through the VIR were subjected to the following statistical analyses:

### 3.9.1 Mean (Arithmetic Average)

The **Mean** is the most basic and widely used measure of central tendency. It represents the average score of a group on a given variable and provides a single number that summarises the group's typical performance.

**Formula:**

$$\bar{X} = \frac{\Sigma X}{N}$$

Where:
- $\bar{X}$ = Mean
- $\Sigma X$ = Sum of all scores
- $N$ = Total number of scores in the group

The Mean was computed for boys and girls separately, and for rural and urban students separately, on each of the nine VIR subscales.

**Educational Relevance**: Comparing the mean scores of boys and girls (or rural and urban students) on each subscale provides a direct indication of which groups show higher average interest in each vocational area.

### 3.9.2 Standard Deviation (SD)

The **Standard Deviation** is the most commonly used measure of variability or dispersion. It indicates how much individual scores in a group deviate, on average, from the group mean. A small SD indicates that scores are clustered closely around the mean (homogeneous group); a large SD indicates greater spread (heterogeneous group).

**Formula:**

$$SD = \sqrt{\frac{\Sigma(X - \bar{X})^2}{N}}$$

Where:
- $SD$ = Standard Deviation
- $X$ = Individual score
- $\bar{X}$ = Mean of the group
- $N$ = Number of scores

Alternatively, the computational formula used for convenience:

$$SD = \sqrt{\frac{\Sigma X^2}{N} - \left(\frac{\Sigma X}{N}\right)^2}$$

The SD was computed for each group (boys, girls, rural, urban) on each of the nine subscales.

**Educational Relevance**: The SD provides information about the consistency of vocational interests within groups. A large SD suggests that students within the group have widely divergent interest levels in a given area, while a small SD suggests relatively uniform interest levels.

### 3.9.3 t-test (Independent Samples)

The **independent samples t-test** is a parametric inferential statistical test used to determine whether the difference between the means of two independent groups on a continuous variable is statistically significant — i.e., whether the observed difference is likely to reflect a genuine population-level difference rather than random sampling error.

**Formula:**

$$t = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{SD_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{SD_2^2}{N_2}}}$$

Where:
- $\bar{X}_1$ = Mean of Group 1 (e.g., Boys)
- $\bar{X}_2$ = Mean of Group 2 (e.g., Girls)
- $SD_1$ = Standard Deviation of Group 1
- $SD_2$ = Standard Deviation of Group 2
- $N_1$ = Sample size of Group 1
- $N_2$ = Sample size of Group 2

**Degrees of Freedom** = $N_1 + N_2 - 2$

For the present study, with $N_1 = N_2 = 30$:
Degrees of Freedom = 30 + 30 − 2 = **58**

The calculated t-value is compared against the **critical value of t** at 58 degrees of freedom from the t-distribution table:
- At **0.05 level of significance** (two-tailed): critical t = **2.00**
- At **0.01 level of significance** (two-tailed): critical t = **2.66**

If the calculated t-value exceeds the critical value, the null hypothesis is **rejected**, and the difference is declared statistically significant.

**Assumptions of the t-test** observed in this study:
1. The dependent variable (VIR subscale scores) is measured at interval level.
2. The two comparison groups are independent (no overlap in membership).
3. The scores are approximately normally distributed within each group.
4. The variances of the two groups are roughly equal (homogeneity of variance).

**Educational Relevance**: The t-test provides the statistical evidence needed to either reject or retain the null hypotheses stated in Chapter 1, and thus forms the basis for the study's principal conclusions.

### 3.9.4 Percentage Analysis

**Percentage analysis** is a simple but powerful descriptive technique that expresses a part as a proportion of the whole, multiplied by 100. It was used in this study to determine the percentage of students in each group (boys, girls, rural, urban) whose scores placed them in the "High Interest" category on each VIR subscale.

**Formula:**

$$Percentage = \frac{f}{N} \times 100$$

Where:
- $f$ = Frequency (number of students in the category)
- $N$ = Total number of students in the group

For this study, a score of **15 or above** (out of 20) on any subscale was classified as **"High Interest"**; a score of **8–14** was classified as **"Moderate Interest"**; and a score below **8** was classified as **"Low Interest."**

## 3.10 Ethical Considerations

The conduct of educational research involving human participants, especially minor students, requires careful attention to ethical principles. The following ethical standards were observed throughout this study:

1. **Informed Consent**: Written permission was obtained from the principals of both schools before any data collection was conducted. Students were informed about the purpose and nature of the study in age-appropriate language before administration.

2. **Voluntariness**: Participation in the study was entirely voluntary. Students were clearly informed that they could choose not to participate or could withdraw at any time without any negative consequences to their schooling.

3. **Confidentiality**: Students were assured — and this assurance was honoured — that their individual responses would remain confidential and would not be shared with their teachers, parents, or school authorities. Data were stored using student code numbers rather than names in all analysis files.

4. **Anonymity in Reporting**: All findings are reported at the group level (means, SDs, t-values). No individual student's data is identified or disclosed in the dissertation.

5. **Non-deception**: The purpose of the study was honestly explained to students. No deceptive practices were employed.

6. **Respect for School Routines**: Data collection was scheduled to minimise disruption to the schools' normal academic schedules. Sessions were conducted during free periods or at times designated by the school administration.

7. **Benefit Sharing**: The researcher committed to sharing a summary of the research findings with both schools as a gesture of reciprocity for their cooperation.

8. **Sensitivity to Student Well-being**: The researcher was alert to any signs of discomfort or anxiety among students during administration and was prepared to provide reassurance as needed.
