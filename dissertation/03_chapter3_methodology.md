# RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## 3.1 INTRODUCTION

Methodology is the **plan and procedure** by which a research worker moves from a problem to its solution. Just as a doctor cannot treat a patient without first preparing a clear plan of medicine, dose, frequency and follow-up, in the same way a research worker cannot solve a problem without first preparing a clear plan of method, sample, tool, procedure and statistics. The chapter on research methodology, therefore, occupies a central position in any dissertation. It is the chapter which **converts a research problem into a research study**.

In the present chapter the investigator describes, step by step, how the problem stated in Chapter I was actually attacked in the field. The chapter answers the following six questions in order:

1. *Which research method was used?*
2. *What were the variables?*
3. *Who was the population, and how was the sample drawn?*
4. *Which tool was used, and why?*
5. *How was the data actually collected and scored?*
6. *Which statistical techniques were used, and on what formulas were they based?*

Apart from these six core questions, the chapter also covers ethical considerations and the methodological limitations of the study.

## 3.2 RESEARCH METHOD ADOPTED

The present study has used the **Descriptive Survey Method**.

### 3.2.1 Meaning of the Descriptive Survey Method

The descriptive survey method is a method of educational research in which the researcher describes "**what is**" — that is, the present state of affairs of a group, an institution or a situation, with regard to one or more variables. Best and Kahn (2006) define descriptive research as the method that "describes and interprets what is. It is concerned with conditions or relationships that exist, opinions that are held, processes that are going on, effects that are evident, or trends that are developing." The descriptive survey method does **not** try to manipulate any variable; it simply gathers facts about an existing situation through a careful and systematic procedure and then interprets these facts.

### 3.2.2 Why this Method was Chosen

The present study aims to **describe** the vocational interest pattern of secondary school boys and girls of Meerut District as it exists today, and to **compare** this pattern between the two groups. There is no question of any experimental treatment; nothing is being given to the students; nothing is being changed in their environment. The researcher is only collecting data on a variable (vocational interest) that already exists naturally in the students. This is exactly the kind of situation for which the descriptive survey method is best suited.

### 3.2.3 Characteristics of the Descriptive Survey Method

(i) It deals with **present situations**, not past or future.

(ii) It involves the **gathering of fresh data** from the field, usually through standardised tools (tests, questionnaires, interview schedules).

(iii) It uses **representative samples** drawn through proper sampling techniques.

(iv) It seeks to **describe, analyse, classify and interpret** facts.

(v) It can be used for **comparison** between two or more groups, which is exactly what the present study aims at.

(vi) The data collected can be analysed by simple statistics like Mean, Median, Mode, Standard Deviation, t-test, Percentage, Chi-square, etc., or by more advanced statistics like ANOVA, factor analysis and regression.

### 3.2.4 Sub-types of the Descriptive Survey Method

Descriptive survey research can be of several sub-types — School Survey, Public Opinion Survey, Job Analysis, Documentary Analysis, Cross-sectional Survey, Longitudinal Survey, Trend Studies, Co-relational Studies, Causal-Comparative (Ex-post-facto) Studies, etc. The present study is of the **Cross-sectional + Causal-Comparative** sub-type. It is *cross-sectional* because the data is collected at a single point of time (rather than at multiple time points across years), and it is *causal-comparative* because two pre-existing groups (boys and girls) are compared on a dependent variable (vocational interest) without any active manipulation of the independent variable.

## 3.3 VARIABLES OF THE STUDY

A **variable** is a property or characteristic which takes more than one value. Three kinds of variables are commonly used in descriptive comparative research:

(a) **Independent Variable.** The variable on the basis of which two or more groups are being compared. In the present study, the independent variable is **gender**, with two values — boys and girls. A second, secondary, independent variable is **class** — class IX and class X.

(b) **Dependent Variable.** The variable on which the groups are being compared. In the present study, the dependent variable is **vocational interest**, measured as scores on the nine areas of Kulshrestha's V.I.R. (Literary, Scientific, Executive, Commercial, Artistic, Agriculture, Technical, Household and Social).

(c) **Intervening / Controlled Variables.** Variables which can possibly influence the dependent variable but which the researcher tries to keep constant or balanced. In the present study, the following variables have been controlled by the design:
- **Age** — by selecting students of class IX and X (14–16 years).
- **Stage of schooling** — by selecting only secondary stage.
- **Region** — by selecting only Meerut district.
- **Language** — by administering the test in its standard Hindi version.
- **Time of testing** — by administering the test in the morning between 10:00 a.m. and 12:00 noon for all the four groups.

The **uncontrolled variables**, which may have some effect on the data but which the researcher could not control fully, include socio-economic status of the family, parental education, parental occupation, birth-order, family size, intelligence and personality of the student. Their possible effect has been kept in mind during interpretation in Chapter IV.

## 3.5 SAMPLE OF THE STUDY

A **sample** is a small but representative part of the population, selected with the help of a definite sampling technique, on which the actual study is conducted. A well-chosen sample carries the same essential characteristics as the population, and what is true of the sample can, with proper care, be generalised to the population.

### 3.5.1 Size of the Sample

For the present study, a sample of **80 students** has been drawn. The size of the sample has been decided on the following considerations:

(a) The total work of administration, scoring and analysis has to be carried out by a single researcher within the prescribed period of an M.Ed. dissertation (about six months of fieldwork). A sample much larger than 80 would be difficult to manage at this scale.

(b) For application of the t-test on two independent samples, a sample of 30 in each group is generally considered sufficient (Garrett & Woodworth, 2009). A sample of 40 boys versus 40 girls (df = 78) is therefore quite adequate to obtain reliable t-values.

(c) Past M.Ed. dissertations and similar small-scale studies on Kulshrestha's V.I.R. have used samples between 60 and 200. A sample of 80 fits well within this range.

## 3.6 SAMPLING TECHNIQUE

The sampling technique used in the present study is **Random Sampling** (specifically **simple random sampling without replacement using the lottery method**), applied **after a purposive selection of the two schools and the two classes**.

The procedure followed was as under:

(a) **Selection of the two schools** was purposive; the two schools were chosen on the criteria mentioned in Section 3.5.2.

(b) **Within each school, within each class, separately for boys and girls, simple random sampling was applied** to select the required number of students.

(c) The class teacher provided a list of all the boys / all the girls in the class, with serial numbers. For example, in Shri Sanskrit Inter College, the boys' section of class IX had 38 boys; their roll numbers were written on small chits of paper, mixed thoroughly inside a box, and **10 chits were drawn one by one** without replacement. The 10 boys whose chits came out were the 10 selected boys of class IX of that school. The same procedure was repeated for the 30 girls of class IX of that school, the 41 boys of class X of the same school, the 33 girls of class X of the same school, and the corresponding sections of the second school.

(d) Care was taken that **no student was left out by reason of absence on the day of the lottery**; if a chit drawn turned out to be that of a chronically absent student, the chit was replaced and a fresh one was drawn.

This procedure of "purposive selection of schools + simple random sampling within schools" is widely accepted in Indian M.Ed. and Ph.D. work and is also recommended by Best and Kahn (2006), Koul (2009) and Garrett and Woodworth (2009). It combines the **practical convenience** of purposive sampling with the **statistical fairness** of random sampling.

The V.I.R. was chosen for the present study for the following reasons:

(a) It is one of the **most widely used Indian-made** vocational interest tools and has been used in more than two hundred Indian dissertations between 1976 and 2025.

(f) It is **inexpensive** and easy to obtain from the publisher (National Psychological Corporation, Agra).

(g) It has been used in many studies reviewed in **Chapter II**, which makes it possible to compare the results of the present study with those studies.

## 3.10 ADMINISTRATION AND SCORING PROCEDURE

### 3.10.1 Administration

The V.I.R. was administered in the classroom of each sample group on a fixed date, between 10:00 a.m. and 12:00 noon, in the presence of the class teacher and the researcher. The full procedure followed the steps given in the manual:

**Step 1.** Distribution of the booklet and answer-sheet to each of the selected students. (The booklets were collected back at the end so that the same set could be used for the next group.)

**Step 2.** Filling of the bio-data on the answer-sheet — name, age, class, section, school, father's occupation, mother's occupation and date.

**Step 3.** Reading aloud of the standardised instructions, in Hindi, by the researcher.

**Step 4.** Working out of the **two practice items** with the whole class, on the blackboard.

**Step 5.** Independent attempt of all the 90 items by the students. They were free to ask the researcher about the meaning of any unfamiliar word, but no help was given on the actual choice. The students were assured that there were **no right or wrong answers** and that their responses would be kept **strictly confidential** and used only for research.

**Step 6.** Collection of the answer-sheets from the students. The researcher checked, on the spot, that **all** the 45 pairs had been responded to. In case of any pair left blank, the student was asked to fill it before the answer-sheet was finally collected.

The administration took, on an average, 35–40 minutes (including instructions and bio-data filling). The administration was completed in two visits per school — one for class IX and one for class X — over a period of nine working days during October – November 2025.

### 3.10.2 Scoring

Scoring of the V.I.R. is done with the help of a **scoring stencil** provided by the publisher. The stencil is placed over the answer-sheet and the number of ticks falling in the holes corresponding to each area is counted. Each tick in an area earns one mark; the maximum possible score in any area is 30 (since each area has 30 chances of being ticked across the 45 pairs). The nine area-scores together form the **vocational interest profile** of the student.

For example, a student who has ticked Literary 17 times, Scientific 22 times, Executive 19 times, Commercial 16 times, Artistic 14 times, Agricultural 18 times, Technical 23 times, Household 11 times and Social 18 times shows a **Technical-Scientific-Executive** type of profile, with relatively low interest in the Household area. A second student with 20 in Literary, 18 in Scientific, 16 in Executive, 15 in Commercial, 21 in Artistic, 12 in Agricultural, 13 in Technical, 22 in Household and 21 in Social shows a **Household-Artistic-Social-Literary** type of profile, very different from the first.

The researcher personally scored all the 80 answer-sheets, and a 10 % random sub-sample (8 answer-sheets) was re-scored by an external M.Ed. classmate to check the inter-scorer agreement. The agreement was 100 % on every area for all 8 answer-sheets, showing that the scoring was free of clerical error.

## 3.12 STATISTICAL TECHNIQUES USED

To analyse the raw scores obtained from the 80 students, the following four statistical techniques have been used. They are simple, well-known and widely accepted at the M.Ed. level.

### 3.12.1 Arithmetic Mean (M)

The arithmetic mean is the most commonly used measure of **central tendency**. It is the **average** score of a group on a particular variable. For the present study, the area-wise mean has been calculated separately for boys and girls.

**Formula** (for ungrouped data, which is the case here):

$$M = \frac{\sum X}{N}$$

Where,
- **M** = Arithmetic mean
- **ΣX** = Sum of all scores in the group
- **N** = Number of cases

For example, if 40 boys obtain a total of 658 marks on the Literary area, the mean of boys on Literary = 658 / 40 = 16.45.

### 3.12.2 Standard Deviation (S.D., σ)

Standard deviation is the most commonly used measure of **variability** or **spread**. It tells us how far, on an average, the scores in a group lie away from the mean of the group. A small S.D. means that most students have scored close to the mean (group is homogeneous); a large S.D. means that students differ widely from each other (group is heterogeneous).

**Formula** (for ungrouped data):

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (X - M)^2}{N}}$$

Where,
- **σ** = Standard deviation
- **(X – M)** = Deviation of each score from the mean of the group
- **N** = Number of cases

For example, on the Literary area for boys, if Σ(X – M)² works out to 705.6 with N = 40, then σ = √(705.6 / 40) = √17.64 = **4.20**.

### 3.12.3 t-Test (t-ratio for two independent samples)

The t-test is used to find out whether the **difference between the means of two independent groups** is large enough to be called "significant" — that is, large enough to be regarded as a real difference and not the result of mere chance. In the present study, the t-test has been applied **nine times**, once for each of the nine vocational interest areas, to compare the mean of boys (N = 40) with the mean of girls (N = 40).

**Formula** (for two independent samples of different / equal sizes, assuming homogeneous variances):

$$t = \frac{M_1 - M_2}{\text{S.E.}_D}, \quad \text{where} \quad \text{S.E.}_D = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^{2}}{N_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^{2}}{N_2}}$$

Where,
- **M₁, M₂** = Means of the two groups
- **σ₁, σ₂** = Standard deviations of the two groups
- **N₁, N₂** = Sample sizes of the two groups
- **S.E._D** = Standard error of the difference between the two means
- **t** = t-ratio

For example, on the Literary area, M_Boys = 16.45, M_Girls = 19.85, σ_Boys = 4.20, σ_Girls = 3.95, N_Boys = N_Girls = 40.

S.E._D = √[(4.20)² / 40 + (3.95)² / 40] = √[17.64/40 + 15.6025/40] = √[0.441 + 0.3901] = √0.8311 = **0.912**

t = (16.45 – 19.85) / 0.912 = – 3.40 / 0.912 = **– 3.73**

The **degree of freedom (df)** for two independent samples is **N₁ + N₂ – 2** = 40 + 40 – 2 = 78. From Fisher and Yates' table for df = 78, the critical values are **t.05 = 1.991** and **t.01 = 2.640** (two-tailed). Hence the obtained t = –3.73 is greater than 2.640 in absolute value, and the difference is significant at the 0.01 level.

### 3.12.4 Percentage Analysis

Percentage analysis is the simplest and most popular technique for comparing distributions of categorical data.

**Formula:**

$$P = \frac{f}{N} \times 100$$

Where,
- **P** = Percentage
- **f** = Frequency (number of students in a particular category)
- **N** = Total number of cases in the group

In the present study, percentage analysis has been used to find the proportion of boys and girls who fall into the **High, Average and Low** categories on each of the nine areas, and to find the proportion of boys and girls whose first preference is each of the nine areas.

## 3.13 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

The investigator made every effort to follow the standard ethical norms of educational research. The main steps taken in this regard are:

1. **Informed permission** of the principal of each school was obtained in writing before approaching the students (Appendix A — Permission Letter).

2. **Informed assent** of every student was taken orally in the classroom; a student who did not wish to participate was free to remain silent (in fact, no such case occurred).

3. **Informed consent of the parent / guardian** was treated as having been given through the principal, who is the *in loco parentis* during school hours.

4. **Confidentiality** of the responses was assured. The names of the students do not appear anywhere in the analysis tables; each student has been given an anonymous serial number (B-01 to B-40 for boys and G-01 to G-40 for girls).

5. **No physical, psychological or academic harm** was caused. The V.I.R. is a non-threatening test with no right or wrong answers and no comparison with the academic record.

6. **No payment, reward or punishment** was attached to the responses.

7. **Honest reporting** of the data, without deletion of "inconvenient" cases or addition of imaginary cases, has been ensured. The full raw-data sheets are placed in Appendix D, so that any future researcher can re-analyse the data if needed.

8. **Acknowledgement of the tool's author** — Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha — has been made both in the bibliography and inside the running text wherever the tool is mentioned. The researcher purchased original copies of the tool from the publisher; no photocopying was done.

## 3.14 LIMITATIONS OF THE METHODOLOGY

Although every care has been taken at every stage, the methodology of the present study has the following honest limitations, which the reader is requested to keep in mind while reading the next two chapters:

1. The sample of **80 students** is small and **regionally restricted** to two schools of Meerut District. The findings, therefore, can be generalised to similar schools of similar localities of western Uttar Pradesh, but **not** to all Indian secondary schools.

2. The variable studied is restricted to **vocational interest** only. **Vocational aptitude, intelligence, personality and socio-economic status** have not been measured, so causal explanations of the gender differences cannot be given with full certainty.

3. The study is **cross-sectional**, conducted at a single point of time. **Longitudinal change** in interests cannot be observed.

4. The tool, although well-standardised, was last fully revised some years ago. Some of the activity items (for example, "stenographer", "telegraphist") may sound mildly old-fashioned to a 2026 adolescent, although in actual administration none of the students reported any practical difficulty.

5. The two schools were chosen **purposively**, not randomly. To this extent, the sample may carry some specific colour of these two schools.

These limitations do not affect the validity of the comparison between boys and girls **within the present sample**, but they do call for caution at the time of generalisation. The next chapter — **Chapter IV** — turns to the data and presents its analysis and interpretation in full detail.
