## 4.1 Introduction

The present chapter constitutes the analytical heart of this dissertation. All the preceding chapters — the theoretical framework, the literature review, the research design — were preparatory steps leading to this moment: the systematic examination of the data collected from 60 secondary school students across two schools in the Meerut district. The purpose of this chapter is to present, analyse, and interpret the data in a manner that is statistically rigorous, educationally meaningful, and practically insightful.

The chapter is organized into the following sections:

- **Section 4.2**: Descriptive Statistics (Mean and Standard Deviation) — Gender-wise and School-wise
- **Section 4.3**: Hypothesis Testing using the Independent Samples t-test
- **Section 4.4**: Percentage Analysis of High, Moderate, and Low Interest Categories
- **Section 4.5**: Gender-wise Comparative Analysis with visual representations
- **Section 4.6**: Rural vs. Urban Comparative Analysis with visual representations
- **Section 4.7**: Summary of Analysis

Throughout this chapter, every statistical table is followed by a detailed interpretation of the findings. The discussion draws on the theoretical frameworks and prior research reviewed in Chapters 1 and 2 to contextualise the statistical results within the broader landscape of educational psychology and vocational guidance.

The nine vocational interest areas assessed are abbreviated for convenience as follows: **LIT** (Literary), **SCI** (Scientific), **TEC** (Technical), **ART** (Artistic), **COM** (Commercial), **EXE** (Executive), **AGR** (Agricultural), **HOU** (Household), **SOC** (Social).

Scores on each subscale range from **0 to 20** (10 items × maximum 2 marks each).

### Interpretation of Table 4.1

A careful examination of Table 4.1 reveals a clear and consistent pattern of gender differentiation in vocational interests. The data tells a compelling story about how boys and girls at the secondary level — even within the same age group and geographical setting — express very different patterns of occupational preference.

**Boys' Dominant Interests**: The three highest mean scores for boys are in **Technical (16.47)**, **Scientific (15.13)**, and **Agricultural (13.67)**. This profile is highly consistent with the findings of prior Indian research (Sharma, 2018; Mishra, 2019) and with the theoretical expectations generated by Holland's RIASEC model and Social Cognitive Career Theory (Lent et al., 1994). Boys appear strongly drawn to hands-on, mechanical, and investigative activities — precisely the kinds of activities that Indian culture tends to associate with masculine competence and capability.

**Girls' Dominant Interests**: The three highest mean scores for girls are in **Social (16.20)**, **Artistic (15.80)**, and **Literary (15.10)**. This is equally consistent with the literature — girls show a pronounced orientation towards people-oriented, expressive, and communicative vocational domains. The particularly high Social interest score (16.20) is noteworthy: it suggests that working with, helping, and caring for people is the strongest vocational pull for girls in this sample, whether they come from a rural government school or an urban private school.

**Most Striking Difference — Household Interest**: The most dramatic gender gap in the data is in Household interest — Boys mean = **5.57** vs. Girls mean = **14.40**, a difference of **8.83 points** on a 20-point scale. This is a very large difference and reflects the deep-rooted cultural associations between domestic work and femininity in the Indian social context. Even urban private school girls (mean = 12.60) score substantially higher on Household interest than boys in either school.

**Second Most Striking Difference — Technical Interest**: The second largest gap is in Technical interest — Boys mean = **16.47** vs. Girls mean = **8.60**, a difference of **7.87 points**. Boys are drawn to technical activities with very high intensity, while girls show relatively low interest in this domain. This finding has important implications for STEM education and gender equity in technical fields.

**Commercial and Executive**: The gender differences in Commercial (12.20 boys vs. 11.60 girls) and Executive (12.87 boys vs. 12.60 girls) interests are relatively small, suggesting that these are areas of broadly similar interest — or lack of strong interest — for both genders at the secondary level. This is interesting and perhaps reflects the growing awareness of business and management careers among both boys and girls in contemporary India.

## 4.4 Hypothesis Testing: t-test Results

The independent samples t-test was applied to test each of the eleven null hypotheses stated in Chapter 1. The critical value of t at df = 58 is **2.00** at the 0.05 level and **2.66** at the 0.01 level (two-tailed).

### 4.4.1 Table 4.3: t-test Results — Boys vs. Girls on All Nine Vocational Areas

| **Vocational Area** | **Boys Mean** | **Girls Mean** | **Mean Diff.** | **SE Diff.** | **t-value** | **df** | **Significance** | **Decision on H₀** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Literary | 12.17 | 15.10 | -2.93 | 0.31 | **9.45** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Scientific | 15.13 | 11.67 | +3.47 | 0.38 | **9.13** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Technical | 16.47 | 8.60 | +7.87 | 0.32 | **24.59** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Artistic | 10.27 | 15.80 | -5.53 | 0.42 | **13.17** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Commercial | 12.20 | 11.60 | +0.60 | 0.37 | **1.62** | 58 | p > 0.05 | **Retained** |
| Executive | 12.87 | 12.60 | +0.27 | 0.44 | **0.61** | 58 | p > 0.05 | **Retained** |
| Agricultural | 13.67 | 11.10 | +2.57 | 0.73 | **3.52** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Household | 5.57 | 14.40 | -8.83 | 0.43 | **20.53** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |
| Social | 12.87 | 16.20 | -3.33 | 0.30 | **11.10** | 58 | p < 0.01 | **Rejected** |

*Critical t at df=58: 2.00 (p<0.05), 2.66 (p<0.01)*

### Interpretation of Table 4.3

The t-test results provide statistically rigorous confirmation of the patterns observed in the descriptive analysis. Of the nine gender-based hypotheses, **seven null hypotheses are rejected** (Literary, Scientific, Technical, Artistic, Agricultural, Household, Social), meaning statistically significant gender differences were found in these areas. **Two null hypotheses are retained** (Commercial and Executive), indicating no statistically significant gender differences in these areas.

**H₀₁ — Literary Interest (t = 9.45, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: Girls score significantly higher on Literary interest. This is consistent with the well-established finding that girls in Indian schools show a stronger orientation towards language, reading, and communication-related activities (Sharma, 2018; Kumari, 2015). Literary interest also connects naturally with the Social and Artistic interests that characterise girls' vocational profiles.

**H₀₂ — Scientific Interest (t = 9.13, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: Boys score significantly higher on Scientific interest. The preference for systematic enquiry, experimentation, and analytical reasoning is more pronounced among boys. This finding, while consistent with the literature, also raises important questions about the socialization processes that may discourage girls from scientific domains — particularly in the rural school context where science infrastructure is limited and female role models in science are rare.

**H₀₃ — Technical Interest (t = 24.59, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: The t-value of 24.59 is by far the largest in this table, indicating an exceptionally strong gender difference in Technical interest. Boys' dominance in this area is overwhelming. However, the researcher notes that this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of any inherent male superiority in technical aptitude — rather, it reflects years of differential socialization, in which boys are encouraged to engage with tools and machines while girls are steered away from such activities from a very early age (Gottfredson, 1981; Lent et al., 1994).

**H₀₄ — Artistic Interest (t = 13.17, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: Girls score significantly higher on Artistic interest. Creative expression — whether through art, music, dance, or craft — has traditionally been more culturally encouraged in Indian girls. The high Artistic interest score for girls in this sample (mean = 15.80) suggests a genuine and strong orientation towards creative and aesthetic domains.

**H₀₅ — Commercial Interest (t = 1.62, p > 0.05 — Retained)**: No significant gender difference was found in Commercial interest. This is an interesting finding — it suggests that both boys and girls at the secondary level show broadly similar (moderate) interest in business and commercial activities. This may reflect the growing visibility of commercial and entrepreneurial career possibilities for both genders in contemporary India.

**H₀₆ — Executive Interest (t = 0.61, p > 0.05 — Retained)**: No significant gender difference was found in Executive interest either. Both boys and girls show similar moderate interest in leadership and management roles — a finding that is somewhat more surprising given traditional gender norms around leadership. The retention of this null hypothesis suggests that at the secondary school level, aspirations for leadership and administrative roles are not yet strongly gender-differentiated.

**H₀₇ — Agricultural Interest (t = 3.52, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: Boys score significantly higher on Agricultural interest. This is partly expected, given that agricultural work in Indian rural communities is still predominantly performed by men. However, the difference, while significant, is less extreme than the Technical and Household differences — suggesting some exposure of girls to agriculture in the rural school context.

**H₀₈ — Household Interest (t = 20.53, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: The second largest t-value in the table. Girls score dramatically higher on Household interest. This finding reflects the powerful cultural conditioning that associates domestic activities with femininity in Indian society. Even urban private school girls (mean = 12.60) score far higher on Household interest than boys in either school type (rural boys = 5.33; urban boys = 5.80).

**H₀₉ — Social Interest (t = 11.10, p < 0.01 — Rejected)**: Girls score significantly higher on Social interest. The orientation towards helping, caring for, and working with people is markedly stronger in girls. This finding is consistent across all fifteen studies reviewed in Chapter 2, making it perhaps the single most robustly replicated finding in the vocational interest literature pertaining to gender.

### Interpretation of Table 4.4

The rural-urban t-test results show that **seven of the nine vocational areas** yield statistically significant differences between rural and urban students. Only **Technical** and **Household** show no significant rural-urban difference.

**Scientific, Artistic, Commercial, Executive, and Social** areas all show significantly higher scores for urban students, confirming that the richer educational and occupational environment of the urban private school fosters broader and more diverse vocational interest profiles.

The **Commercial (t = 11.33)** and **Executive (t = 13.32)** differences are particularly large — urban students are dramatically more interested in business and leadership roles. This finding likely reflects the occupational composition of urban families (with parents employed in business, government service, and management) versus rural families (primarily in agriculture and skilled trades).

**Agricultural interest** shows the strongest and most significant rural advantage (t = 12.95, p < 0.01). Rural students score almost 5.57 points higher on this subscale. This finding is both expected and important — it underscores that rural students' vocational identities are deeply rooted in the agrarian occupational culture of their communities.

The **non-significant Technical difference** is somewhat surprising but can be explained by the fact that rural boys in this sample show high Technical interest (mean = 15.93) while rural girls show very low Technical interest (mean = 7.60), and urban boys show high Technical interest (mean = 17.00) while urban girls show moderate-low interest (mean = 9.60). The averaging of these gender sub-groups within each school creates a somewhat misleading picture of overall similarity — the within-school gender variation masks the between-school difference.

**H₀₁₀ is rejected**: The overall vocational interest profiles of rural and urban students are significantly different across multiple areas.

### Interpretation of Table 4.5

The comparison between rural and urban boys is illuminating. Both groups share the masculine pattern of high Technical and Scientific interests and very low Household interest. However, urban boys score significantly higher on **Artistic (t=4.57)**, **Commercial (t=5.10)**, **Executive (t=5.52)**, and **Social (t=3.40)** interests — suggesting that the urban school environment broadens boys' interest profiles beyond the traditionally masculine domains.

The **Agricultural interest gap** between rural and urban boys (16.73 vs. 10.60, t=5.89, p<0.01) is the most dramatic finding — rural boys are deeply connected to agricultural pursuits in a way that urban boys are not. This has important implications for agricultural education and rural development policy.

**H₀₁₁ is rejected** for most vocational areas: Rural and urban boys differ significantly in their vocational interest profiles.

## 4.8 Summary of Analysis

The data analysis presented in this chapter yields clear and consistent findings, which can be summarised as follows:

**Finding 1 — Technical Interest**: Boys score significantly higher than girls on Technical interest (t = 24.59, p < 0.01). 93.3% of boys versus 0% of girls achieved high interest scores in this area.

**Finding 2 — Household Interest**: Girls score significantly higher than boys on Household interest (t = 20.53, p < 0.01). 86.7% of girls versus 0% of boys achieved high scores.

**Finding 3 — Social Interest**: Girls score significantly higher on Social interest (t = 11.10, p < 0.01). 96.7% of girls achieved high interest scores — the highest percentage for any category.

**Finding 4 — Artistic Interest**: Girls score significantly higher on Artistic interest (t = 13.17, p < 0.01).

**Finding 5 — Scientific and Agricultural Interest**: Boys score significantly higher on both Scientific (t = 9.13) and Agricultural (t = 3.52) interests.

**Finding 6 — Commercial and Executive**: No significant gender differences were found — both boys and girls show moderate and broadly similar interest levels in these areas.

**Finding 7 — Agricultural vs. Urban gap**: Rural students score dramatically higher on Agricultural interest (mean = 15.17 vs. 9.60, t = 12.95, p < 0.01).

**Finding 8 — Commercial and Executive Urban Advantage**: Urban students show significantly higher Commercial (t = 11.33) and Executive (t = 13.32) interests than rural students.

**Finding 9 — Broader Interest Profile for Urban Students**: Urban students show significantly higher interest in most domains except Agricultural, reflecting the richer and more diverse occupational exposure of the urban private school environment.

**Finding 10 — Consistency of Gender Patterns**: Gender differences in the expected direction are consistent across both rural and urban settings, confirming the robustness of gender as a determinant of vocational interest patterns among Indian secondary school students.
