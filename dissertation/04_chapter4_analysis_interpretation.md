# CHAPTER – IV

# DATA ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION

## 4.1 Introduction

The present chapter presents the systematic analysis and interpretation of the data collected from the 40 secondary school students of Meerut District (20 boys of Shri Sanskrit Inter College, Aitmadpur and 20 girls of K.P. International School, Kila Parikshit Garh) by means of *Dr. S.P. Kulshrestha's Vocational Interest Record*. It is organised so as to answer, one by one, the five objectives set out in Chapter I and to test the three null hypotheses formulated there. The principal sections are: 4.2 Descriptive Analysis of the Total Sample; 4.3 Area-wise Vocational Interest Scores of Boys (Objective 1); 4.4 Area-wise Vocational Interest Scores of Girls (Objective 2); 4.5 Comparison of Boys and Girls by *t*-test (Objective 3, H₀1); 4.6 Percentage Distribution of Levels of Vocational Interest (Objective 4, H₀2); 4.7 Rank Order of Areas (Objective 5, H₀3); 4.8 Graphical Presentation; and 4.9 Discussion. Every quantitative table is followed by a textual *interpretation*. The data have been processed manually and verified in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.

## 4.5 Comparison of Boys and Girls — Area-wise t-test (Objective 3, H₀1)

The mean vocational interest scores of boys and girls were compared on each of the ten areas using the *t*-test for two independent means (df = 38; critical *t*₀.₀₅ = 2.024; *t*₀.₀₁ = 2.711). The results are presented in Tables 4.6 to 4.15.

### 4.5.1 Comparison on Literary Interest

**Table 4.6 — Significance of the Difference between the Mean Scores of Boys and Girls on Literary Interest**

| Group | N | Mean | S.D. | df | t-value | Level of Significance | Remark |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Boys | 20 | 7.05 | 2.30 | 38 | **4.02** | 0.01 | Significant |
| Girls | 20 | 9.85 | 2.10 | | | | |

**Interpretation.** Girls' mean (9.85) is substantially higher than boys' (7.05); *t* = 4.02 exceeds *t*₀.₀₁ = 2.711. The difference is *significant at the 0.01 level*; H₀1 is *rejected* for the Literary area. *Girls have significantly stronger Literary interest than boys* — supporting Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009), Singh and Verma (2023) and Reddy and Kumar (2021), who report that girls are characteristically drawn towards reading, writing and language-based occupations.

**Interpretation.** Boys' mean (11.65) is substantially higher than girls' (7.95); *t* = 4.54 exceeds 2.711. The difference is *significant at the 0.01 level*; H₀1 is *rejected*. *Boys have significantly stronger Scientific interest than girls* — consistent with Reddy and Kumar (2021), Lee, Kim and Park (2023), Watt, Eccles and Durik (2020) and Su, Rounds and Armstrong (2009): the gender gap in Scientific interest, although gradually narrowing in urban samples, remains substantial in adolescence and is particularly so in samples drawn from rural Indian schools.

| 8 | Persuasive | 30 % | 15 % | +15 % | (Boys > Girls — small) |
| 9 | Social | 15 % | 40 % | −25 % | Girls > Boys |
| 10 | Household | 5 % | 60 % | −55 % | Girls > Boys |

**Interpretation.** The largest *boys-favouring* gaps are on Constructive (+50 %), Scientific (+45 %) and Agricultural (+35 %); the largest *girls-favouring* gaps are on Household (−55 %), Artistic (−30 %) and Social (−25 %). The Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas show smaller gaps (+10 % to +20 %), with Literary at a moderate −20 %. H₀2 is therefore *rejected* for the areas with large gaps (Constructive, Scientific, Agricultural, Household, Artistic, Social, Literary) and *retained* for those with small gaps (Executive, Commercial, Persuasive). The pattern of percentage gaps closely mirrors the pattern of *t*-values in Table 4.16 — the two analyses thus provide *converging evidence* of the gender-typed pattern; *triangulation* across two different statistical lenses strengthens our confidence in the overall interpretation.

**Spearman's Rank-Order Correlation Coefficient (ρ).**

$$\rho = 1 - \dfrac{6 \sum d^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)} = 1 - \dfrac{6 \times 310}{10 \times 99} = 1 - \dfrac{1860}{990} = 1 - 1.879 = \mathbf{-0.879}$$

With *n* = 10, the critical value of ρ at the 0.01 level (two-tailed) is ±0.794, which the obtained value clearly exceeds. The negative correlation is therefore *significant at the 0.01 level*; H₀3 is *rejected*, with the additional finding that the rank orders are *systematically reversed*. The boys' top three (Constructive 1, Scientific 2, Agricultural 3) are nearly the bottom three of the girls' rank order (Constructive 9, Scientific 8, Agricultural 10); the girls' top three (Household 1, Artistic 2, Social 3) are nearly the bottom three of the boys' (Household 10, Artistic 8, Social 7). The Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas, which were *not* significant in the *t*-test, occupy *very similar middle ranks* (Executive 5/6, Commercial 6/7, Persuasive 4/5). The internal consistency of the three statistical lenses — *t*-test, percentage analysis and rank-order correlation — reinforces the substantive conclusion: *the vocational interests of secondary school boys and girls of Meerut District are sharply, systematically and statistically significantly gender-typed*.

## 4.9 Discussion of the Results

The analyses presented in Sections 4.2 to 4.7 are now drawn together for an interpretative discussion.

**(a) The gender-typed pattern.** The most important finding of the present study is the *clear, systematic and statistically significant gender-typed pattern* of vocational interest. *Boys* are higher on the Scientific, Constructive (Technical) and Agricultural areas; *girls* on the Literary, Artistic, Social and Household areas; the Executive, Commercial and Persuasive areas are *not* significantly differentiated by gender. This pattern is identical in its broad structure to the pattern reported by virtually every Indian study reviewed in Chapter II (Singh & Verma, 2023; Reddy & Kumar, 2021; Joshi, 2020; Mehta, 2019; Yadav, 2016) and by every major foreign meta-analysis (Su, Rounds & Armstrong, 2009; Williams & O'Connor, 2022). Its replicability across decades, cultures, samples and tools points to the *deep socialisation roots* of the differences.

**(b) Magnitude of the differences.** The differences are *much larger* on Constructive (Δ = 4.85), Household (Δ = 6.30), Scientific (Δ = 3.70), Agricultural (Δ = 3.65) and Artistic (Δ = 3.45) areas than on Executive (Δ = 1.45), Commercial (Δ = 0.85) and Persuasive (Δ = 0.95). The largest differences fall on the *most strongly gender-typed* vocations of Indian society — building and machine work for boys, home management for girls — while the smallest differences fall on the *gender-neutral* vocations of management, commerce and persuasion, which the modern Indian society has slowly come to share between the two sexes.

**(c) Role of the school environment.** The two schools sampled lie at *opposite ends* of the locality and management continua. This contrast intensifies certain effects (Agricultural — boys higher partly because of rural background; Scientific — boys higher *despite* the urban-CBSE advantage of the girls) and dilutes others (Executive, Commercial — boys higher despite urban CBSE girls' exposure to administrative role-models). The *gender* effect is therefore unmistakable, but, strictly, the present study compares "*Government-rural-U.P.-Board boys versus Private-urban-CBSE girls*" rather than "boys versus girls" *in general* — a point flagged in the Limitations section of Chapter V.

**(d) Hypothesis testing.** *H₀1* — *partly rejected*: rejected for Literary, Scientific, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Social and Household; retained for Executive, Commercial and Persuasive. *H₀2* — *partly rejected*: rejected wherever the high-level gap exceeds 20 percentage points (Scientific, Constructive, Artistic, Agricultural, Social, Household); retained where the gap is small (Commercial, Persuasive, with Literary at the boundary). *H₀3* — *strongly rejected*: ρ = −0.88, a *systematic reversal* rather than mere absence of agreement.

**(e) Educational message.** The three statistical lenses converge on the same picture: in the Indian secondary school of today, *boys and girls live in two largely different vocational worlds*. The Constructive–Scientific–Agricultural world of the boys and the Household–Artistic–Social–Literary world of the girls lie almost wholly apart. This separation is partly the natural expression of biological and psychological gender differences but is largely the product of *differential socialisation, differential exposure and differential expectations*. The educational system can do something about the second component — and in a country where the National Education Policy 2020 has explicitly pledged "equity and inclusion in school education", the responsibility to do so is real and immediate. Concrete educational implications, suggestions and recommendations are presented in Chapter V.
